Chapter 1: The Walking Stick

Mr Sherlock Holmes usually got up very late in the morning, but on that morning he was already sitting at the breakfast table in our rooms at Baker Street. I was standing by the fire, holding a walking stick which a visitor had left there the night before, while we were out.

It was a fine, thick stick of good wood, with a wide silver band just below the handle. On the band someone had written: "To James Mortimer, M.R.C.S., from his friends of the C.C.H., 1884."

"Well, Watson, what can you tell me about it?" said Holmes, without turning round.

"How did you know what I was doing? You have eyes in the back of your head."

"No, but I have a shiny silver coffee pot in front of me," he said. "Come on, tell me what kind of man our visitor is. We missed him, so his stick must speak for him."

I tried to think like my friend. "I think Dr Mortimer is a successful country doctor, quite old, and much respected," I said. "His friends gave him this stick as a present. "And he walks a lot, because the end of the stick is badly worn, and a town doctor would not walk so much."

"Excellent!" said Holmes.

"And the letters C.C.H. mean some kind of hunting club. Perhaps he helped its members as a doctor, and they gave him a present to thank him."

"Really, Watson, you are doing very well," said Holmes. He took the stick from me and looked at it for a few minutes. Then he went to the window with it and studied it again with his magnifying glass. "I am afraid, my dear Watson, that most of your ideas were wrong."

"Wrong? What exactly did I miss?"

"When I said that you did well, I meant that your mistakes helped me to find the truth. The man is certainly a country doctor, and he walks a good deal, but C.C.H. is not a hunting club; it must be Charing Cross Hospital. People give a present like this when a doctor leaves a hospital. So he worked in London and then moved to the country. He was not an important doctor there but a young one, probably a house surgeon, the kind who lives at the hospital. A senior man would not leave London for a country practice. So he is young, under thirty, friendly, not ambitious, a little absent-minded, and he owns a dog. I would say that the dog is bigger than a terrier but smaller than a mastiff."

I laughed. "How can you possibly know about the dog?"

"Look at these marks on the wood, where a dog has carried this stick in its mouth many times, walking behind its master. The marks of its teeth are quite clear, and the jaw is too wide for a small dog and not wide enough for a big one. It could be a curly-haired spaniel." He had stood up while he was speaking, and now he stopped at the window. "Yes, my dear fellow, it is a curly-haired spaniel."

"How on earth can you be so sure?"

"Because I can see the dog on our doorstep at this moment, and that is its owner ringing the bell. Do not go, Watson. He is a doctor like you, and your help may be useful."

A moment later our visitor came in. He was a tall, thin man with a long nose like a beak, and his grey eyes were close together and shone brightly behind gold glasses. His coat was old and his trousers were worn, but he looked clean and kind. He was young, but he walked with his head forward, like a man who reads too much.

"Ah, my stick!" he cried happily. "I was not sure where I had left it. I would not lose that stick for the world."

"A present, I see," said Holmes.

"Yes, sir, from my friends at Charing Cross Hospital, when I got married."

"Dear, dear, that is very bad," said Holmes, shaking his head.

Dr Mortimer looked at him in surprise. "Why is it bad?"

"Only because you have destroyed some of my little ideas about you. Your marriage, you say?"

"Yes, sir. I married, and so I left the hospital, because I needed a home of my own."

"Well, we were not so far wrong after all," said Holmes with a smile. "Now, Dr James Mortimer, how can I help you?"

Our visitor sat down. "I have come to you, Mr Holmes, because I have a very serious and unusual problem. I am a practical man of science, but this problem is not like anything I have known before. And you are the second-best expert in Europe, as everybody knows."

"Indeed, sir! May I ask who is the first?" said Holmes, a little coldly.

"Monsieur Bertillon of Paris is a very great scientist."

"Then perhaps you should ask him instead."

"I said scientist, sir, but as a practical man of business, everybody knows that you are the best. I hope I have not offended you."

"Only a little," said Holmes, "so now tell me plainly, Dr Mortimer, what is your problem?"

Dr Mortimer took a folded paper out of his pocket. "I have here an old manuscript," he said. "It belonged to my friend Sir Charles Baskerville. His sudden and tragic death three months ago caused a lot of excitement in Devonshire. Before he died, he gave this paper to me, and I promised to look after it carefully. He was a strong, practical man, like me, but he took this paper very seriously, and he was ready for the end that came to him."

Holmes held out his hand for the manuscript.

"It is dated 1742," said Dr Mortimer. "It tells an old story which is known in the Baskerville family, and with your permission, I will read it to you."

Holmes leaned back in his chair, put his fingertips together and closed his eyes. "Please do," he said quietly.

Chapter 2: The Curse of the Baskervilles

Dr Mortimer turned the paper towards the light, and in a high, cracking voice he read this strange old story.

"Many people have written about the Hound of the Baskervilles. I am a direct descendant of Hugo Baskerville, and I heard the story from my father, who heard it from his. So I write it down for my sons, so that they may listen and learn that God punishes evil men.

"Long ago, Baskerville Hall belonged to Hugo Baskerville, a wild, cruel and godless man. In those days he fell in love with the daughter of a farmer who had some land near the Hall. But the young girl was good and modest, and she was so afraid of him that she always kept away from him.

"One day, while her father and brothers were away from home, Hugo came to the farm with five or six of his wicked friends. They took the girl away and carried her to the Hall. There they locked her in a room upstairs, and then Hugo and his friends sat down to a long night of drinking and shouting below.

"The poor girl was nearly mad with fear, so she climbed out of the window and down the ivy on the wall. Then she ran across the moor towards her home. The moor is a wide, wild, empty land of grass, rocks and dangerous bogs, and it was nine miles to her father's farm.

"A little later Hugo went upstairs with some food and drink for her. He found the room empty, and he became like a man possessed by the devil. He ran down the stairs and jumped onto the dinner table. In front of everyone he shouted that he would give his body and his soul to the Evil One if he could catch the girl. Then he ordered his servants to saddle his horse and let out the dogs. He gave the dogs a piece of the girl's scarf to smell, and away they went in the moonlight across the moor.

"His friends sat still for a time, until they understood what was happening and followed on their horses, thirteen of them. After a mile or two they passed a shepherd and asked him if he had seen the hunt. The man was so frightened that he could hardly speak. At last he said that he had seen the poor girl, with the dogs behind her. But he had seen more than that. Hugo Baskerville had passed him on his black horse, and behind him ran a hound of hell. The shepherd prayed to God that it would never follow him.

"The drunken men laughed at the shepherd and rode on, but soon their blood turned cold. Hugo's black horse galloped past them with an empty saddle and its mouth white with foam. Then they found the dogs, which were famous for their courage, but now they were crying in a group at the top of a deep valley. Most of the men turned back, but the three boldest rode down into the valley.

"There, in the moonlight, lay the poor girl. She had died of fear and tiredness, and near her lay the body of Hugo Baskerville. But it was not the sight of the bodies that terrified the three men. Standing over Hugo, and tearing at his throat, was a great black beast. It was shaped like a hound, but it was larger than any hound that a man has ever seen. As they looked, it turned its blazing eyes and dripping jaws towards them. The three men screamed with fear and rode away for their lives. One of them died that same night, and the other two never recovered.

"That is the story of the hound. Since that night, many men of our family have died sudden, bloody and mysterious deaths. So, my sons, I warn you: never cross the moor in the dark hours, when the powers of evil are strong."

Dr Mortimer finished reading. He pushed his glasses up onto his forehead and looked at Sherlock Holmes. Holmes yawned.

"Well?" he said at last.

"Do you not find it interesting?"

"To a collector of fairy tales, perhaps, it would be interesting."

Dr Mortimer took a newspaper out of his pocket. "Now, Mr Holmes, I will give you something a little more recent. This is the Devon County Chronicle of the fourteenth of June this year. It reports the death of Sir Charles Baskerville."

My friend leaned forward with more interest while our visitor read the report aloud. Sir Charles had come back to England from South Africa, where he had made a large fortune. He was a kind and generous man who did a lot of good in the area. He had no wife or children, and his servants were a husband and wife called Barrymore.

Every night Sir Charles took a walk before going to bed, along the famous Yew Alley of Baskerville Hall. This is a long path between two high, dark walls of yew trees. On the night of the fourth of June he went out as usual, smoking a cigar. At twelve o'clock Barrymore found the door of the house still open, so he went out with a lamp to look for his master. Halfway down the alley there was a gate which opened onto the moor. It seemed that Sir Charles had stood there for some time before he walked on, down the alley. At the far end of it, Barrymore found his body.

There were no signs of violence. But Sir Charles's face was so twisted that Dr Mortimer at first could not recognise his old friend. The doctor at the inquest said that his heart had stopped, because he had been ill for some time. So the death was natural, and nobody was to blame. The newspaper said that it was important to find Sir Charles's heir, his nephew Henry Baskerville, as soon as possible.

"Those are the public facts," said Holmes, "so now tell me the private ones."

Dr Mortimer's face became serious. "I will tell you something that I have told nobody," he said quietly. "Sir Charles believed the old story. He was nervous, and he never went out onto the moor at night. Several times he asked me if I had ever seen a strange animal there, or heard the baying of a hound. Three weeks before his death I arrived at his house one evening. He was standing at the door when suddenly he looked past me, and his face filled with horror. I turned round quickly and saw something like a large black calf disappear behind the trees. He was so upset that I stayed with him all evening. That was when he gave me the manuscript."

"And on the night of his death?"

"I arrived at the Hall soon after Barrymore found him. I noticed something which nobody at the inquest mentioned. Barrymore said that there were no footprints on the grass round the body. He did not see any, but I did, a little distance away. They were fresh and clear."

"A man's or a woman's?"

Dr Mortimer looked at us strangely, and his voice fell almost to a whisper.

"Mr Holmes, they were the footprints of a gigantic hound!"

Chapter 3: Sir Henry Baskerville

I must say that a cold feeling ran down my back. Holmes leaned forward with that hard, dry light in his eyes which I knew so well.

"You saw this yourself?"

"As clearly as I see you now."

"And you said nothing to anybody?"

"What was the use? The police would only laugh at me."

Holmes asked him many careful questions, and we learnt that the footprints were about twenty yards from the body. Sir Charles had stood at the gate to the moor for five or ten minutes, because the ash had fallen twice from his cigar. And after that, strangely, the footprints of Sir Charles changed. He seemed to walk on his toes down the alley.

"He was not walking on his toes," said Holmes. "He was running, running for his life, away from the moor, until his heart burst. But why did he wait at the gate? And who was he waiting for? Oh, Dr Mortimer, why did you not call me at once? Now the rain and the curious farmers have destroyed everything, but what do you want from me now?"

"Advice. Sir Henry Baskerville, the heir, arrives at Waterloo Station in exactly one hour and a quarter. He has been living in Canada as a farmer. Is it safe to take him to Baskerville Hall?"

"Bring him here tomorrow at ten o'clock," said Holmes, "because I need time to think."

Holmes spent the rest of the day in his armchair, smoking his pipe. When I came home in the evening, the room was so full of smoke that I could hardly see him. He had asked a shop for a large map of the moor, and in his mind he had walked across it all day.

At exactly ten the next morning, Dr Mortimer arrived with Sir Henry Baskerville. He was a short, strong man of about thirty, with thick black eyebrows and a brown face, and he looked like a man who spent most of his time outdoors.

"A strange thing happened this morning, Mr Holmes," said Sir Henry. "I received this letter at my hotel, although nobody knew that I was going to stay there."

He put an envelope on the table, and inside it was a sheet of paper. In the middle, there was a single sentence made of printed words cut out and glued onto the paper. Only one word, "moor", was written in ink. The message said: "As you value your life or your reason keep away from the moor."

Holmes studied it for a long time, and then he took a copy of yesterday's Times and found an article about free trade. "Look," he said. "The words 'keep away', 'value', 'your life' and 'reason' all come from this page. They were cut out with short nail scissors by a person who was educated, but wanted to look uneducated. And he was in a hurry, because the words are not glued in a straight line."

"That is wonderful!" said Dr Mortimer, full of admiration.

"Now, Sir Henry, has anything else unusual happened to you since you arrived?"

"Not really. Unless you count losing one boot."

"Only one boot?"

"I put a pair of new brown boots outside my door last night for cleaning. In the morning there was only one. I have never worn them, because I bought them in London only yesterday."

Holmes was silent for a moment, and then Dr Mortimer told Sir Henry the whole story of the hound, and the young man listened with great attention.

"I have heard the story of the dog since I was a child," he said at last. "But I never took it seriously. Well, Mr Holmes, no devil in hell and no man on earth will stop me from going to the home of my family. You may take that as my final answer."

His dark eyebrows came down and his face turned red, and I could see that he had the famous Baskerville temper.

The two men left at half past eleven, and agreed to come back to lunch at two. The moment the door closed, Holmes jumped up.

"Your hat and boots, Watson, quick! There is no time to lose!"

We ran down into the street, where Dr Mortimer and Sir Henry were walking along Oxford Street about two hundred yards in front of us. We followed them until suddenly Holmes gave a little cry of pleasure. A cab was moving slowly along the other side of the street, behind our friends. Inside, I saw a man with a thick black beard and sharp eyes. He saw us too, and at once he shouted something to the driver, and the cab rushed away down the street. Holmes looked round for another cab, but there was none.

"What bad luck, and what bad management!" he said angrily. "But I got the number of his cab: 2704. Someone is following Sir Henry closely, Watson. And he is a clever man."

When we arrived at the Northumberland Hotel at two, Sir Henry was in a great temper. "They are making a fool of me in this hotel!" he cried. "Now they have taken one of my old black boots! Last night it was a new brown one, and today an old black one!"

That afternoon, a hotel servant found the new brown boot in Sir Henry's room, but the old black one was still missing, and Holmes looked very thoughtful.

At lunch, Holmes asked about Barrymore, whose beard was black. Was he in Devonshire, or perhaps in London? To check, Holmes sent a telegram to Baskerville Hall: "Is all ready for Sir Henry?" The answer had to be given to Barrymore in person.

Later that evening, the cab driver, John Clayton, came to see us at Baker Street, because Holmes had sent a message to the cab company. Clayton told us that a gentleman with a black beard had hired his cab for the whole day and had followed two men from Baker Street.

"And did he tell you his name?" asked Holmes.

"Yes, sir, he did," said the cabman, "and his name was Mr Sherlock Holmes."

I have never seen my friend more surprised, but then he burst out laughing. "A hit, Watson, a clear hit! We have a very clever enemy."

The next day the answer to the telegram arrived: Barrymore was at the Hall. Holmes could not leave London because of other important work, so he turned to me.

"Watson, I want you to go to Devonshire with Sir Henry. Keep close to him, and never let him walk on the moor alone at night. Send me reports of everything, every little fact. And take your revolver."

Chapter 4: Baskerville Hall

On Saturday Sir Henry, Dr Mortimer and I took the train to Devonshire, and Holmes came with us to the station.

"Remember, Watson," he said, as the train began to move. "Watch the Barrymores and watch the neighbours, and whatever you do, keep Sir Henry away from the moor at night."

Sir Henry had not seen England since he was a boy. He looked out of the window like a child as the train passed red earth, green fields and comfortable farms. Then, in the distance, the land rose into a long, grey line of hills, strange and dark against the sky. "That is the moor," said Dr Mortimer quietly.

At the small station a carriage was waiting for us. As we drove through the narrow country lanes, I was surprised to see a soldier on horseback by the side of the road, holding a rifle.

"What is happening here?" asked Dr Mortimer.

"A prisoner has escaped from Princetown prison, sir," said our driver. "Three days ago. His name is Selden, the Notting Hill murderer, and he is a very dangerous man. The farmers are afraid."

I remembered the case, because the man had killed someone in a very cruel way, without any reason. Now he was somewhere out on that cold, endless moor, hiding among the rocks. It was not a pleasant thought.

The sun was going down when we saw Baskerville Hall. Two tall towers rose above the trees, and the house itself was covered with dark ivy. A pale lamp shone over the door, and Sir Henry was very quiet.

"It is not surprising that my uncle felt nervous here," he said at last, and his voice shook a little. "I will have electric lights put in. In six months it will be bright and cheerful."

Dr Mortimer drove on to his own house. We were met at the door by Barrymore. He was a tall, handsome man with a square black beard and a pale face. His wife, a large, serious woman, took our coats.

"Would you like dinner at once, Sir Henry?" said Barrymore. Then he added, "My wife and I will be happy to stay with you until you find new servants. But after that we would like to leave. Sir Charles's death was a great shock to us. We cannot be happy at Baskerville Hall any more."

The dining room was long and dark, with a high ceiling of black wood. On the walls hung many old portraits of the Baskerville family, from the time of Queen Elizabeth onwards. They watched us silently while we ate, and we did not talk much. After dinner we went to bed early.

I could not sleep, so from my window I looked out at the moor under the moon. The wind was moaning round the old house. And then, in the middle of the night, I heard a sound. It was the sound of a woman crying. It was the deep, hopeless crying of someone with a terrible sorrow. It came from somewhere inside the house. I lay awake for a long time, listening, but I heard nothing more.

At breakfast the next morning, Sir Henry asked me, "Did you hear a woman crying in the night?"

"Yes, I did, very clearly."

He rang the bell for Barrymore. When we asked him, the butler's pale face became paler still. "There are only two women in the house, Sir Henry," he said. "One is the kitchen maid, who sleeps in the other wing. The other is my wife. I can tell you that the sound did not come from her."

But he was lying. After breakfast I met Mrs Barrymore in the long corridor, with the sun on her face. Her eyes were red and swollen, because she had been crying. Why had Barrymore lied? And why was his wife so unhappy?

I remembered Holmes's telegram. Had it really been put into Barrymore's own hands? I decided to walk to the village of Grimpen, four miles away, and ask at the post office.

"Certainly, sir," said the postmaster. "My boy gave it to Mrs Barrymore, because Mr Barrymore was up in the attic, and she said that she would give it to him."

"Then nobody actually saw Barrymore himself?"

"Well, no, sir, but his wife said he was in the house."

So we still did not know if Barrymore had been in London on that day. Was he the man with the black beard in the cab? I walked back along the road, thinking hard. And then I heard running footsteps behind me, and a voice calling my name.

Chapter 5: The Stapletons of Merripit House

I turned round and saw a small, thin man of between thirty and forty years old, with a clean-shaven face and fair hair. He was carrying a green butterfly net in one hand and a tin box over his shoulder.

"Please excuse me, Dr Watson," he said, out of breath. "Here on the moor we are simple people, and we do not wait for formal introductions. My name is Stapleton, and I live at Merripit House. Our friend Mortimer told me about you. I hope Sir Henry is well after his journey?"

"He is very well, thank you, and happy to be here at last."

"We were all afraid that he would not come, after the sad death of Sir Charles. Of course you know the old story of the hound?"

"I have heard it, of course."

"It is surprising how the farmers here believe it," he said. "Many of them say that they have seen the creature on the moor. Sir Charles believed it too, and I am sure that it led to his tragic end. His heart was weak, and perhaps he saw a dog in the dark and died of fright. But tell me, Dr Watson, is Mr Sherlock Holmes interested in the case? We have heard of your famous friend even here."

I was surprised by the question, but I answered carefully. "Mr Holmes is busy with other cases in London. I am simply here as Sir Henry's friend."

"Of course. Forgive me for asking. Will you come to Merripit House? It is not far, and I would like you to meet my sister."

We left the road and followed a narrow path through the heather. As we walked, Stapleton talked about the moor with great love. He was a naturalist, and he knew every plant, bird and insect there. He pointed to a wide green area in the distance, which looked like a pleasant field.

"That is the great Grimpen Mire," he said. "One false step there means death to a man or an animal. Only yesterday I saw one of the moor ponies walk into it, and it never came out. I watched its head for a long time, and then the mud sucked it down. Even in dry weather it is dangerous to cross, but after the autumn rains it is a terrible place. And yet I can find my way to the centre of it and come back alive."

"Why would you want to go into such a horrible place?"

"Because there are rare plants and butterflies on the little islands in the middle of it. Look! There is another of those poor ponies."

Something brown was moving among the green reeds, and then a long neck rose up, and a terrible cry of pain echoed over the moor. A moment later the pony was gone.

At that same moment, a long, low howl came across the moor. It began as a deep sound and rose into a loud roar, and then it fell again into a sad moan. It filled the whole air, and yet it was impossible to say where it came from. I felt cold all over.

"What on earth was that?" I asked.

Stapleton looked at me with a strange expression. "The farmers say that it is the Hound of the Baskervilles, calling for its victim. I have heard it once or twice before, but never so loudly."

"You are a man of science. You do not believe that nonsense?"

"Perhaps it is only a bird called a bittern. They are very rare now in England, but nothing is impossible on the moor." Then suddenly he ran off after a small butterfly, jumping from one patch of grass to another, with his green net waving in the air.

While I was watching him, I heard steps behind me, and a woman was coming along the path. I knew at once that she was Miss Stapleton, but she did not look like her brother at all. He was pale and fair, but she was dark and tall, and she was one of the most beautiful women that I had ever seen. To my great surprise, she spoke to me in a quick, urgent voice.

"Go back!" she said. "Go straight back to London, immediately. Never set foot on the moor again!"

"But why should I go? I have only just arrived."

"I cannot explain, but for God's sake, do what I ask! Be quiet, my brother is coming. Not a word of what I said."

Stapleton came back, breathing hard, and looked at us both, and I thought there was something unfriendly in his eyes. "Well, Beryl," he said, "I see that you have already introduced yourself."

"Yes, I was telling Sir Henry that it was too late in the year to see the true beauty of the moor."

"But this is not Sir Henry," said Stapleton. "This is Dr Watson."

Her face turned red. "Then we have been talking at cross purposes," she said quickly.

I went with them to Merripit House, a simple grey farmhouse with an orchard round it. Inside there were many books and a large collection of butterflies. Stapleton told me that he had once owned a school in the north of England, but it had failed after an illness had killed three of the boys. Now he spent his life studying the moor.

When I left, Miss Stapleton ran after me along the path. "I am sorry, Dr Watson," she said. "I made a mistake, because I thought you were Sir Henry. Please forget my words."

"I cannot forget them, Miss Stapleton. Tell me plainly what you meant."

"It was only a woman's foolish fear. My brother and I were very shocked by Sir Charles's death. When I heard that another member of the family had come here, I felt that he should be warned. That is all." And she turned and hurried back towards the house.

Chapter 6: The Light in the Window

During the next weeks I wrote long reports to Holmes in London. I will tell the story as I told it to him.

Sir Henry soon became very interested in our beautiful neighbour; in fact, he fell in love with her, and they met almost every day. She seemed to like him too, but her brother did not seem pleased. He was always with them, and he never left them alone together. That was a problem for me, because Holmes had told me never to let Sir Henry go out on the moor alone.

One day Sir Henry put on his hat and went out without me, so I followed him at a distance. From the top of a hill I saw him walking with Miss Stapleton on the path. They stopped, and he put his arm round her. At that moment I saw Stapleton running towards them with his butterfly net. He was waving his arms and shouting at Sir Henry, and then he took his sister by the hand and led her away.

Sir Henry walked back to me with a red, angry face. "What is wrong with me?" he cried. "I asked her to marry me, and that man behaved like a madman. Am I not good enough for his sister?"

But that afternoon Stapleton came to the Hall and apologised. He said that his sister was everything to him, and the thought of losing her had made him lose his temper. He asked Sir Henry to wait for three months before speaking of love again. Sir Henry agreed, and peace was made.

Meanwhile I had discovered something else. One night, at about two o'clock, I heard soft footsteps outside my room. I opened the door and saw Barrymore walking down the corridor with a candle in his hand. He went into an empty room at the end. Through the open door I saw him standing at the window, holding the candle against the glass and looking out at the black moor. After some minutes he made a sound of disappointment and put out the light.

The next night Sir Henry and I sat up and waited, and at two o'clock we heard the footsteps again. We followed Barrymore quietly and found him at the same window, with the candle in his hand.

"What are you doing here, Barrymore?" said Sir Henry angrily.

The butler jumped, and his hand shook so much that the candle almost fell. "Nothing, sir. I was checking that the windows were closed."

"Do not lie to me! You are making a signal to someone on the moor. Who is it?"

"I cannot tell you, sir, because it is not my secret."

When I looked out of the window myself, far away in the darkness there was a tiny point of yellow light.

"There is the answer to your signal!" I said.

At that moment Mrs Barrymore came in. "John, what is happening?" she cried. Then she turned to Sir Henry. "It is my fault, sir, not his, because he did it for my sake. My unhappy brother is starving out there on the moor. He is Selden, the escaped prisoner."

So that was the secret. Mrs Barrymore told us that her younger brother had been a sweet child. But he had grown up wild and wicked, and he had broken her mother's heart. When he escaped from prison, he came to her because he had nowhere else to go. Every few nights Barrymore put a light in the window, and if Selden answered, he took food out to him.

"Go to your room," said Sir Henry. "We will talk about this in the morning."

When they had gone, Sir Henry turned to me. "Watson, that man is a murderer, and he is dangerous to every family on the moor. Let us go out and catch him."

So we took our revolvers and went out into the night. The light was still burning among the rocks, and we walked towards it across the dark, wet moor. Suddenly that long, terrible howl rose again out of the darkness, from far away. Sir Henry caught my arm.

"Watson, what in heaven was that?" he whispered.

"The local people say it is the hound," I said.

He was silent for a moment. "My God, can there be any truth in it? I am not a coward, but that sound froze my blood."

We went on. At last we came to the candle, which was stuck between two rocks. Suddenly an evil face looked at us over the rocks. It was a terrible face, dirty and bearded, like the face of a wild animal. Then the man jumped up and ran, and we ran after him, but he was too fast for us, and soon he disappeared among the rocks.

We stopped, out of breath, and then I saw something strange. The moon was rising, and against its silver light, on the top of a rocky hill, stood the figure of a tall, thin man. He was standing very still, with his arms crossed, looking down at the moor. I turned to show Sir Henry, but when I looked again, the man had gone.

He was not the convict, because he was taller and thinner. Who was the stranger on the tor, the rocky hill? I wrote to Holmes about it that same night, but I had no answer to the question.

Chapter 7: The Lady of Coombe Tracey

The next morning Sir Henry told Barrymore that we had tried to catch Selden. Barrymore was very upset. "You have broken your promise to us, Sir Henry," he said. "My wife has trusted you."

"I made no promise," said Sir Henry. "But I am sorry for your wife."

After a long talk, Barrymore explained that Selden was going to leave England by ship in a few days. Sir Henry agreed to say nothing to the police, and Barrymore thanked him with tears in his eyes. Then he said something which surprised us both.

"You have been very good to us, sir, and I want to do something in return. I know something about poor Sir Charles's death. I have never told anybody."

"What is it, Barrymore?"

"On the morning of his death, Sir Charles received a letter which came from Coombe Tracey, and the handwriting was a woman's. A few weeks ago my wife was cleaning the fireplace in his study when she found the ashes of a burnt letter. Only a small part of it was still readable, at the end of the page. It asked him, as a gentleman, to burn the letter and to be at the gate by ten o'clock. And it was signed with two letters: L.L."

"So Sir Charles went to the gate to meet a woman!" I said. "Who is L.L.?"

"I am afraid I do not know, sir."

I asked Dr Mortimer, and he soon found the answer. Her name was Laura Lyons, and she lived in Coombe Tracey. She was the daughter of a man called Frankland, who lived near us. She had married an artist, but he had left her, and her father had refused to help her. Sir Charles and some other people had given her money to start a small typewriting business.

The next day I drove to Coombe Tracey. Mrs Lyons was a beautiful woman with brown hair and hazel eyes, but there was something hard in her face. When I mentioned Sir Charles, she turned pale.

"Did you write to him and ask him to meet you at the gate at ten o'clock, on the day of his death?"

For a long moment she was silent, and then she said, "Yes, I did. I wanted to ask him for help, because I wanted to get a divorce from my husband, and I needed money for it. I thought that if I spoke to him in person, he would help me."

"And did you go to the gate that night?"

"No. Something happened which made it unnecessary, because I received help from another person."

"Then why did you not write again to say that you were not coming?"

"I meant to, but I did not." She would not tell me any more. I felt sure that she was hiding something, but I did not know what.

On my way home, I passed the house of old Mr Frankland, her father. He was a red-faced man who loved going to court about everything. He invited me in and proudly showed me the large telescope on his roof.

"I could tell the police a lot, but I will not help them," he said. "They have been rude to me. For example, I know where the escaped convict is hiding. Every day a boy carries food to him across the moor. I watch him through my telescope. Look! There he is now."

I looked through the telescope. A small boy with a bundle on his shoulder was climbing a distant hill. He looked round carefully and then disappeared over the top. But I knew that Selden was being helped by Barrymore, not by a boy. So who was the boy carrying food to? I remembered the man on the tor, and I decided to find out.

Chapter 8: The Man on the Tor

I walked across the moor to the hill where the boy had disappeared. On the far side I found a group of ancient stone huts, where people had lived thousands of years ago. Most of them were ruins, but one still had a roof. I went towards it quietly, with my revolver in my hand.

The hut was empty, but someone was clearly living there. There were blankets on the floor, a cooking pot, some bread and some tins of food. On a flat stone, under a cup, I found a sheet of paper. My heart jumped when I read it. In pencil someone had written: "Dr Watson has gone to Coombe Tracey."

So the stranger was not following Sir Henry. He was following me, and he knew everything I did! I sat down in the dark corner of the hut with my revolver, and waited, because I was determined to see his face.

The sun went down, and the sky turned red. At last I heard footsteps in the distance, coming nearer and nearer. Then a shadow fell across the door, and a voice I knew very well said:

"It is a lovely evening, my dear Watson. I think you will be more comfortable outside than inside."

For a moment I could not speak, and then I ran outside. There, sitting on a stone, was Sherlock Holmes himself. He was thin and brown from the sun, but his face was shaved and his clothes were clean, as if he were in Baker Street.

"Holmes!" I cried in amazement. "I have never been so glad to see anyone in my life! But how did you know I was here?"

"I saw your footprints, and you smoke a cigarette from Bradley's in Oxford Street. I found the end of one outside."

"But I thought you were in London, working on another case."

"That is what I wanted everyone to think. If I had come to the Hall with you, our enemy would have been careful. Here, unknown, I could watch and act. My only helper was young Cartwright, a boy from London, who brought me food and clean shirts."

I felt a little hurt. "Then my reports were all for nothing!"

"Not at all. My friend in London sent them on to me at once, and they were very useful. Your visit to Mrs Lyons was excellent. Did you know, Watson, that she and Stapleton are very close friends? He has promised to marry her when she is free from her husband."

"But Stapleton lives with his sister!"

"That lady is not his sister, Watson. She is his wife."

I stared at him. "Then why does he say that she is his sister?"

"Because he wanted her to seem free, and he needed Sir Henry to fall in love with her, for his plan. He is our enemy, Watson. Stapleton was the man in the cab in London, with a false beard. He killed Sir Charles, and now he wants to kill Sir Henry."

Before I could answer, a terrible scream came across the moor. It was a scream of horror and pain. Then we heard the deep, heavy sound of a hound, and then silence.

"The hound!" cried Holmes. "Come, Watson, quick! We may be too late!"

We ran across the moor in the dark, falling over rocks and through the heather. At the bottom of a rocky hill we found a man lying on his face. His head was broken, and he was quite dead, because he had fallen from the rocks above. He was wearing a red-brown suit that we knew very well. It was Sir Henry's suit.

Holmes sat down beside him, and for once he looked completely beaten. "I should have come sooner," he said. "I failed. I wanted to have a complete case, and now our client is dead."

But then Holmes lit a match and looked at the face, and he gave a cry of joy. The dead man had a thick beard, so it was not Sir Henry. It was Selden, the convict.

Then we understood. Sir Henry had given some old clothes to Barrymore, and Barrymore had given them to Selden to help him escape. The hound had been given the smell of Sir Henry's old boot, the one stolen in London. So it had followed the smell of Sir Henry's clothes and chased the poor man to his death.

At that moment we saw a figure walking towards us in the dark: it was Stapleton, with a cigar in his hand. When he saw the body he stopped, and for a second his face showed surprise. "Is it Sir Henry?" he asked.

"No," said Holmes calmly. "It is the convict, Selden."

Stapleton hid his disappointment well. "We heard a cry," he said. "I was worried about Sir Henry. Did you hear anything else? A hound, perhaps?"

"No," said Holmes. "We heard nothing else. I am going back to London tomorrow. This case has been too difficult for me."

Chapter 9: The Portrait on the Wall

Holmes and I walked back to Baskerville Hall together that night. Sir Henry was very pleased to see my friend, although he was surprised to hear that he had been living on the moor. We told him about Selden's death, but we did not tell him everything about the hound, because Holmes did not want to frighten him yet.

"Stapleton has invited me to dinner at Merripit House tomorrow evening," said Sir Henry. "I hope you will both come too."

"I am afraid that Watson and I must go to London tomorrow," said Holmes. Sir Henry looked very disappointed, and so did I, but Holmes had a reason for everything he did, and I waited.

After supper Holmes stood looking at the old family portraits in the dining room, while Sir Henry told him who they all were. At last Holmes stopped in front of a picture of a man in a black velvet hat, with a lace collar and long hair.

"Who is this gentleman in the black hat?"

"That is the cause of all our trouble," said Sir Henry. "That is wicked Hugo, who started the story of the hound."

Holmes said nothing more, but later, when Sir Henry had gone to bed, he took a candle and held it up to the portrait. Then he stood on a chair and covered the long hair and the hat with his arm.

"Good heavens!" I cried in astonishment.

The face of Stapleton looked down at us from the old painting.

"Yes," said Holmes, laughing. "The eyes are trained to look at faces, not at hats and hair. This is the reason for the whole crime, Watson. Stapleton is a Baskerville. That is quite clear."

"And he wants the money and the land for himself!"

"Exactly. If Sir Henry dies, Stapleton becomes the heir, and the family has no idea that he exists. He came here secretly to be near the Hall. He made friends with Sir Charles, and he learnt about the old man's weak heart and his fear of the legend. We have him in our net, Watson. By tomorrow night he will be caught like one of his own butterflies."

The next morning Holmes asked Sir Henry to do something strange. "Go to dinner with Stapleton tonight, as you planned," he said. "Tell him that Watson and I have gone to London. Take your carriage to Merripit House, but send it home and tell them that you will walk back across the moor."

"Walk across the moor at night? But you always told me never to do that!"

"This time you may do it in safety. I promise you. Do you trust me?"

"Completely," said Sir Henry, but he looked rather unhappy.

Holmes and I drove to the station and sent the carriage back to the Hall, and then we went to Coombe Tracey to see Mrs Laura Lyons. Holmes put a few photographs on her table. One showed Stapleton and his wife together, many years earlier, when they had a school in Yorkshire under a different name.

"This lady is Mrs Stapleton," said Holmes. "Her husband is not free to marry you, madam."

Mrs Lyons looked at the photograph, and her face became white, and then her eyes filled with anger. "He lied to me," she said. "He promised to marry me if I could get a divorce, and he told me what to write to Sir Charles. Then, when I had sent the letter, he stopped me from going to the gate. He said that it would hurt his feelings if another man gave me money. Afterwards, when I read about the death, he told me that people would suspect me. He frightened me into silence."

"You have had a lucky escape, Mrs Lyons," said Holmes. "We will not trouble you again."

On our way back to the station, Holmes received a telegram. "Lestrade is coming on the evening train from London," he said. "He is the best of the professionals at Scotland Yard, and we may need him tonight."

Chapter 10: The Hound of the Baskervilles

Inspector Lestrade arrived on the London train, and the three of us drove towards Merripit House in the dark. We left the carriage some distance away and walked the last part of the way. Holmes would not say what he expected, but I could feel his excitement. At last we stopped behind some rocks about two hundred yards from the house.

"We will wait here," said Holmes in a low voice. "Watson, go quietly towards the house and look in through the window, and tell me what you see."

I moved slowly along the path and looked into the dining room. Sir Henry and Stapleton were sitting at the table, smoking cigars and drinking wine. Sir Henry looked pale and nervous, and there was no sign of Mrs Stapleton. While I watched, Stapleton got up and left the room. I heard a door open and close, and then I saw him go into a small building in the garden. From inside it came a strange noise of movement. After a minute he came out, locked the door again, and went back to his guest. I returned to my friends and told them what I had seen.

"And the lady is not there with them?" asked Holmes.

"No, there is no sign of her."

"Then where can she be?"

But there was a bigger problem. A thick white fog was slowly coming towards us across the Grimpen Mire. It moved like a great white sea, and it was already reaching the house.

"This is very serious," whispered Holmes. "It is the one thing that could ruin my plans. If the fog covers the path, we will not be able to see Sir Henry. And our lives may depend on seeing him."

We moved back a little, to higher ground, but the fog came on until the house was hidden. We could see only the lights of the windows, like yellow stars in a white sea. Holmes was hitting the rock with his hand in his impatience.

At last the door opened, and we heard Sir Henry's quick footsteps on the path. He passed close to us, looking back over his shoulder like a man who was afraid. Then Holmes caught my arm.

"Listen!" he whispered. "It is coming!"

There was a sound of quick, soft running from somewhere inside the fog. I took out my revolver. Then, out of the fog, came the creature.

It was a hound, an enormous coal-black hound, but not a hound such as mortal eyes have ever seen. Fire burst from its open mouth, its eyes shone with a burning light, and its jaws and neck were outlined in flickering flame. Never in my worst dreams had I imagined anything more terrible than that dark animal. With long jumps it was racing down the path after our friend.

We were so shocked that for a moment we could not move, and the creature passed us. Then Holmes and I fired together, and the hound gave a terrible howl. One of the bullets had hit it, but it did not stop. Far away on the path we saw Sir Henry turn round, with his face white in the moonlight and his hands lifted in horror.

I have never seen any man run as Holmes ran that night. The hound jumped onto Sir Henry and threw him to the ground, and it was going for his throat. But at that moment Holmes reached it, and he emptied five bullets from his revolver into its side. With a last howl of pain the beast rolled over on its back, kicked the air with its four feet, and then lay still.

Sir Henry lay where he had fallen, and we opened his collar, and Holmes thanked God when he saw that there was no wound. Lestrade put his brandy bottle to Sir Henry's lips, and he opened his eyes.

"My God!" he whispered. "What in heaven was it?"

"It is dead, whatever it was," said Holmes. "We have killed the family ghost once and for all."

The dead animal lay in front of us. It was a mix between a bloodhound and a mastiff, and it was as large as a small lioness. Even now its great jaws seemed to drip with a blue flame. I touched its face, and my fingers shone in the dark.

"Phosphorus," I said in surprise.

"A clever preparation of it," said Holmes, smelling the dead animal. "It has no smell, so it would not stop the hound from following Sir Henry. We owe you a deep apology, Sir Henry, for frightening you like this. I did not expect such a creature, and the fog gave us very little time."

"You have saved my life, Mr Holmes."

"After first putting it in danger. Can you stand? Then let us go to the house. Stapleton must have heard the shots."

Chapter 11: The End of the Hunt

We left Sir Henry sitting on a rock and ran to Merripit House. The front door was open, but there was no sign of Stapleton. We searched every room until at last we came to a locked bedroom upstairs, and Lestrade broke the door open.

Inside the room, a figure was tied to a wooden post in the middle of the floor, with a towel round its face. It was Mrs Stapleton. When we untied her, we saw that there were red marks on her arms where her husband had beaten her.

"Is he safe?" she asked anxiously. "Has he escaped?"

"He cannot escape from us, madam," said Holmes.

"No, no, I did not mean my husband. Sir Henry! Is he safe?"

"Yes. And the hound is dead."

She gave a long sigh of happiness. "Thank God! That terrible man has hurt me in mind and body. I could bear everything while I still hoped that he loved me. But now I know that I was only his tool." Then her eyes became hard. "He has only one place to go. On an island in the middle of the mire there is an old tin mine. That is where he kept the hound. He has gone there."

But the fog was so thick that night that nobody could follow him. In the morning the fog lifted, and Mrs Stapleton led us to the path through the mire. Her husband had marked it with small sticks. On either side lay the green, soft mud, which was waiting to swallow anybody who made a false step. Halfway along, among the reeds, Holmes found something black. It was Sir Henry's old boot, the one that had been stolen in London. Stapleton had used it to give the hound Sir Henry's smell, and he had dropped it there as he ran.

We found no other trace of him, because the fog had been too thick that night, and the path had been invisible. Somewhere in the heart of the great Grimpen Mire, the cold mud had pulled him down forever. On the island we found the old mine, a chain, and the bones of the moor pony which the hound had eaten. There was also a tin of the shining paste which he had put on its face.

A month later, in Baker Street, on a cold and foggy evening, Holmes explained the whole story to me.

"Stapleton was the son of Rodger Baskerville, the younger brother of Sir Charles. Rodger had a bad name in England and went to South America, where he married. Everybody thought that he had died without children, but he had a son, who also had his father's name. This son married Beryl Garcia, a beauty from Costa Rica, and stole a large sum of money. Then he changed his name to Vandeleur and came to England. He opened a school in Yorkshire, but it failed, and he changed his name again, this time to Stapleton.

"He discovered that only two lives stood between him and a great fortune. So he came to Devonshire, made friends with Sir Charles and heard the old story of the hound. That gave him the idea for his crime. He bought the fiercest dog that he could find, from a shop in London, and hid it in the mire. He painted its face with phosphorus to make it look like a creature from hell.

"He used Mrs Lyons to bring Sir Charles to the gate at night, and then he let the hound go. The poor old man saw the terrible beast, ran for his life, and died of fear. The hound did not touch him. It only came close, and then Stapleton called it back.

"When Sir Henry came, Stapleton followed him in London with a false beard, and stole his boot. The first boot, the new one, had never been worn, so it was useless to the dog. That is why a second, old boot disappeared. His wife hated his plans, but she was afraid of him. She sent the warning letter made from the newspaper, and on the moor she tried to warn you and Sir Henry. That is why he tied her up last night."

"And what would he have done if he had succeeded?" I asked. "He could not suddenly appear in England and claim the Hall as a Baskerville."

"That is a very hard question, my dear Watson. Perhaps he could claim it from South America. Perhaps he would come to London in disguise. I am sure that a man like Stapleton would find a way."

Sir Henry had been badly shaken by that terrible night, and by the discovery that the woman he loved was another man's wife. Dr Mortimer was taking him on a long journey round the world, to help him recover. When he came back, he would be ready to live at Baskerville Hall at last, without fear.

Holmes stood up and stretched his long arms. "And now, my dear Watson, we have had some weeks of hard work. I think that one evening we can turn our thoughts to more pleasant things. I have a box for the opera tonight. Could you be ready in half an hour? We can stop at a restaurant on the way."

The End
