Chapter 1: A Man Who Lived Like a Clock

In the year 1872, a gentleman called Phileas Fogg lived at number 7, Savile Row, in London. He was tall and handsome, about forty years old, with fair hair and a calm face. Nobody knew very much about him. He was not a merchant, a banker or a lawyer. He had no wife and no children. He was rich, but nobody knew where his money came from.

Mr Fogg did everything at exactly the same time every day. He got up at eight. He ate his breakfast at twenty-three minutes past eight. At half past eleven he left his house and walked to the Reform Club, one of the great gentlemen's clubs of London. He always took the same number of steps. He ate lunch and dinner at the club, at the same table. He read the newspapers, and in the evening he played whist, a card game, with a few other members. At midnight, exactly, he went home to bed.

He spoke very little. He never hurried and he never seemed surprised. He knew a great deal about the world and its maps, although people said he had not left London for many years.

On the morning of the second of October, Mr Fogg was without a servant. He had dismissed his old servant, James Forster, the day before. The poor man had brought him shaving water at eighty-four degrees instead of eighty-six. For Mr Fogg, two degrees were a serious mistake.

At twenty past eleven, there was a knock at the door. A young man came in and bowed. He was about thirty, with a round, friendly face, strong shoulders and bright blue eyes.

"You are French, and your name is John?" asked Mr Fogg, who was sitting in his armchair.

"Jean, if you don't mind, sir," said the young man. "Jean Passepartout. It is a nickname. It means someone who can get out of any difficulty. I have had many jobs, sir. I have been a singer, a circus rider and a teacher of gymnastics. I was a fireman in Paris. But I left France five years ago, and now I want a quiet life. People say that you are the most regular man in England. That is why I am here."

"Passepartout suits me," said Mr Fogg. "What time is it by your watch?"

Passepartout took out an enormous silver watch. "Twenty-two minutes past eleven, sir."

"Your watch is four minutes slow. It does not matter. Just remember the difference. From this moment, twenty-nine minutes past eleven, on Wednesday the second of October, you are in my service."

Then Mr Fogg stood up, put on his hat and left the house, without another word.

Passepartout was alone. He walked round the house from the cellar to the roof. Everything was clean and tidy. In his own room he found a list on the wall. It told him what to do at every minute of the day, from eight in the morning until midnight.

"This is perfect," he said happily. "A man like a machine! I will have a calm life at last."

He was very wrong.

Chapter 2: The Bet at the Reform Club

That day the members of the Reform Club were talking about only one thing. Three days earlier, a thief had stolen fifty-five thousand pounds from the Bank of England. He had simply walked up to a cashier's desk and taken a pile of banknotes while the cashier was busy.

"The police will catch him," said Gauthier Ralph, who was one of the directors of the bank. "They have sent their best detectives to every port in Europe, America and the East. The bank is offering a reward of two thousand pounds."

"But the world is very big," said Andrew Stuart, an engineer. "The thief can hide anywhere."

"It was big once," said Mr Fogg quietly, as he shuffled the cards.

"What do you mean, once?" asked Stuart. "Has the world become smaller?"

"Mr Fogg is right," said Ralph. "Today a man can travel round the world ten times faster than a hundred years ago. Look at this article in the Morning Chronicle." He read it aloud:

"From London to Suez by train and boat, seven days. From Suez to Bombay by steamer, thirteen days. From Bombay to Calcutta by train, three days. From Calcutta to Hong Kong by steamer, thirteen days. From Hong Kong to Yokohama, six days. From Yokohama to San Francisco, twenty-two days. From San Francisco to New York by train, seven days. From New York to London by steamer and train, nine days. Total: eighty days."

"Eighty days!" laughed Stuart. "Yes, on paper. But what about storms, accidents and delays? What about trains that break down, ships that sink, wild animals and bad weather?"

"All included," said Mr Fogg, and he played a card.

"I would like to see you do it," said Stuart.

"Then let us go together."

"Never! But I will bet four thousand pounds that it is impossible."

"On the contrary, it is quite possible," said Mr Fogg. "I will bet twenty thousand pounds that I can travel round the world in eighty days or less. That means nineteen hundred and twenty hours, or one hundred and fifteen thousand two hundred minutes. Do you accept?"

The members looked at each other. Twenty thousand pounds was half of everything Mr Fogg owned. The other half, he explained, was for the journey.

"We accept," said Stuart, Ralph and the three other players, John Sullivan, Samuel Fallentin and Thomas Flanagan.

"Very well," said Mr Fogg. "There is a train to Dover at a quarter to nine this evening. I will take it. Today is Wednesday the second of October. So I must be back in this room, at this club, by a quarter to nine on Saturday the twenty-first of December. If I am not, the twenty thousand pounds will be yours."

They wrote down the bet and signed it. Then, because it was only seven o'clock, they finished their game of whist. Mr Fogg won twenty guineas. He did not look excited at all.

At ten past seven he walked home. Passepartout was amazed to see him. According to the list on the wall, his master should not come home until midnight.

"Passepartout," said Mr Fogg. "We are leaving for Dover and Calais in ten minutes."

The Frenchman's round face fell. "Is Monsieur going on a trip?"

"Yes. We are going round the world."

Passepartout's eyes opened wide. "Round the world!"

"In eighty days. So we have no time to lose. No trunks. Just a small bag, with two shirts and three pairs of socks for each of us. We will buy what we need on the way. Hurry."

Passepartout went to pack. "Round the world!" he said to himself. "And I wanted a quiet life!"

At eight o'clock they were ready. Mr Fogg put a thick bundle of banknotes into the bag. "Look after this carefully," he said. "There are twenty thousand pounds inside."

Passepartout nearly dropped it.

At Charing Cross station, the five gentlemen from the club came to say goodbye. Mr Fogg showed them his passport. "I will ask the consuls on my way to stamp it," he said. "Then you will see the dates, and you will know that I have really been to each place."

At exactly a quarter to nine, the whistle blew and the train left the station.

Suddenly Passepartout cried out in horror.

"What is the matter?" asked Mr Fogg.

"In my hurry, I forgot to turn off the gas in my room!"

"Then it is burning at your expense," said Mr Fogg calmly.

Chapter 3: A Detective at Suez

News of the bet soon spread across London. The newspapers wrote about it, and people started to make bets of their own. Most people thought that Mr Fogg was mad. "Eighty days!" they said. "It cannot be done."

Then, a week later, a telegram arrived at Scotland Yard from the port of Suez, in Egypt.

"I have found the bank robber, Phileas Fogg. Send a warrant to arrest him in Bombay. Fix, Detective."

At once everybody changed their minds. Of course! The calm gentleman of the Reform Club was the thief. The bet was only a clever trick to escape from England with the stolen money. And there was more evidence. The police had a description of the robber, and it looked very much like Mr Fogg.

Detective Fix was a small, thin man with sharp eyes. He was one of the detectives the police had sent to the ports. On the morning of the ninth of October he was walking up and down the quay at Suez, waiting for the steamer Mongolia, which was coming from Italy.

When the ship arrived, a young man came up to him with a passport in his hand. It was Passepartout.

"Excuse me, sir," he said politely. "Where is the British consul's office? My master needs a stamp in his passport."

Fix took the passport and looked at the description. His heart beat faster. Tall, fair hair, about forty years old. It was the robber, he was sure!

"Your master must go to the consul himself," he said. "The office is over there, at the corner of the square."

While Passepartout went back to the ship, Fix hurried to the consul.

"Sir, the bank robber is on the Mongolia," he said. "Please do not stamp his passport. Then he cannot continue his journey."

"I am sorry," said the consul. "If his passport is in order, I must stamp it. I cannot stop an honest traveller, and I cannot stop a thief without a warrant."

A few minutes later Mr Fogg came in, and the consul stamped his passport. Mr Fogg thanked him and went back to the ship to write in his notebook. Every day of the journey was in that notebook, with the date he should arrive at each place and the date he really arrived. So far, he was neither early nor late.

Meanwhile, Fix went to find Passepartout, who was walking around the market with wide eyes.

"So you are travelling with your master?" asked Fix in a friendly voice.

"Yes, and very fast," said Passepartout. "We left London a week ago, at a quarter to nine in the evening. I have not even had time to buy new shoes!"

"And where are you going?"

"Round the world! In eighty days! It is a bet, my master says. But between you and me, I do not believe it. There must be another reason."

"Is Mr Fogg rich?" asked Fix.

"Oh yes. He is carrying a huge pile of banknotes. And he spends money like water. He has promised the engineer of the Mongolia a large reward if we reach Bombay early."

Fix said nothing, but his eyes shone. Everything fitted. The man was running away with the stolen money. He sent his telegram to London and asked for the warrant. Then he bought a ticket and climbed aboard the Mongolia himself.

The ship crossed the Red Sea and the Indian Ocean. The sea was rough at times, but the Mongolia was fast. Mr Fogg hardly looked at the coasts they passed. He ate four meals a day and played whist with some other passengers from morning to night. He had found three good players: a tax collector, a priest and an officer of the British army called Sir Francis Cromarty, who was returning to his troops in Benares.

Passepartout, on the other hand, enjoyed every minute. He ate well, slept well and chatted with his new friend, Mr Fix.

"What a lucky meeting!" he said one day. "You are going to Bombay too?"

"Yes. I work for a shipping company," said Fix. "Will your master stay long in Bombay?"

"Not even one night. He will get on the train for Calcutta at once."

"I see," said Fix thoughtfully. And he began to worry. What if the warrant did not reach Bombay in time?

On the twentieth of October, the Mongolia arrived in Bombay. It was two days ahead of the timetable. Mr Fogg wrote the two days carefully in his notebook, under the heading "Gains".

Chapter 4: Shoes in the Temple and a Railway That Ends

Mr Fogg went straight to the passport office in Bombay, and then to the station restaurant for dinner. He had no wish to see the city. The train to Calcutta was leaving at eight o'clock that evening, and he told Passepartout to buy a few shirts and meet him at the station in good time.

Fix, meanwhile, ran to the police office. The warrant had not arrived. It was still on its way from London, and without it he could not arrest anyone. He decided to follow the thief across India and wait for his chance.

Passepartout, for his part, was beginning to understand that his master was serious. They really were going round the world, and in eighty days. He bought the shirts quickly, but then he could not resist a walk through the streets. Bombay was full of colour and noise. There were Persians in tall hats, merchants in long robes and people of every nation, and there was a festival in the city that day. Then he saw a wonderful temple on Malabar Hill, with gold on its walls and towers, and he walked straight in to admire it.

He did not know that Christians were not allowed inside that temple. Worse, he did not know that everybody had to take off their shoes before entering. Suddenly three angry priests jumped on him. They pulled off his shoes and socks and began to beat him, shouting loudly. But Passepartout was strong and quick, because he had once been a teacher of gymnastics. He knocked two of the priests down, escaped from the third and ran all the way to the station without his shoes, without his hat and without the shirts.

He arrived just five minutes before the train left. Out of breath, he told Mr Fogg the whole story.

"I hope this will not happen again," said Mr Fogg coldly, and he got into the train.

Fix had followed Passepartout to the temple and seen everything. Now he smiled to himself as the train left the station. He did not get on it. He had a new plan. "A crime has been committed on Indian soil," he thought. "That is enough to keep them here. I have my man."

In the train, Mr Fogg found an old friend from the Mongolia. It was Sir Francis Cromarty, the officer who had played whist with him. Sir Francis knew India very well, and he was going to join his troops in Benares. He thought that the bet was quite crazy, and he looked at his travelling companion with curiosity. Did this cold, calm man have a heart at all?

The train crossed hills and forests, rivers and plains, and Passepartout watched everything through the window. He saw jungles full of monkeys, little villages, temples and fields of cotton. Sometimes the train passed herds of elephants, which watched it go by with their small, wise eyes.

Then, on the morning of the twenty-second of October, at a village called Kholby, the train stopped. The conductor walked along the carriages calling, "Everybody out! Passengers get off here!"

Sir Francis asked a guard what was the matter.

"The railway is not finished, sir," said the guard. "There are fifty miles without rails between here and Allahabad. The trains start again over there."

"But the newspapers said that the railway was open across the whole of India!" said Sir Francis angrily.

"The newspapers were wrong, sir. The passengers know they must find their own way from Kholby to Allahabad."

Sir Francis was furious. Passepartout wanted to hit the guard. Mr Fogg was not even surprised.

"I expected an obstacle of this kind sooner or later," he said. "I am two days ahead of my timetable, so there is no harm done. We shall find a way."

The other passengers had already taken every cart, horse and carriage in the village. There was nothing left. Then Passepartout came back with an idea.

"Sir, I have found an elephant!"

The elephant belonged to an Indian man who lived near the village. Its name was Kiouni, and it was strong, young and fast. Its owner did not want to sell it. Mr Fogg offered to rent it for ten pounds an hour, then twenty, then forty. The man said no each time. So Mr Fogg offered to buy it for a thousand pounds. The owner still refused. Twelve hundred. Fifteen hundred. Eighteen hundred. At last, at two thousand pounds, the owner agreed.

"What a price for an elephant!" said Passepartout, who was pale.

They also found a guide, a young Parsee who knew the way through the forest. Mr Fogg offered him a good reward. Then they climbed onto Kiouni's back. Mr Fogg and Sir Francis sat in two little seats on either side, and Passepartout sat on the elephant's back between them. The guide sat on its neck, and they set off through the jungle.

Chapter 5: The Rescue in the Forest

The journey on the elephant was not comfortable. Kiouni moved quickly, but at every step the three travellers were thrown up and down like balls. Passepartout, who had been warned not to open his mouth, nearly bit his tongue in half. But the elephant made excellent progress through the thick forest, and by evening they had travelled more than half the way.

They spent the night in an old, broken hut. Wild animals howled in the darkness, but nothing attacked them. The next day they started again at six in the morning.

At about four in the afternoon, the guide suddenly stopped Kiouni. He listened carefully. From somewhere in the trees came the sound of voices, drums and music, coming closer.

"A procession," the guide whispered. "We must hide. These people do not want strangers to see them."

They led the elephant behind some bushes and waited in silence. Soon a strange procession came along the path. First came priests in long robes, singing sad songs. Behind them walked men and women playing drums and other instruments. Then came a group of men pulling a cart. On it lay the body of an old man, richly dressed, with jewels on his turban. It was a rajah, a local prince, who had died. Next to him walked a young woman with soldiers on either side of her. She was beautiful, and she was dressed in fine clothes and gold, but she walked slowly, as if in a dream.

Sir Francis looked very serious. "It is a suttee," he said quietly. "That poor woman is the widow of the rajah. Tomorrow morning, at sunrise, they will burn her alive with the body of her husband."

"The scoundrels!" cried Passepartout.

"But this custom is against the law now," said Mr Fogg.

"In most of India, yes," said Sir Francis. "But here, in the wild areas, the British have no power. These terrible things still happen."

The procession went past and disappeared among the trees. Then the guide told them more. The young woman was called Aouda. She was the daughter of a rich merchant from Bombay and had received an English education. When her parents died, she was married against her will to the old rajah. Now his family wanted her to die with him. She had tried to escape, but they had caught her and given her opium, so that she could not resist.

Mr Fogg was silent for a moment. Then he said, "I still have twelve hours to spare. Let us save that woman."

Sir Francis stared at him. "Mr Fogg, you have a heart after all!"

"Sometimes," said Mr Fogg simply. "When I have the time."

The guide agreed to help them. He knew Aouda's story, and he did not want her to die either. They followed the procession through the forest until it reached an old temple. There the priests built a great pile of wood for the fire. They laid the body of the rajah on it. Aouda was taken into the temple, with guards at the door.

The travellers waited for night. When it was dark, they crept towards the temple. But the guards were awake, with their swords in their hands. Then the travellers tried to cut a hole through the back wall of the temple, but other guards came, and they had to go back into the trees. Hours passed. The sky slowly began to grow lighter. They had no plan left.

"I will go myself," said Mr Fogg calmly. "I will run to the fire when they bring her out."

"You will be killed!" said Sir Francis.

But at that moment, Passepartout had disappeared.

At sunrise, the drums began again. The priests brought Aouda out of the temple, and she was laid on the wood beside her dead husband. Men lit the fire with torches, and smoke rose into the air. Mr Fogg was about to rush forward when Sir Francis held his arm.

Then something amazing happened. A great cry of fear came from the crowd. The dead rajah was moving! Slowly, through the smoke, he stood up on the pile of wood. He lifted the young woman in his arms and climbed down from the fire. He walked through the crowd like a ghost, and all the people fell to the ground with their faces in the dust. Nobody dared to look at him.

The "ghost" walked straight to Mr Fogg and Sir Francis.

"Let's go!" he said in a low voice.

It was Passepartout! In the darkness and the smoke, he had climbed onto the pile of wood, lain down in the rajah's place and covered himself with the dead man's long cloak. Now he was carrying the sleeping woman in his arms.

A moment later, they were all running into the forest. They put Aouda on the elephant, and the guide drove Kiouni away as fast as he could. Behind them, the priests realised that they had been tricked. They began to shout and shoot arrows and bullets, but the travellers were soon too far away.

Sir Francis shook Passepartout's hand warmly. "My dear fellow, you are a hero!"

"Oh, it was nothing," said Passepartout modestly. But his face was red with pride. "Do you know, sir," he whispered to Sir Francis, "I think that I have been a dead man for the first time in my life!"

Chapter 6: Trouble in Calcutta

At ten o'clock they reached the railway station at Allahabad. Aouda slowly woke up from her sleep. When she understood what had happened, she thanked her rescuers with tears in her eyes. She spoke perfect English, and she had a gentle, intelligent face.

Mr Fogg now had to pay the guide. He gave him the money that he had promised. Then, to everybody's surprise, he gave him something else as well.

"You have been loyal and brave," he said. "Kiouni is yours."

The young Parsee could not believe his luck. An elephant worth two thousand pounds! He would be rich for the rest of his life. Passepartout, who had grown fond of the elephant, gave Kiouni some lumps of sugar, and Kiouni lifted him into the air with its trunk as a goodbye.

"Mrs Aouda cannot stay in India," said Sir Francis. "Sooner or later, the rajah's family will find her. They will kill her."

"Then she will come with us to Hong Kong," said Mr Fogg. "She has a relative there, a rich merchant, who can look after her."

Sir Francis left them at Benares to join his troops. The others went on to Calcutta, where they arrived at seven in the morning on the twenty-fifth of October. The steamer to Hong Kong left at noon. Mr Fogg had neither gained nor lost a day.

But as they came out of the station, a policeman stopped them.

"Mr Phileas Fogg? Mr Passepartout? Please follow me."

They were taken to a court, where a judge was waiting. To Passepartout's horror, three Indian priests were there too. They were the same priests from the temple in Bombay! And on the judge's table lay a pair of shoes. They were his own shoes.

"My shoes!" he cried, before he could stop himself.

Fix, of course, had arranged everything. He had found the priests in Bombay and brought them to Calcutta by the fastest train, because he needed a reason to keep Mr Fogg in India until the warrant arrived. He was sitting at the back of the court, watching with great satisfaction.

The judge listened to the priests. Then he looked at Passepartout. "You have admitted that these are your shoes. You entered a holy place and insulted its priests. I sentence you to fifteen days in prison and a fine of three hundred pounds. And because a master is responsible for his servant, Mr Fogg will spend eight days in prison and pay one hundred and fifty pounds."

Passepartout felt sick. Fifteen days! The bet was lost, and it was his fault.

But Mr Fogg remained calm. "I offer bail," he said.

"Bail is two thousand pounds," said the judge.

Mr Fogg took a thick bundle of banknotes out of the bag and paid at once. Then he picked up the shoes, gave them to Passepartout, and walked out of the court. They were free until the trial, and they did not intend to come back for it.

"Two thousand pounds for a pair of shoes!" sighed Passepartout. "They are the most expensive shoes in the world."

Fix was furious. "He leaves two thousand pounds behind as if it were nothing!" he thought. "Well, it is stolen money, so he does not care. But I will follow him to the end of the world if I must."

The steamer Rangoon left Calcutta at noon, with Mr Fogg, Aouda and Passepartout on board, and Fix hidden in a cabin below. The voyage to Hong Kong was long. The ship stopped for coal at Singapore, and the passengers walked among the palm trees and spice gardens. Aouda grew stronger every day. She was grateful to Mr Fogg, and she watched him with growing admiration. But he was always polite, correct and cold, like a perfect machine. Passepartout told her all about his master and the famous bet.

One morning Passepartout found Fix on deck, and he was amazed.

"Mr Fix! You here? Are you going round the world too?"

"No, no," said Fix, smiling. "My company has sent me to Hong Kong. What a coincidence!"

Passepartout thought it was a rather strange coincidence. He began to watch Fix, and soon he had a theory of his own. "I know what he is!" he thought. "He is a spy from the Reform Club. The gentlemen have sent him to check that my master really goes round the world." He decided to say nothing and to laugh about it secretly.

After Singapore, the weather changed. A strong wind blew from the north, and the sea became wild. For two days the Rangoon moved slowly through enormous waves. Passepartout was in despair, and he shouted at the captain, at the sea and at the wind. Fix, on the other hand, was delighted. Every hour of delay was good for him. Mr Fogg simply played whist and showed no feeling at all.

The Rangoon finally reached Hong Kong on the sixth of November, a day late. Now there was another problem. The steamer for Yokohama, the Carnatic, was supposed to leave that very morning.

But at the port they heard some good news. The Carnatic had needed repairs to her boilers, and she would not leave until the next morning. Mr Fogg had not lost his day after all.

Chapter 7: A Pipe of Opium and a Little Boat

Mr Fogg took Aouda to a hotel in Hong Kong and then went to look for her relative, the rich merchant Jejeeh. He came back with bad news. Jejeeh had left Hong Kong two years before and was now living somewhere in Holland, on the other side of the world.

Aouda was silent for a moment, and her dark eyes filled with tears. "What must I do, Mr Fogg?" she asked quietly.

"It is very simple," he replied. "You will come with us to Europe."

"But I cannot accept so much from you. I am already a great trouble to you."

"You are not a trouble at all, and you do not upset my programme," said Mr Fogg. "Passepartout, go to the Carnatic and book three cabins."

Passepartout was delighted, because he liked Aouda very much and he was glad that she was coming with them. He hurried down to the port, whistling happily. There he found Fix walking up and down the quay with a dark face. The warrant had still not arrived in Hong Kong, and Hong Kong was the last British territory on the route. After this, Fix could no longer arrest Mr Fogg anywhere until they were back in England.

At the shipping office, Passepartout heard something important. The repairs to the Carnatic had been finished early, and the ship would leave that same evening at eight o'clock instead of the next morning.

"That is excellent news!" said Passepartout. "My master will be very pleased. I will go and tell him at once."

At that moment Fix made a decision. "Come and have a drink with me first," he said, and he took Passepartout's arm. "I have something important to tell you."

He led him into a tavern by the harbour. It was a dark, smoky room, and on the beds along the walls men lay sleeping with long pipes beside them. It was an opium house. Fix ordered two bottles of port wine, and when Passepartout had drunk a few glasses, the detective began to speak in a low voice.

"Listen to me. I am not who you think. I am a police detective, and your master is the man who robbed the Bank of England. I have been following him since Suez. Help me to keep him in Hong Kong until my warrant arrives, and I will give you half of the reward. That is a thousand pounds for you."

Passepartout listened with his mouth open. Then he jumped up angrily and banged his fist on the table.

"Mr Fogg, a thief? Never! He is the most honest and generous man in the world. Do you know how he saved Mrs Aouda? Do you know how much he gave to the guide? I will never betray him, not for all the money in England!"

Fix saw that he could not buy the young man. So he tried a different plan. While Passepartout was talking, he put a pipe of opium into his hand. Passepartout, whose head was already full of wine, smoked it without thinking. A few minutes later he fell asleep on one of the beds, and Fix walked out of the tavern alone. Now Mr Fogg would not know that the Carnatic was leaving early.

The next morning, Mr Fogg and Aouda went down to the port with their bags. There was no Passepartout, and there was no Carnatic either. The ship had left the evening before, and Passepartout had not come back. Mr Fogg did not show any feeling. He simply began to look for another ship to take them to Yokohama.

On the quay, Fix came up to him, pretending to be another unhappy traveller. "Have you also missed the Carnatic, sir? How unlucky! There will be no other ship for a week."

But Mr Fogg did not intend to wait a week. He walked along the harbour until he found a small, fast pilot boat called the Tankadere. Her captain was a sailor named John Bunsby, a man with a brown face and clever eyes.

"Can you take me to Yokohama?" asked Mr Fogg.

"In this little boat, sir? It is a voyage of eleven or twelve hundred miles, and it is the season of storms. It is too dangerous."

Mr Fogg thought for a moment. He knew that the American steamer for San Francisco did not leave from Yokohama. It started its journey from Shanghai, and only stopped at Yokohama on its way. "Then take me to Shanghai," he said. "It is only eight hundred miles. I will pay you one hundred pounds a day, and two hundred more if we arrive in time."

The captain agreed at once. Mr Fogg turned politely to Fix and invited him to come with them. Fix did not want to accept a favour from the man he planned to arrest. But he could not let his thief escape, so he climbed on board.

Before they left, Mr Fogg spent several hours searching the city for Passepartout. Nobody had seen him. Then, at three in the afternoon, the Tankadere set sail. For two days she flew over the water like a bird, while Aouda sat on the deck watching the waves. Then, as Bunsby had feared, a typhoon came. The sky turned black, and the wind roared like a wild animal. Enormous waves broke over the little boat, which was thrown up and down for a whole night. Every person on board expected to drown. Mr Fogg sat calmly at the back, and Aouda did not complain once.

At last the storm passed, but they had lost precious time. When they came near Shanghai, on the evening of the eleventh of November, they could see the American steamer coming out of the river. It was leaving for Yokohama, and they were still too far away.

"Signal her!" said Mr Fogg.

Bunsby fired the little cannon on the deck and raised the flag upside down. That was the signal for a ship in trouble. The great steamer saw it, turned slowly and came towards them.

Chapter 8: The Long Noses of Yokohama

But what had happened to Passepartout?

Three hours after Fix had left him, he woke up in the opium house with a terrible headache. One idea came into his mind: the Carnatic! He jumped up, ran to the port like a madman, and reached the ship just before she left. He fell on the deck, and the sailors carried him to a cabin, where he slept until the next morning.

When he woke up, the ship was far out at sea. He soon discovered the terrible truth. His master and Aouda were not on board. He had never told Mr Fogg that the ship was leaving early, and now it was too late. Passepartout pulled his hair in despair.

"It is my fault!" he cried. "That Fix made me drunk to stop my master! Oh, if I see him again, he will be sorry!"

He arrived in Yokohama on the thirteenth of November with nothing. He had no money, no friends, and he could not speak a word of Japanese. His fare had been paid by Mr Fogg at the shipping office, so at least he had not been thrown into the sea. But he was very hungry. For a whole day he walked through the streets of the city, among temples, tea houses, paper lanterns and gardens full of cherry trees. He sold his European clothes to buy some rice, and he put on an old Japanese robe instead.

Then, in a street near the port, he saw a man carrying a large poster. It said in English:

"ACROBATIC JAPANESE TROUPE OF THE HONOURABLE WILLIAM BATULCAR. LAST PERFORMANCES BEFORE THEIR DEPARTURE FOR THE UNITED STATES. THE LONG NOSES! THE LONG NOSES!"

"The United States!" thought Passepartout. "That is exactly where I need to go!"

He went to the theatre and found the Honourable William Batulcar, an American who looked more like a pirate than a gentleman. Passepartout told him that he could sing, dance and stand on his hands, because he had once worked in a circus. Batulcar needed a strong man for his show, and he hired him at once.

The star of the evening was the human pyramid of the Long Noses. The acrobats wore long wooden noses, some of them six or eight feet long, and they built a tower of bodies, standing on each other's noses. Passepartout's job was to lie at the bottom of the pyramid and hold up the noses of all the others.

That evening, the theatre was full. In the middle of the show the great pyramid rose higher and higher towards the roof, while the audience cheered. Suddenly, Passepartout looked up and saw two faces in the front row. One of them was the calm face of Phileas Fogg!

With a great shout, Passepartout jumped up. The whole pyramid fell down like a house of cards, and acrobats and noses crashed onto the stage. Passepartout climbed over the seats and threw himself at his master's feet.

"Ah, my master! My master!"

"Ah, it is you," said Mr Fogg calmly. "Come. We are leaving."

Batulcar was shouting that he wanted money for the broken pyramid, so Mr Fogg gave him a handful of banknotes. Then the three of them left the theatre and went straight to the port. The Long Noses were left behind for ever.

On the way, Passepartout learned how the Tankadere had been carried to Yokohama by the American steamer. Mr Fogg had come to the theatre by chance, while he was searching every corner of the city for his servant. They were all together again, and they had not lost a day. That evening, the General Grant set sail for San Francisco.

The voyage across the Pacific Ocean lasted twenty-two days. The weather was fine, and Passepartout told Aouda and his master the story of his adventures. But he did not say a word about Fix's confession. Perhaps Mr Fogg would not believe it, and perhaps it would only upset him. He decided to wait.

Then one day he saw a familiar face on deck. Fix was on board too! The detective had sailed on the General Grant, and he was hiding in a cabin. Passepartout did not hesitate. He walked straight up to Fix and hit him hard, several times. The passengers who saw it were very impressed. Fix got up slowly, with a bruised face.

"Have you finished?" he asked.

"For the moment, yes."

"Then let me speak to you. I have changed my mind about your master. Until now I have tried to stop him. From now on, I want to help him."

"Ah!" said Passepartout. "So you now believe that he is honest?"

"No, I still think he is a thief. But in America I have no power to arrest him, and it seems that he really is returning to England. So I will help him to get there as quickly as possible. In England, we will see who is right. Until then, we are on the same side."

Passepartout looked at him for a long time. "All right," he said. "We are friends again, but only until England. And if you try one more trick, I will break your neck."

"Agreed," said Fix.

Chapter 9: Across America by Train

The General Grant arrived in San Francisco on the third of December. Mr Fogg had lost no time and gained no time either. The train for New York left at six o'clock that evening, so the travellers had a few hours to visit the city. The streets were full of busy people from every country, and there were tall buildings, banks and shops everywhere.

There happened to be an election in San Francisco that day, and in the main square two groups of supporters were shouting at each other. Suddenly they began to fight. Mr Fogg and Fix, who were walking past with Aouda, were caught in the middle of the crowd. A big man with a red beard punched Fix, and then he tried to hit Mr Fogg, but Fix stepped in front of him and took the blow himself. He lost his hat and had a black eye, but Mr Fogg was not hurt.

"Who are you, sir?" asked Mr Fogg.

"Colonel Stamp W. Proctor," said the American rudely.

"Then we will meet again, Colonel. An Englishman does not forget an insult."

"Whenever you like, sir," said the colonel, and he disappeared into the crowd.

That evening the travellers took their seats on the great train. The railway from San Francisco to New York was more than three thousand miles long, and it crossed mountains, deserts and enormous plains. The carriages were long and comfortable, with a passage down the middle, and there were sleeping cars for the night.

The journey across America was full of surprises. On the second day, a huge herd of bison crossed the line in front of the train. There were ten or twelve thousand of them, and they moved slowly across the rails for three hours, while the engine waited and the passengers grew impatient. Passepartout was so angry that he wanted to shoot at them, but Mr Fogg only waited calmly. Later, Passepartout listened to a long lecture by a Mormon missionary about life in Salt Lake City, which made him think that one wife was quite enough for any man.

A few days later, in the mountains near Medicine Bow, the train stopped again. A signalman came running along the line.

"You cannot go any further! The bridge over the river is damaged. It will not hold the weight of a train."

The bridge was a long, high construction made of wood and iron. It crossed a deep valley, and at the bottom of the valley a river rushed over the rocks. The passengers got out of the train and began to argue about what they should do. Some wanted to walk across the mountains. Some wanted to wait for help.

Then the engine driver made a suggestion. "If we drive at our top speed," he said, "we may be able to cross the bridge before it breaks."

The passengers were frightened at first, but the Americans were people who liked bold ideas, and soon nearly everybody agreed. Only Passepartout thought there might be a better way.

"It would be more sensible," he said, "if the passengers walked across first, and then the train came after them."

But nobody listened to him. Everybody got back into the carriages. The train went back a mile, and then it rushed forward again, faster and faster, until it was flying along at a hundred miles an hour. It hardly touched the rails. It crossed the bridge like a flash of lightning and reached the other side. A second later, the bridge broke behind them and fell with a terrible noise into the river below.

"So, Mr Passepartout," said Fix with a smile, "you see that sometimes it is better to go fast."

Passepartout did not reply. But in his heart he was very happy to be alive.

Chapter 10: The Attack on the Train

The train continued eastwards across the high plains of Wyoming and Nebraska. For two days the journey was quiet, and Mr Fogg spent his time playing whist with Aouda, Fix and another passenger. Then, one morning, a man stopped beside their table and looked down at Mr Fogg with a scornful smile. It was Colonel Stamp W. Proctor, the man from San Francisco.

"So it is you, the Englishman," he said. "You play cards very badly."

Mr Fogg stood up calmly. "Colonel Proctor, I believe that we have a little matter to settle."

The two men agreed to fight a duel with pistols at the next station. But the conductor came with a better idea. The train would not stop for long, he said, so they could fight in the last carriage, which was empty. The other passengers were sent out of it, and the two men went inside, each with a revolver in his hand. They had to fire when the train whistled.

Aouda was pale with fear. Everyone waited. But before the whistle came, they heard wild cries and gunshots outside. A band of Sioux warriors had attacked the train! They had climbed onto the carriages from their horses, and some of them had even taken control of the engine. Bullets flew through the windows, and the passengers fought back with their revolvers. Mr Fogg and the colonel came out of the empty carriage and began to fight side by side, against the attackers instead of each other.

The engine driver had been knocked out, and nobody was driving the train. It was rushing forward faster and faster. The conductor shouted that they must stop it before Fort Kearney, a military station two miles ahead, where soldiers could help them. If they passed the fort, they were lost.

"I will stop it," said Passepartout.

Before anyone could stop him, he opened a door and slipped under the carriage. Holding on to the chains and iron bars, he climbed along beneath the train, hidden from the warriors, until he reached the engine. There, with enormous effort, he unhooked the engine from the carriages. The engine raced away along the line, and the carriages slowly came to a stop near Fort Kearney. Soldiers ran out of the fort, and the attackers rode away across the plain.

When the passengers were counted, several were wounded. Colonel Proctor had a bullet in his arm. And three people were missing, among them Passepartout. The warriors had taken them prisoner.

Mr Fogg did not hesitate for a second. He knew that if he went to look for Passepartout, he would probably lose the train and perhaps the bet. But he turned to Aouda and said simply, "I will find him, dead or alive."

"Ah, Mr Fogg!" she cried, and she took his hands.

He asked the captain of the fort for help. Thirty soldiers volunteered to go with him, and they rode off into the snowy plain. Aouda stayed at the station with Fix. Hours passed, and night came. The train left without them. At last, the next morning, they heard gunshots in the distance. Mr Fogg and the soldiers were coming back, and Passepartout and the other two prisoners were with them! They had found the Sioux camp ten miles to the south and set them free after a short fight.

Passepartout was full of joy. Then he saw that the train had gone, and his face fell. "It is my fault again," he said. "We have lost twenty hours."

Fix was worried too, because now he wanted Mr Fogg to reach England. He thought hard and then remembered a man who had spoken to him at the station. His name was Mudge, and he had a strange vehicle: a sledge with a sail. It was a kind of boat on runners, which flew over the frozen snow when the wind blew behind it.

Mr Fogg hired it at once. The travellers wrapped themselves in blankets and fur coats, and the sledge set off across the white plain towards Omaha, where they could catch another train. The cold was terrible, but the wind was strong, and the sledge moved at forty miles an hour. Once a pack of hungry wolves ran after them, howling, but the sledge was too fast for them. Passepartout, his nose red with cold, sang French songs to keep warm.

They reached Omaha that afternoon and jumped onto a train just as it was leaving. They crossed Iowa and Illinois and arrived in Chicago, and from there an express train took them to New York. They reached the port on the evening of the eleventh of December, and they ran to the pier of the Cunard company. The China, the steamer for Liverpool, had left forty-five minutes earlier.

Chapter 11: A Ship Made of Wood

The China had been their last hope, it seemed. The next steamers to Europe did not leave for several days. Passepartout was in despair, because he believed that he had ruined his master. If he had not been captured by the Sioux, they would have caught the China easily. Aouda said nothing, but she watched Mr Fogg sadly.

Mr Fogg simply said, "We shall see tomorrow," and took them to a hotel.

The next morning he went down to the river Hudson alone and walked along the quays, looking at every ship. At last he found a merchant ship that was getting ready to leave. She was called the Henrietta, and she had both sails and a steam engine. Her captain was a man of about fifty with red hair, a hard face and a loud voice. His name was Andrew Speedy.

"Are you leaving soon?" asked Mr Fogg.

"In an hour."

"Where are you going?"

"To Bordeaux, in France."

"Will you take me and three other people to Liverpool?"

"No. I am going to Bordeaux, and I never take passengers," said the captain. "Passengers are always a nuisance."

"Then will you sell me your ship?"

"No."

"Will you take us to Bordeaux, then, for two thousand dollars each?"

The captain scratched his head. Eight thousand dollars was a lot of money, and he was going to Bordeaux anyway. "I leave at nine o'clock," he said. "Be here on time."

At nine o'clock the travellers were on board. But Mr Fogg had no intention of going to Bordeaux. During the night, while the captain was asleep, he went down to talk to the crew. He paid them generously, and the sailors agreed to follow his orders. The next morning, the captain was locked in his own cabin, and Phileas Fogg was standing at the wheel. The Henrietta was sailing towards Liverpool.

Captain Speedy was furious. He shouted and banged on the door of his cabin all day, but nobody let him out. Passepartout, who brought him his meals, was careful to keep his distance.

It turned out that Mr Fogg was an excellent sailor. The Henrietta sped across the Atlantic, with her sails full of wind and her engine working day and night. For a while there was a storm, but Mr Fogg did not slow down. Then, on the eighteenth of December, the engineer came to him with bad news.

"We have no more coal, sir. We started with only enough for a short trip. We have been burning it at full speed for days."

Mr Fogg thought for a moment, then he asked to see the captain. Captain Speedy was brought up from his cabin, red with anger.

"Pirate!" he shouted. "Thief! Where is my ship?"

"Captain," said Mr Fogg politely, "I would like to buy your ship."

"Never! Never, never!"

"I need to burn her."

"Burn my ship! A ship worth fifty thousand dollars!"

"Here are sixty thousand," said Mr Fogg, and he held out a bundle of banknotes. "But I want only the wooden parts. You may keep the iron hull and the engine."

Captain Speedy stopped shouting and looked at the money. He calculated quickly. The Henrietta was twenty years old, and she was not worth half that price. Suddenly his anger disappeared completely.

"And I keep the iron hull?" he asked.

"The hull and the engine. Is it agreed?"

"Agreed!" said the captain, and he shook Mr Fogg's hand very warmly.

From that moment, the sailors began to cut the ship to pieces and burn them in the engine. First they burned the cabins and the beds. Then they burned the furniture, the masts, the boats on the deck, and finally the deck itself. Passepartout chopped wood all day with great energy. Captain Speedy watched his old ship disappear and did not know whether to laugh or cry.

On the twentieth of December they saw the coast of Ireland. There was nothing left of the Henrietta except her iron hull, but she was still moving. But there was not enough wood left to reach Liverpool in time. So Mr Fogg decided to stop at Queenstown, an Irish port where the mail steamers called. From there a fast train ran to Dublin, and from Dublin fast boats crossed to Liverpool. That way they could arrive many hours earlier.

He said goodbye to Captain Speedy, who was now quite happy. Then the travellers left the ship. At one in the morning they took the train to Dublin, and from there a fast steamer to Liverpool. At twenty to twelve on the twenty-first of December, Phileas Fogg stepped onto English soil. London was only six hours away by train, and he had more than nine hours left. He had won.

Then Fix came up to him and put a hand on his shoulder.

"Are you Phileas Fogg?"

"I am."

"In the name of the Queen, I arrest you."

Chapter 12: Five Minutes Too Late

Mr Fogg was taken to the police station at the Customs House in Liverpool and locked in a small room. He would have to stay there until he was sent to London for trial.

Passepartout wanted to throw himself on Fix, but policemen held him back. Aouda could not understand what was happening, and she wept. Passepartout told her everything about Fix and the stolen money. "It is my fault," he groaned. "I knew he was a detective, and I did not warn my master!"

Inside the little room, Mr Fogg sat on a wooden bench, without any sign of anger. He took his watch out of his pocket and put it on the table in front of him. He watched the hands move. It was the last day of the bet, and the hours were going by.

One o'clock passed. Then two o'clock passed. If he left now, by a special train, he could still reach the Reform Club before a quarter to nine.

At thirty-three minutes past two, there was a noise outside. The door opened, and Aouda, Passepartout and Fix rushed in. Fix's hair was untidy, and he was so out of breath that he could hardly speak.

"Sir," he said, "forgive me. A terrible mistake. The real thief was arrested three days ago in Edinburgh. You are free!"

Phileas Fogg stood up. He looked straight at the detective. Then, with the only quick movement he had made in his whole life, he pulled back his arms and knocked Fix to the ground with both fists.

"Well hit!" cried Passepartout happily.

Fix lay on the floor and said nothing. He knew that he had deserved it.

Mr Fogg, Aouda and Passepartout took a cab to the station. The express train for London had just left. So Mr Fogg hired a special train, and at three o'clock it set off at top speed. But there were delays on the line. At ten minutes to nine, the special train arrived in London, and the clocks of the city were striking the hour. Phileas Fogg had travelled round the world, and he was five minutes late.

He had lost the bet.

Mr Fogg went home to Savile Row without a word. His fortune was gone. He had spent nearly all the money in the bag on the journey, and the twenty thousand pounds at his bank now belonged to the gentlemen of the Reform Club. Passepartout turned off the gas in his room, which had been burning for eighty days. Then he sat in a corner and cried. He was sure that it was all his fault.

The next day was very sad. Mr Fogg stayed in his room all day. Passepartout watched his door, because he was afraid that his master might do something desperate. In the evening, Mr Fogg asked to speak to Aouda.

"Madam," he said, "I brought you to England because I wanted to give you a safe and comfortable life. I was rich then. Now I am a poor man, but I still have a little money, and I want you to have it."

"And what will you do, Mr Fogg?"

"I need nothing."

Aouda stood up and took his hand. "Mr Fogg," she said, "would you like to have a friend and a relative? Would you like me to be your wife?"

At these words, Mr Fogg stood up too. His eyes shone, and his lips trembled a little. For the first time, the cold, perfect machine looked like a man.

"I love you," he said simply. "Yes, by all that is holy in the world, I love you, and I am entirely yours."

"Ah!" cried Aouda, and she pressed his hand to her heart.

They called Passepartout, who was delighted. Mr Fogg sent him to the Reverend Samuel Wilson to arrange the wedding for the next day, Monday.

It was five minutes past eight. Passepartout ran to the church. But ten minutes later he came running back, without his hat, red in the face and so breathless that he could hardly speak.

"Master!" he cried. "The wedding... it is impossible tomorrow!"

"Why?"

"Because tomorrow is Sunday!"

"Monday," said Mr Fogg.

"No! Today is Saturday!"

"Saturday? Impossible!"

"Yes, yes! You made a mistake of one day! We arrived twenty-four hours early! But there are only ten minutes left!"

Passepartout seized his master by the collar, pulled him out of the house and pushed him into a cab. He promised the driver a hundred pounds, and they flew through the streets of London.

At the Reform Club, the five gentlemen were sitting in the great room, watching the clock. It was twenty to nine.

"He has lost," said Stuart. "The last train from Liverpool came in at seven twenty-three."

"Do not be too sure," said Fallentin. "You know how exact he is."

At eight forty-four, nobody spoke. Stuart was already counting his money. The clock ticked. Fifty-five seconds, fifty-six, fifty-seven. At the fifty-seventh second, the door opened.

"Here I am, gentlemen," said Phileas Fogg.

How had he made a mistake of one day? It was very simple. He had travelled round the world towards the east, towards the rising sun. Every time he crossed a line of longitude, the sun rose a little earlier for him. In total, his days had been shorter by four minutes for each degree, and the three hundred and sixty degrees of the earth made exactly twenty-four hours. So he had seen eighty sunrises, while his friends in London had seen only seventy-nine. Passepartout's old silver watch still showed London time, but a watch cannot count days, and nobody had looked at a calendar.

Mr Fogg had won twenty thousand pounds, but he had spent about nineteen thousand on the journey. He shared the difference between Passepartout and the unhappy Fix, whom he had already forgiven. But he took from Passepartout the price of the gas that had burned for one thousand nine hundred and twenty hours.

Two days later, Phileas Fogg married Aouda, and Passepartout gave away the bride.

So what had Mr Fogg gained from his journey round the world? Nothing at all, some people said. Nothing, except a charming wife, who made him the happiest of men. And surely, for that, any man would go round the world.

The End
