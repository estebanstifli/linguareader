Chapter 1: Scrooge and Marley

Marley was dead, and there is no doubt about that. He had died seven years before this story begins, on a cold Christmas Eve. Scrooge had signed the paper at the funeral, and Scrooge was his only friend, his only partner and the only person who came to say goodbye.

Even after seven years, the words "Scrooge and Marley" were still painted above the door of their office, because Scrooge never spent money on paint if he could avoid it. Sometimes new customers called him Scrooge and sometimes Marley, and he answered to both names, as it was all the same to him.

Oh, but he was a hard man, this Scrooge! He was tight-fisted and cold, and he loved only one thing in the world: money. The coldness inside him froze his old features, sharpened his pointed nose and made his lips thin and blue. Nobody ever stopped him in the street to greet him, beggars never begged him for a penny, and children never asked him the time. Even the dogs of blind men pulled their masters away when they saw him coming.

One Christmas Eve, old Scrooge sat busily working in his counting house. The weather was cold and foggy, and although it was only three o'clock, it was already quite dark. The fog came in through every crack and keyhole. Outside, people hurried up and down the street, beating their hands together and stamping their feet to keep warm.

The door of Scrooge's room was open, so he could watch his clerk. The clerk's name was Bob Cratchit, and he worked in a small, dark room that was more like a cupboard. Scrooge had a very small fire, but Bob's fire was even smaller. It looked like one single piece of coal. Bob could not add more, because Scrooge kept the coal box in his own room. So the clerk put on his long white scarf and tried to warm himself at the candle. As he was not a man of strong imagination, he did not succeed.

"A merry Christmas, Uncle! God save you!" cried a cheerful voice.

It was Fred, the only son of Scrooge's dead sister, who had hurried so energetically through the fog that his face was glowing and his eyes were sparkling.

"Bah!" said Scrooge. "Humbug!"

"Christmas a humbug, Uncle? You don't mean that, I'm sure."

"I do," said Scrooge. "What right have you to be merry, when you're so poor?"

"Come, then," replied Fred happily. "What right have you to be miserable, when you're so rich?"

Scrooge had no better answer ready, so he said "Bah!" again, and then "Humbug!"

"What else can I be," asked Scrooge, "when I live in a world of fools like this? What is Christmas to you but a time for paying bills without money? A time for finding yourself a year older and not an hour richer? If I could do what I wanted, every idiot who goes about with Merry Christmas on his lips would be boiled in his own pudding!"

"Keep Christmas in your own way, and let me keep it in mine."

"But you don't keep it at all," said Fred. "I have always thought of Christmas as a good time. A kind, forgiving, pleasant time. It is the only time of the year when men and women open their shut-up hearts and think of poorer people as fellow travellers, and not as another race of creatures. It has never put a piece of gold in my pocket, Uncle, but I believe it has done me good, and it will do me good. So I say, God bless it!"

In the cupboard, Bob Cratchit clapped his hands without thinking. Then he realised his mistake and quickly poked the fire. He only put out the last little spark.

"Let me hear another sound from you," Scrooge told him, "and you'll keep your Christmas by losing your job!"

Fred did not give up. "Come and have dinner with us tomorrow, Uncle, because my wife would be delighted."

Scrooge said that he would see him in a very bad place first.

"Why did you get married?" asked Scrooge.

"Because I fell in love."

"Because you fell in love!" growled Scrooge, as if that was the only thing in the world more foolish than a merry Christmas. "Good afternoon."

"I want nothing from you, Uncle, and I ask nothing, so why can't we be friends?"

"Good afternoon," said Scrooge.

"I'm sorry to find you so stubborn, but I came here in honour of Christmas, and I'll keep my Christmas spirit to the end. So, a merry Christmas, Uncle, and a happy New Year!"

"Good afternoon!" said Scrooge.

Fred left the room without an angry word. He stopped at the door to wish a merry Christmas to the clerk, and Bob, though he was cold, wished him the same warmly.

As Fred went out, two other men came in. They were large, pleasant gentlemen with books and papers in their hands.

"Scrooge and Marley's, I believe," said one of them. "Do I have the pleasure of speaking to Mr Scrooge or Mr Marley?"

"Mr Marley has been dead for seven years," replied Scrooge. "He died seven years ago, this very night."

"At this happy time of the year, Mr Scrooge," said the gentleman, taking up a pen, "we try to provide a little help for the poor and destitute. Thousands of people have no food, no warm clothes and no fire. We are collecting money to buy them meat, drink and coal. How much can we write down for you?"

"Nothing!" replied Scrooge. "I wish to be left alone. Are there no prisons? Are there no workhouses, where the poor can live and work?"

"There are. But many people cannot go there, and many would rather die."

"If they would rather die," said Scrooge, "they had better do it, and decrease the extra population. Besides, it isn't my business. A man should understand his own business and not interfere with other people's. Mine keeps me busy all the time. Good afternoon, gentlemen!"

The two gentlemen saw that it was useless to argue, and they left.

The fog grew thicker and the cold grew sharper. A boy stopped at Scrooge's keyhole and began to sing a Christmas song:

"God bless you, merry gentlemen! May nothing you dismay!"

Scrooge picked up his ruler so angrily that the singer ran away in fear, leaving the keyhole to the fog.

At last, it was time to close the office, and Bob immediately blew out his candle and put on his hat.

"You'll want all day tomorrow, I suppose?" said Scrooge.

"If it's convenient, sir."

"It's not convenient," said Scrooge, "and it's not fair. I pay you a day's wages for no work."

"It's only once a year, sir," said Bob politely.

"A poor excuse for picking a man's pocket every twenty-fifth of December!" said Scrooge, buttoning his coat to the chin. "But I suppose you must have the whole day. Be here very early the next morning!"

Bob promised that he would, and Scrooge walked out with a growl. The office was closed in a moment. The clerk had no coat, so with the long ends of his white scarf hanging below his waist, he ran home to Camden Town as fast as he could. On the way, he slid down the icy street twenty times behind a line of boys, in honour of Christmas Eve.

Chapter 2: Marley's Ghost

Scrooge ate his miserable dinner in his usual miserable restaurant. He read all the newspapers, and then he went home to bed.

He occupied the rooms that had once belonged to his dead partner. They were in a gloomy old building at the end of a dark yard. Nobody lived there except Scrooge, because all the other rooms were let as offices, so at night the building was empty and silent.

Now, there was nothing special about the knocker on the front door. It was large, but it was an ordinary iron knocker. Scrooge had seen it every night and morning for years. He was also a man with very little imagination. But as Scrooge put his key in the lock, he looked up, and the knocker was not a knocker any more. It was Marley's face.

It was not angry. It looked at Scrooge as Marley used to look, with its ghostly glasses pushed up on its forehead. Its hair moved strangely, as if there was a hot wind. Its eyes were wide open, but they did not move. It was horrible.

As Scrooge stared at this phenomenon, it became a knocker again.

He was surprised, but he turned the key, walked in and lit his candle. Before he shut the door, he looked carefully behind it, half expecting to see Marley's hair sticking out into the hall. But there was nothing on the back of the door. "Pooh, pooh!" he said, and closed it with a bang.

He walked slowly up the wide staircase in the dark, because darkness is cheap and Scrooge liked it. Before he shut his heavy door, he walked through all his rooms and looked under the table, under the bed and behind his dressing gown on the wall, but nobody was hiding there. Then he locked himself in, which was not his custom, put on his nightcap and sat down in front of a very low fire with a bowl of soup.

As he sat there, his eyes happened to rest upon an old, unused bell that hung in the room. To his astonishment, the bell began to swing. Softly at first, and then loudly, it rang. Every bell in the house rang with it.

Then the bells stopped together, and Scrooge heard a noise far below, in the cellar. It sounded like a person dragging a heavy chain over the wine barrels. The cellar door flew open with a boom, and the noise came up the stairs. It came straight towards his door.

"It's humbug still!" said Scrooge, "and I won't believe it."

But his face changed when, without a pause, the thing came through the heavy door and into the room in front of his eyes.

It was Marley. The same face, the same coat, the same boots. The chain he dragged was wrapped around his middle. It was long, and it was made of cash boxes, keys, padlocks, account books and heavy purses made of steel. His body was transparent, so Scrooge could see the two buttons on the back of his coat.

"Well!" said Scrooge, as cold as ever. "What do you want with me?"

"Much!" It was Marley's voice, no doubt about it.

"Who are you?"

"Ask me who I was."

"Who were you, then?"

"In life I was your partner, Jacob Marley. You don't believe in me," said the Ghost.

"I don't," said Scrooge. "You may be a piece of bad meat, or a bit of cheese, or an underdone potato. There's more of gravy than of grave about you, whatever you are!"

Scrooge was not in the habit of making jokes, and he did not feel funny in his heart. He was trying to be clever only to keep his terror down, because the ghost's voice frightened him to the bones.

At this, the spirit gave a terrible cry and shook its chain with such a dreadful noise that Scrooge held on to his chair. Then the ghost took off the cloth that was tied around its head, and its lower jaw dropped down onto its chest!

Scrooge fell on his knees and covered his face. "Mercy!" he cried. "Dreadful ghost, why do you trouble me?"

"Do you believe in me or not?"

"I do, I must," said Scrooge. "But why do spirits walk the earth, and why do they come to me?"

"Every man must travel among other people while he is alive," replied the Ghost. "He must go out and help them. If he does not do it in life, he must do it after death. He must wander through the world and see the happiness that he could have shared, but cannot share now. Oh, woe is me!"

Again the spirit cried out and shook its chain.

"You are in chains," said Scrooge, trembling. "Tell me why."

"I wear the chain I made in life," replied the Ghost. "I made it link by link, and yard by yard, of my own free will. Do you recognise it? Your own chain was as heavy and as long as this, seven Christmas Eves ago. You have worked hard on it since. It is a huge chain now!"

"Jacob," he begged, "old Jacob Marley, tell me more and say something to comfort me!"

"I have no comfort to give," the Ghost replied. "I cannot rest or stay anywhere. In life, my spirit never walked beyond our counting house. Mark me! It never left the narrow hole where we counted money. And now I must make long, tiring journeys for ever."

"But you were always a good man of business, Jacob," said Scrooge, who was beginning to think about himself.

"Business!" cried the Ghost, wringing its hands. "Mankind was my business. The common good was my business. Kindness, mercy and patience were all my business. The dealings of my trade were only a drop of water in the great ocean of my business!"

It held up its chain, as if that was the cause of all its sadness, and threw it heavily on the ground again.

"Hear me!" cried the Ghost. "My time is nearly gone, and I am here tonight to warn you that you still have a chance of escaping my fate, a chance that I obtained for you, Ebenezer."

"You were always a good friend to me," said Scrooge. "Thank you!"

"You will be visited," said the Ghost, "by three spirits."

Scrooge's face fell almost as low as the Ghost's jaw had fallen. "Is that the chance you mentioned, Jacob?" he asked in a shaking voice. "I think I would rather not."

"Without their visits," said the Ghost, "you cannot hope to avoid my path. Expect the first tomorrow, when the bell strikes one. Expect the second on the next night at the same hour. The third will come on the next night, when the last stroke of twelve has stopped. Look to see me no more. And for your own sake, remember what has passed between us!"

The Ghost walked backwards towards the window. With every step it took, the window rose a little, so that when the spirit reached it, it was wide open.

Scrooge heard strange noises in the air outside. They were sounds of sorrow and regret, cries of pain. The Ghost listened for a moment, and then it joined in the sad song and floated out into the dark, cold night.

Scrooge went to the window and looked out. The air was full of ghosts, wandering here and there in restless hurry, and every one of them wore chains like Marley's. One old ghost, with a huge iron safe tied to its ankle, was crying miserably because it could not help a poor woman with a baby on a doorstep below. They all wanted to do good in human matters, and they had lost the power to do it for ever.

Then the ghosts and their voices faded together into the fog, and the night became as it had been when he walked home.

Scrooge closed the window and checked the door. It was locked, as he had left it. He tried to say "Humbug!" but he stopped at the first syllable. He was exhausted by the emotions of the day and his glimpse of the invisible world, so he went straight to bed without undressing and fell asleep immediately.

Chapter 3: The Ghost of Christmas Past

When Scrooge awoke, it was so dark that he could hardly distinguish the window from the walls. A church clock was striking the hour, and he listened to count the strokes.

It struck twelve, which was impossible, because it had been past two when he went to bed, so an icicle must have got into the machinery.

"Why, it isn't possible," said Scrooge, "that I have slept through a whole day and far into another night. Has something happened to the sun? Is it twelve at noon?"

He got out of bed and rubbed the frost from the window with the sleeve of his dressing gown. All he could see was that it was still very foggy and extremely cold. There was no noise of people running about, as there would be if night had beaten bright day and taken over the world.

He went back to bed and thought, and thought, and thought about it. Every time he decided that it was, his mind flew back, like a strong spring, to the same question: "Was it a dream or not?"

Then he remembered that the Ghost had warned him of a visit when the bell struck one. He decided to lie awake until the hour had passed. The quarter of an hour was so long that he thought he had fallen asleep more than once. At last, he heard the bell.

He counted the chimes as they came: a quarter past, half past, a quarter to, and then the hour itself. "And nothing else!" said Scrooge, with a sigh of relief.

But as the deep, dull, hollow bell struck one, light flashed up in the room, and the curtains of his bed were pulled open by a hand.

Scrooge found himself face to face with a strange visitor. It was like a child, and yet it was like an old man. Its hair was white, as if with age, but its face had not a single wrinkle and its skin was soft and pink. It wore a white robe with a belt of beautiful shining light. It held a branch of fresh green holly in its hand, but its dress was decorated with summer flowers. The strangest thing of all was a clear, bright jet of light that sprang from the top of its head. Under its arm, it carried a great cap, which it could use to put the light out.

"Are you the spirit whose coming was foretold to me?" asked Scrooge.

"I am!" The voice was soft and gentle, and strangely low, as if it came from far away.

"Who and what are you?"

"I am the Ghost of Christmas Past."

"Long past?" asked Scrooge.

"No, your past."

Scrooge politely asked what business had brought the spirit there.

"Your welfare!" said the Ghost. "Rise, and walk with me!"

It took his arm gently. Scrooge wanted to say that the weather was too cold for walking, and that he was only wearing his slippers, his dressing gown and his nightcap. But the hand, though gentle as a woman's, was impossible to resist. The spirit walked towards the window.

"I am a mortal," Scrooge protested, "and I will fall."

"Just let my hand touch you there," said the Spirit, laying it on his heart, "and you will be held up in more than this!"

As the words were spoken, they passed through the wall and stood on an open country road, with fields on either side. The city had completely disappeared. The darkness and the fog had disappeared with it, because it was a clear, cold winter day, with snow upon the ground.

"Good heavens!" said Scrooge, holding his hands together as he looked around him. "I was born in this place. I was a boy here!"

The Ghost looked at him gently. Its soft touch seemed to remain on the old man's heart. He was aware of a thousand smells floating in the air, and each one was connected with a thousand thoughts, and hopes, and joys, and cares, long, long forgotten.

"Your lip is trembling," said the Ghost, "and what is that on your cheek?"

Scrooge said, with an unusual catch in his voice, that it was nothing. He asked the Ghost to lead him wherever it wanted.

They walked along the road, and Scrooge recognised every gate, every post and every tree. Soon a little market town appeared in the distance, with its bridge, its church and its winding river. Some shaggy ponies trotted towards them with boys on their backs, who called to other boys in carts. They were all in great spirits and shouted to each other until the fields were full of happy music.

"These are only shadows of the things that have been," said the Ghost. "They cannot see us or hear us."

The travellers came nearer, and Scrooge knew every one of the boys and called them by name. His cold eye shone and his heart jumped up as they went past, wishing each other a merry Christmas on their way home for the holidays. And yet what was a merry Christmas to Scrooge, and what good had it ever done to him?

"The school is not quite empty," said the Ghost. "A lonely child, forgotten by his friends, is left there still."

Scrooge said he knew it, and he cried.

They came to a large mansion of dull red brick, which had once been grand, although now its walls were damp and its windows broken. They went into a long, bare, sad room with lines of old wooden desks. At one of these, a lonely boy was reading near a weak fire. Scrooge sat down at a desk and cried to see his poor forgotten self as he used to be.

The Spirit touched him on the arm and pointed to his younger self, who was deep in a book. Suddenly a man in foreign clothes stood outside the window, wonderfully real, with an axe in his belt, leading a donkey loaded with wood.

"Why, it's Ali Baba!" Scrooge cried in delight. "It's dear old honest Ali Baba! One Christmas, when that child was left here all alone, he came for the first time, just like that. And there's Robinson Crusoe's parrot, with its green body and yellow tail, and there's Friday, running for his life to the little creek!"

Then, with a sudden change of feeling, he said, "Poor boy!" and cried again.

"I wish," Scrooge murmured, putting his hand in his pocket and looking around him, after drying his eyes with his sleeve, "but it's too late now."

"What is the matter?" asked the Spirit.

"Nothing," said Scrooge, "nothing at all. There was a boy singing a Christmas song at my door last night. I should like to have given him something, that's all."

The Ghost smiled thoughtfully and waved its hand, saying, "Let us see another Christmas!"

The room became a little darker and dirtier. Scrooge's younger self was older now, and he was walking up and down, alone again. All the other boys had gone home for the jolly holidays.

The door opened, and a little girl, much younger than the boy, came running in. She put her arms around his neck and kissed him again and again.

"Dear, dear brother," she said. "I have come to bring you home! Father is so much kinder than he used to be, and home is like heaven! He spoke to me so gently one night that I wasn't afraid to ask him again if you could come home, and he said yes! We're going to be together all the Christmas long, and have the merriest time in all the world!"

"You are quite a woman, little Fan!" exclaimed the boy.

She clapped her hands and laughed, and pulled him towards the door. A trunk was carried down to a carriage, and the children drove away, singing, through the snow.

"She was always a delicate creature," said the Ghost, "whom a breath might have withered. But she had a large heart!"

"So she had," cried Scrooge. "You're right, Spirit, and I won't deny it, God forbid!"

"She died a woman," said the Ghost, "and had, I think, children."

"One child," Scrooge replied.

"True," said the Ghost, "your nephew!"

Scrooge seemed uncomfortable, and answered shortly, "Yes."

Chapter 4: Fezziwig and Belle

Although they had only just left the school, the Ghost and Scrooge were now standing in the busy streets of a city, where shadowy people passed and shadowy carts fought for space. It was Christmas time again, and the shops were lit up for the evening. The Ghost stopped at the door of a warehouse and asked Scrooge if he knew it.

"Know it!" said Scrooge. "I was an apprentice here!"

They went inside, where an old gentleman in a Welsh wig was sitting behind a high desk. He was so tall that his head nearly touched the ceiling.

"Why, it's old Fezziwig! Bless his heart, it's Fezziwig alive again!"

Old Fezziwig put down his pen and looked at the clock, which pointed to seven. He rubbed his hands, laughed from his shoes to his wig and called out in a rich, fat, jolly voice, "Yo ho, there! Ebenezer! Dick!"

Scrooge's younger self, now a young man, came in quickly with his fellow apprentice.

"No more work tonight, my boys!" cried Fezziwig. "It's Christmas Eve, Dick. It's Christmas, Ebenezer! Let's have the shutters up and clear away everything!"

In a minute, the young men had moved every desk and chair. The floor was swept, the lamps were trimmed and more coal was thrown on the fire, until the warehouse was as warm and bright as a ballroom. In came a fiddler with his music book, who sat at the high desk and tuned up his violin. In came Mrs Fezziwig, one big friendly smile. In came the three Miss Fezziwigs, who were beautiful and loved, and the six young men whose hearts they had broken. In came all the young people who worked in the business, the housemaid with her cousin the baker, and the cook with her brother's best friend, the milkman.

They danced, and then they danced again. There were cakes, a great piece of cold roast meat, mince pies and plenty of beer. When the fiddler struck up the old dance called "Sir Roger de Coverley", Mr and Mrs Fezziwig stood up to lead it. They danced so well that light seemed to shine from Fezziwig's legs. At eleven o'clock the party ended. Mr and Mrs Fezziwig stood at the door and shook hands with every person as they went out, wishing them a merry Christmas.

During the whole time, Scrooge had acted like a man who had lost his mind. His heart and soul were in the scene with his former self. He remembered everything, enjoyed everything and felt everything.

"Such a small matter," said the Ghost, "to make these silly people so full of gratitude. He has spent only three or four pounds of your mortal money. Does he deserve so much praise?"

"It isn't that, Spirit," said Scrooge, speaking unconsciously like his younger self. "He had the power to make us happy or unhappy, to make our work light or heavy, a pleasure or a pain. His power lay in words and looks, in things so small that it is impossible to count them. The happiness he gave was as great as if it had cost a fortune."

He felt the Spirit's eyes on him, and stopped.

"What is the matter?" asked the Ghost.

"Nothing, except that I should like to say a word or two to my clerk just now."

"My time grows short," said the Spirit. "Quick!"

The scene changed again. Scrooge saw himself as a man in the prime of life. His face did not yet have the hard lines of later years, but it had begun to show signs of worry and greed. There was a restless, eager look in his eye, which showed the passion that had taken root in his heart.

He was not alone. He was sitting next to a pretty young woman in a black dress, and there were tears in her eyes.

"It matters little," she said softly, "and to you, very little. Another idol has taken my place in your heart. If it can comfort you in time to come, as I would have tried to do, I have no just cause to be sad."

"What idol has taken your place?" he asked.

"A golden one."

"This is the fairness of the world!" he said. "There is nothing it treats so hard as poverty, and nothing it criticises so severely as the search for wealth!"

"You fear the world too much," she answered gently. "I have seen your nobler hopes fall away, one by one, until the master passion, gain, takes all of you. Haven't I?"

"What then? Even if I have grown so much wiser, I have not changed towards you."

She shook her head. "Our promise to marry is an old one, made when we were both poor and contented to be so, until we could improve our fortune by patient work. You are changed. When it was made, you were another man. If you were free today, Ebenezer, would you choose a girl without money? I know you would not. So I release you, with a full heart, for the love of the man you once were."

He was about to speak, but she turned away from him and went on.

"You may feel pain at this, but only for a very short time. Then you will forget it gladly, like a useless dream from which you were lucky to wake. May you be happy in the life you have chosen!"

She left him, and they parted.

"Spirit!" said Scrooge. "Show me no more, but take me home! Why do you enjoy torturing me?"

"One shadow more!" exclaimed the Ghost.

It took him to a warm, comfortable room, where a beautiful young girl was sitting near the fire. Scrooge thought at first that it was the same young woman, until he saw her, now a pleasant middle-aged mother, sitting opposite her daughter. The room was very noisy, because there were more children in it than Scrooge could count. They were laughing, shouting and climbing all over their mother, and she laughed with them. Then the father came home, loaded with Christmas presents, and the children attacked him with screams of joy.

Later, when the house was quiet, the husband sat down with his wife by the fire.

"Belle," he said with a smile, "I saw an old friend of yours this afternoon."

"Who was it?"

"Mr Scrooge. I passed his office window, and as the shutters were open and he had a candle inside, I could hardly avoid seeing him. His partner lies close to death, I hear. And there he sat alone. Quite alone in the world, I believe."

"Spirit!" said Scrooge in a broken voice. "Remove me from this place."

"I told you these were shadows of the things that have been," said the Ghost. "Do not blame me for what they are!"

"Remove me!" Scrooge cried. "I cannot bear it! Leave me, take me back and haunt me no longer!"

He seized the extinguisher cap and pressed it down hard on the Spirit's head. But though Scrooge pressed it down with all his strength, he could not hide the light, which streamed from under it in an unbroken flood upon the ground. He was exhausted, and he felt very sleepy. He realised that he was back in his own bedroom, and he fell into a heavy sleep.

Chapter 5: The Ghost of Christmas Present

Scrooge woke in the middle of a loud snore and sat up in bed. He knew that the bell was about to strike one again. This time he wanted to be ready, so he pulled back the curtains of his bed himself and waited.

The bell struck one, but no spirit appeared. Five minutes, ten minutes and a quarter of an hour passed, and nothing came. All this time, Scrooge lay in a bright red light which poured into his bed from the next room. At last he got up, went quietly to the door and looked through.

The moment Scrooge touched the lock, a strange voice called him by his name and told him to enter. He obeyed.

It was his own room, but it had changed completely. The walls and ceiling were covered with fresh green holly, ivy and mistletoe, and the leaves reflected the light like little mirrors. A huge fire roared in the fireplace, which had never known such a fire in Scrooge's time or Marley's. On the floor, forming a kind of throne, were turkeys, geese, great joints of meat, sausages, mince pies, plum puddings, barrels of oysters, red-hot chestnuts, rosy apples, juicy oranges and bowls of steaming punch. On top of this pile sat a jolly giant, who held up a glowing torch shaped like a horn.

"Come in!" cried the Ghost. "Come in, and know me better, man!"

Scrooge entered nervously and hung his head before this Spirit. He was not the stubborn Scrooge he had been, and although the Spirit's eyes were clear and kind, he did not like to meet them.

"I am the Ghost of Christmas Present," said the Spirit. "Look upon me!"

Scrooge obeyed, and observed that the Ghost was dressed in a simple green robe, decorated with white fur. Its feet were bare, and on its head it wore a crown of holly with shining icicles. Its dark brown curls were long and free, and its face was full of joy.

"Spirit," said Scrooge humbly, "take me where you will. I went out last night unwillingly, and I learned a lesson that is working now. Tonight, if you have anything to teach me, let me profit by it."

"Touch my robe!" commanded the Spirit, and Scrooge held it tight.

The holly, the food, the fire and the room all disappeared, and they stood in the city streets on Christmas morning. The weather was severe, and people were making a rough but cheerful music as they cleared the snow from the pavements and the roofs of their houses. The boys were throwing snowballs and laughing loudly when they hit their targets, and loudly again when they missed.

Soon the church bells called all good people to church, and they came streaming along the streets in their best clothes and with their happiest faces. At the same time, from many side streets and lanes, poor people came out carrying their dinners to the bakers' shops to be cooked, because they had no ovens of their own. The Spirit seemed very interested in these poor people. It stood with Scrooge in a baker's doorway, and when the bearers passed, it lifted the covers of their dinners and sprinkled incense from its torch on the food. Once, when some men began to argue in the crowd, the Ghost shook a few drops of water from its torch on them, and their good humour came back immediately. They said it was a shame to quarrel on Christmas Day.

Then they went on, invisible as they had been before, into the outer parts of the town. Perhaps it was its kind and generous nature, and its sympathy for all poor men, that led it straight to the house of Scrooge's clerk. On the doorstep, the Spirit smiled, stopped to bless Bob Cratchit's home with the drops from its torch, and went in.

Think of that! Bob earned only fifteen shillings a week, and yet the Ghost of Christmas Present blessed his four-roomed house!

Chapter 6: Christmas at the Cratchits'

Mrs Cratchit, Bob's wife, was dressed in a cheap old gown, but she had decorated it bravely with ribbons, which are cheap and make a fine show for sixpence. She was laying the cloth on the table, helped by Belinda Cratchit, her second daughter, who was also brave in ribbons. Master Peter Cratchit was sticking a fork into the saucepan of potatoes. He was wearing one of his father's enormous shirt collars, which Bob had lent him in honour of the day, and he was very proud to be so finely dressed. Two smaller Cratchits, a boy and a girl, came running in, screaming that outside the baker's they had smelt the goose and known it for their own.

"Where is your precious father, then?" said Mrs Cratchit. "And your brother, Tiny Tim? And Martha was not as late as this last Christmas Day!"

"Here's Martha, Mother!" said a girl, appearing as she spoke.

Martha worked for a dressmaker, and she had worked late the night before to finish her orders. Her mother kissed her a dozen times and took off her shawl and bonnet.

Then in came little Bob, the father, with at least three feet of scarf hanging down in front of him and Tiny Tim on his shoulder. Poor Tiny Tim! He carried a little crutch, and his legs were supported by an iron frame. Martha ran into her father's arms, and he kissed her happily. The two young Cratchits carried Tiny Tim off to the kitchen, so that he could hear the pudding singing in the pan.

"And how did little Tim behave?" asked Mrs Cratchit.

"As good as gold," said Bob, "and better. He becomes thoughtful, sitting by himself so much, and he thinks the strangest things you ever heard. He told me, coming home, that he hoped the people saw him in the church, because he was a cripple. He said it might be pleasant for them to remember on Christmas Day who made lame beggars walk and blind men see."

Bob's voice trembled when he told them this, and it trembled more when he said that Tiny Tim was growing strong and healthy.

Then the goose arrived, and there was such a bustle that you might think a goose was the rarest of all birds. Mrs Cratchit made the gravy hissing hot. Peter mashed the potatoes with incredible energy. Belinda sweetened up the apple sauce. Martha dusted the hot plates, and the two young Cratchits put chairs for everybody, not forgetting themselves. At last the dishes were set on the table, and grace was said. When Mrs Cratchit plunged the carving knife into the breast of the goose, a murmur of delight arose all round the board. Even Tiny Tim beat on the table with the handle of his knife and cried, "Hurrah!"

There never was such a goose. It was not very large, and it was not very expensive, but with the apple sauce and mashed potatoes it was a sufficient dinner for the whole family.

Then Mrs Cratchit left the room alone, too nervous to have witnesses, to take the pudding out of the pan. Suppose it was not done enough! Suppose it broke when she turned it out! Suppose somebody had climbed over the wall of the back yard and stolen it while they were eating the goose! But in half a minute Mrs Cratchit entered, flushed and smiling proudly, with the pudding. It was like a hard round cannonball, blazing in brandy, with a branch of Christmas holly stuck into the top.

Oh, a wonderful pudding! Bob Cratchit said, calmly too, that he regarded it as the greatest success achieved by Mrs Cratchit since their marriage. Everybody had something to say about it, but nobody said or thought that it was a small pudding for a large family. That would have been an unforgivable crime.

When dinner was finished, the cloth was cleared, the fire was made up, and the whole family drew round the hearth. They drank hot apples and lemons mixed with gin from two glasses and a custard cup without a handle. Bob raised his glass and said:

"A merry Christmas to us all, my dears. God bless us!"

The whole family repeated it.

"God bless us every one!" said Tiny Tim, the last of all.

He sat very close to his father's side upon his little stool. Bob held his thin little hand in his, as if he loved the child and wished to keep him by his side, and was afraid that he might be taken from him.

"Spirit," said Scrooge, with an interest he had never felt before, "tell me if Tiny Tim will live."

"I see an empty seat," replied the Ghost, "in the poor chimney corner, and a crutch without an owner, carefully preserved. If these shadows remain unaltered by the future, the child will die."

"No, no," said Scrooge. "Oh no, kind Spirit, say he will be spared!"

"If these shadows remain unaltered, none of my race will find him here. What then? If he is going to die, he had better do it, and decrease the extra population."

Scrooge hung his head to hear his own words repeated by the Spirit, and he was overcome with sadness and regret.

"Man," said the Ghost, "if man you are in heart, and not stone, stop that wicked talk until you have discovered what the extra population is, and where it is. Will you decide what men shall live and what men shall die? It may be that, in the sight of Heaven, you are more worthless and less fit to live than millions like this poor man's child."

Scrooge bent before the Ghost's words and looked down at the ground, trembling. But he raised his eyes quickly when he heard his own name.

"Mr Scrooge!" said Bob. "I'll give you Mr Scrooge, the Founder of the Feast!"

"The Founder of the Feast indeed!" cried Mrs Cratchit, turning red. "I wish I had him here. I'd give him a piece of my mind to feast upon, and I hope he'd have a good appetite for it."

"My dear," said Bob, "the children! Christmas Day."

"I'll drink his health for your sake and the day's," said Mrs Cratchit, "not for his. Long life to him! A merry Christmas and a happy New Year! He'll be very merry and very happy, I have no doubt!"

The children drank the toast after her, but without any pleasure. Scrooge was the ogre of the family. The mention of his name cast a dark shadow on the party, which did not lift for a full five minutes.

After it had passed away, they were ten times merrier than before, and they sang songs. Tiny Tim sang one about a lost child travelling in the snow, in a sad little voice, and he sang it very well indeed.

They were not a handsome family. They were not well dressed, and their shoes were far from waterproof. But they were happy, grateful and pleased with one another. When they faded away in the bright sprinkling of the Spirit's torch, Scrooge kept his eye on them, and especially on Tiny Tim, until the last.

Chapter 7: Fred's Party and Two Strange Children

It was getting dark and snowing heavily when, without a word of warning from the Ghost, they stood upon a bleak and empty moor. Huge stones were scattered about, and nothing grew but moss. Even here, in a little cottage, a family of miners sat singing an old Christmas song round a glowing fire. The Spirit did not stay long. It carried Scrooge over the sea to a lonely lighthouse on a rock, where two men who watched the light had made a fire and wished each other a merry Christmas. Then it took him to a ship far out on the black, heaving sea. Every man on board, good or bad, had a kinder word for another on that day than on any day in the year.

Suddenly, it was a great surprise to Scrooge to hear a hearty laugh. It was a much greater surprise to recognise it as his own nephew's, and to find himself in a bright, dry, shining room, with the Spirit standing smiling by his side.

"Ha, ha, ha!" laughed Scrooge's nephew. "He said that Christmas was a humbug, as I live, and he believed it too!"

"More shame for him, Fred!" said Scrooge's niece angrily. She was a very pretty woman, with a dimpled face and a pair of the sunniest eyes you ever saw.

"He's a comical old fellow," said Fred, "that's the truth. And not so pleasant as he might be. However, his faults carry their own punishment, and I have nothing to say against him."

"I'm sure he is very rich, Fred," said his wife.

"What of that, my dear? His wealth is of no use to him, because he doesn't do any good with it or even make himself comfortable with it. I am sorry for him, and I couldn't be angry with him if I tried. Who suffers from his bad temper? Only himself. He refuses to come and dine with us, and the result is that he loses a very good dinner, and some pleasant company."

Everybody agreed that he lost a very good dinner, because they had just eaten it.

"I mean to give him the same chance every year, whether he likes it or not," Fred went on, "because I pity him. He may laugh at Christmas until he dies, but he can't help thinking better of it, I am sure. If I can make that poor clerk of his a little richer, that's something."

After tea, they had some music. Fred's wife played the harp well, and she played a simple little tune that Scrooge's sister Fan used to sing. When this music sounded, all the things the Ghost of Christmas Past had shown him came back to Scrooge's mind. He grew softer and softer, and he thought that if he had listened to such music years ago, he might have found happiness with his own hands.

Then they played games, because it is good to be children sometimes, and never better than at Christmas. First, they played blind man's buff. Then they played a game called Yes and No, in which Fred had to think of something, and the rest had to find out what it was. He could only answer their questions with yes or no. It was an animal, a disagreeable, savage animal, that growled and grunted sometimes, and talked sometimes, and lived in London. It walked about the streets, but it was not led by anybody, and it did not live in a zoo. At every new answer, Fred laughed so much that he had to get up off the sofa and stamp. At last, his wife's plump sister cried out:

"I have found it out, Fred! I know what it is!"

"What is it?" cried Fred.

"It's your Uncle Scro-o-o-oge!"

And so it was, and everyone was delighted, and when they had stopped laughing, Fred raised his glass.

"He has given us plenty of fun, I am sure," he said, "and it would be ungrateful not to drink his health. Uncle Scrooge! A merry Christmas and a happy New Year to the old man, wherever he is!"

Scrooge had become so light and happy that he wanted to thank them all in a speech that they could not hear. He begged the Ghost to let him stay for one more game, as a child might ask. But the whole scene passed away with the last word spoken by his nephew, and he and the Spirit were travelling again.

They visited many homes and saw much, and always with a happy ending. The Spirit stood beside sick beds, and the sick were cheerful. It went to poor homes and prisons, and to hospitals and workhouses, and it left its blessing everywhere.

It was a long night, if it was only a night, and Scrooge noticed that the Ghost was growing older, clearly older, with hair that was turning grey.

"Are spirits' lives so short?" asked Scrooge.

"My life upon this globe is very brief," replied the Ghost, "and it ends tonight, at midnight. Listen, the time is near!"

Then Scrooge noticed something strange sticking out from under the Spirit's robe. "Forgive me," he said, "but is that a foot or a claw?"

From the folds of its robe, the Ghost brought out two children. They were miserable, ugly, frightful creatures, a boy and a girl. They were thin and dressed in rags, and their faces were pinched and twisted, like old people's. They knelt down at the Spirit's feet and clung to its robe.

"Spirit, are they yours?" Scrooge could say no more.

"They are Man's," said the Spirit, looking down upon them. "This boy is Ignorance. This girl is Want. Beware of them both, and of all their kind, but most of all beware of this boy. On his forehead I see the word Doom, unless the writing is erased."

"Have they no shelter, no help?" cried Scrooge.

"Are there no prisons?" said the Spirit, turning on him for the last time with his own words. "Are there no workhouses?"

The bell struck twelve.

Scrooge looked about him for the Ghost, and saw it not. As the last stroke ended, he remembered the warning of old Jacob Marley. Lifting up his eyes, he saw a solemn phantom, wrapped in a long dark cloak, coming like a mist along the ground towards him.

Chapter 8: The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come

The Phantom moved slowly, seriously and silently towards him. When it came near, Scrooge fell down on his knees, because the very air through which it moved seemed to be filled with gloom and mystery. It was covered in a deep black robe, which hid its head, its face and its shape, and left nothing visible except one outstretched hand.

"Am I in the presence of the Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come?" asked Scrooge.

The Spirit did not answer, but pointed onward with its hand.

"Ghost of the Future!" he exclaimed. "I fear you more than any spirit I have seen, but I know your purpose is to do me good, and I hope to live to be another man from what I was. I am prepared to go with you, and I do it with a thankful heart. Will you not speak to me?"

It gave him no reply, but the hand was pointed straight before them, so Scrooge followed it.

They seemed to enter the city, or rather, the city seemed to rise around them. They were in the heart of it, among the merchants and businessmen, who hurried up and down, looked at their watches and talked in little groups. The Spirit stopped beside one small group, and Scrooge went forward to listen.

"No," said a great fat man with a huge chin, "I don't know much about it, except that he's dead."

"What has he done with his money?" asked a red-faced gentleman.

"I haven't heard," said the man with the large chin, yawning. "He hasn't left it to me, that's all I know. It's likely to be a very cheap funeral, because I don't know anybody who will go to it. Shall we make up a party and volunteer?"

"I don't mind going if lunch is provided," said the red-faced gentleman. Everybody laughed, and the group moved away.

Scrooge knew these men well, and he was surprised that the Spirit thought such a conversation important. He looked around for his own image, but another man stood in his usual corner. This did not surprise him much, however, because he was planning a change of life, and he hoped that his absence showed that his new plans had been carried out.

The Phantom took him next to an obscure part of the town that Scrooge had never visited before. The streets were dirty and narrow, the shops and houses miserable, and the people half naked, drunk and ugly. In a low shop, where iron, old rags, bottles and bones were bought, sat a grey-haired thief of nearly seventy named Old Joe.

Three people came into the shop, one after another, each carrying a heavy bundle. The first was a woman who had cleaned the dead man's house. The second was a woman who had washed his clothes. The third was a man in faded black, an undertaker's assistant. They looked at each other, and then they burst out laughing.

"Every person has a right to take care of themselves," said the cleaning woman. "He always did! If he wanted to keep his things after he was dead, the wicked old miser, why wasn't he natural in his lifetime? If he had been, he'd have had somebody to look after him when he was struck with Death, instead of lying gasping out his last there, alone by himself."

The undertaker's man showed his goods first: a pencil case, two buttons and a cheap brooch. The washerwoman had sheets, towels, two old silver teaspoons and some boots. Old Joe wrote the prices on the wall with a piece of chalk. Then the cleaning woman opened her large bundle and dragged out a heavy roll of dark material.

"What do you call this?" said Joe. "Bed curtains!"

"Ah!" she laughed. "Bed curtains, and his blankets too, and even the shirt he was going to be buried in! It's the best he had, and a fine one too. They'd have wasted it, if it hadn't been for me."

Scrooge listened to this dialogue in horror. "Spirit!" he said, shaking from head to foot. "I see, I see, the case of this unhappy man might be my own. My life tends that way now. Merciful Heaven, what is this?"

The scene had changed, and he almost touched a bed: a bare bed, without curtains. On it, beneath a ragged sheet, there lay something covered up, which spoke for itself in a terrible language. The room was very dark. A pale light fell straight upon the bed, and on it, robbed and deserted, unwatched, unwept and uncared for, was the body of this man.

The Phantom pointed to the head. The cover was so carelessly arranged that the slightest movement of a finger would have shown the face. Scrooge thought how easy it would be to do it, and he longed to do it. But he had no more power to pull back the cover than to send away the Spirit at his side.

He heard no voice, but he felt the silent room speaking to him. The miserable man lay in the dark, empty house alone. There was not a man, a woman or a child to say that he had been kind to them, and that they would be kind to him for the memory of one kind word. A cat was tearing at the door, and there was a sound of rats under the floor.

"Spirit," said Scrooge, "this is a fearful place. In leaving it, I shall not leave its lesson, trust me. Let us go! If there is any person in the town who feels emotion caused by this man's death, show that person to me, Spirit, I beg you!"

The Phantom spread its dark robe before him for a moment like a wing. When it withdrew it, Scrooge saw a room by daylight, where a young mother and her children were waiting anxiously. She walked up and down, started at every sound and looked out of the window. At last her husband came in. His face was worn with care, though he was young.

"Is it good news," she asked, "or bad?"

"Bad," he answered.

"We are quite ruined?"

"No, there is hope yet, Caroline, because he is dead."

She was a mild and patient creature, and she was ashamed of the feeling, but she was thankful in her soul to hear it, and said so with clasped hands.

"To whom will our debt be transferred?" she asked.

"I don't know. But before that time we shall be ready with the money. And even if we are not, it would be bad luck indeed to find so merciless a creditor in his successor. We may sleep tonight with light hearts, Caroline!"

Yes, their hearts were lighter. The children's faces were brighter, and it was a happier house for this man's death. The only emotion that the Ghost could show him, caused by the event, was one of pleasure.

"Let me see some tenderness connected with a death," said Scrooge, "or that dark room, Spirit, will be for ever present to me."

Chapter 9: The Empty Chair and the Grave

The Ghost led him through several streets familiar to his feet, and they entered poor Bob Cratchit's house. The mother and the children were seated round the fire. They were very quiet. The noisy little Cratchits were as still as statues in a corner, and they sat looking up at Peter, who had a book before him. The mother and her daughters were sewing.

The mother laid her work down on the table and put her hand up to her face.

"The colour hurts my eyes," she said. "It makes them weak by candlelight, and I wouldn't show weak eyes to your father when he comes home, for anything in the world. He must be arriving soon."

"Past it, rather," Peter answered, shutting up his book. "But I think he has walked a little slower than he used to, these last few evenings, Mother."

They were very quiet again. At last she said, in a steady, cheerful voice that only faltered once, "I have known him walk with Tiny Tim upon his shoulder very fast indeed. But he was very light to carry, and his father loved him so, that it was no trouble."

She hurried out to meet her husband, and little Bob in his scarf came in. His tea was ready for him, and they all competed to see who could help him most. Then the two young Cratchits got upon his knees, and each child laid a little cheek against his face, as if they said, "Don't mind it, Father. Don't be sad!"

Bob was very cheerful with them, and he spoke pleasantly to all the family. He looked at the work upon the table and praised how quickly Mrs Cratchit and the girls were working. It would be done long before Sunday, he said.

"Sunday! You went today, then, Robert?" said his wife.

"Yes, my dear," returned Bob. "I wish you could have gone, because it would have done you good to see how green a place it is. But you'll see it often, since I promised him that I would walk there on a Sunday. My little, little child!" cried Bob. "My little child!"

He broke down all at once, because he couldn't help it.

After a while, he told them about the extraordinary kindness of Mr Scrooge's nephew, whom he had hardly seen more than once. Meeting Bob in the street that day, and seeing that he looked a little sad, Fred had asked what was the matter. When Bob told him, he had said, "I am heartily sorry for it, Mr Cratchit, and heartily sorry for your good wife." He had given Bob his card and offered to help the family in any way he could.

"And I know," said Bob, "that when we remember how patient and how mild he was, although he was a little, little child, we shall not quarrel easily among ourselves."

"Never, Father!" they all cried.

"I am very happy," said little Bob. "I am very happy!"

Mrs Cratchit kissed him, his daughters kissed him, the two young Cratchits kissed him, and Peter and himself shook hands. Spirit of Tiny Tim, your childish goodness came from God!

"Spectre," said Scrooge, "something tells me that our parting moment is near. I know it, but I cannot explain how. Tell me, what man was that whom we saw lying dead?"

The Ghost of Christmas Yet to Come carried him on, through the busy city, past his own office, where another man now sat. At last they reached an iron gate. It was a churchyard. Here, then, under the ground, lay the wretched man whose name he had now to learn. It was an unpleasant place, walled in by houses and overrun by grass and weeds.

The Spirit stood among the graves and pointed down to one. Scrooge moved towards it, trembling.

"Before I draw nearer to that stone," said Scrooge, "answer me one question. Are these the shadows of the things that will be, or are they shadows of things that may be, only?"

Still the Ghost pointed downward to the grave by which it stood.

"Men's lives lead to certain ends, if they continue along the same path," said Scrooge, "but if they depart from it, the ends will be different. Tell me it is so with these visions you have shown me!"

The Spirit was as still as ever.

Scrooge crept towards it, trembling as he went. Following the finger, he read upon the stone of the neglected grave his own name: EBENEZER SCROOGE.

"Am I that man who lay upon the bed?" he cried, upon his knees.

The finger pointed from the grave to him, and back again.

"No, Spirit! Oh no, no!"

The finger still was there.

"Spirit!" he cried, holding tight to its robe. "Hear me! I am not the man I was. I will not be the man I must have been without your visits. Why show me this, if I am beyond all hope? Assure me that I may yet change these shadows you have shown me, by an altered life!"

For the first time, the kind hand appeared to shake.

"I will honour Christmas in my heart, and try to keep it all the year. I will live in the Past, the Present and the Future. The Spirits of all three shall live within me. I will not shut out the lessons that they teach. Oh, tell me I may wipe away the writing on this stone!"

In his agony, he caught the spectral hand. It tried to free itself, but he was strong in his request and held it. The Spirit, stronger still, pushed him away.

Holding up his hands in a last prayer to have his fate reversed, he saw a change in the Phantom's hood and dress. It shrank, collapsed and became smaller, until it was nothing but a bedpost.

Chapter 10: Christmas Morning

Yes! The bedpost was his own, and so were the bed and the room. Best and happiest of all, the time before him was his own, to make amends in!

"I will live in the Past, the Present and the Future!" Scrooge repeated, as he jumped out of bed. "Oh, Jacob Marley! Heaven and the Christmas time be praised for this! I say it on my knees, old Jacob, on my knees!"

His face was wet with tears, and he held the bed curtains in his arms. "They are not torn down," cried Scrooge, "rings and all! They are here, and I am here. The shadows of the things that would have happened can be dispelled, and I know they will be!"

He was so excited that he could not dress himself properly, and he put his stockings on inside out, laughing and crying at the same time.

"I don't know what to do!" cried Scrooge. "I am as light as a feather, as happy as an angel and as merry as a schoolboy! A merry Christmas to everybody, and a happy New Year to all the world! Whoop! Hallo!"

He danced into the sitting room and stood there, completely out of breath. "There's the door by which the Ghost of Jacob Marley entered! There's the corner where the Ghost of Christmas Present sat! It's all right, it's all true, it all happened. Ha, ha, ha!"

Really, for a man who had been out of practice for so many years, it was a splendid laugh. It was the father of a long, long line of brilliant laughs!

Just then, the church bells rang out the loudest peals he had ever heard. Clash, clang, hammer, ding, dong, bell! Oh, glorious, glorious!

He ran to the window, opened it and put out his head, and discovered that there was no fog. It was a clear, bright, jolly, cold day, with golden sunlight, fresh air and merry bells.

"What's today?" cried Scrooge, calling down to a boy in Sunday clothes.

"Today?" replied the boy, astonished. "Why, it's Christmas Day!"

"It's Christmas Day!" said Scrooge to himself. "I haven't missed it, because the Spirits have done it all in one night! Hallo, my fine fellow! Do you know the poulterer's shop in the next street but one, at the corner?"

"I should hope I did," replied the boy.

"An intelligent boy!" said Scrooge, "a remarkable boy! Do you know whether they've sold the prize turkey that was hanging up there? Not the little prize turkey: the big one?"

"What, the one as big as me?" asked the boy.

"It's hanging there now," replied the boy.

"What a delightful boy! Then go and buy it," said Scrooge, "and tell them to bring it here, so that I can tell them where to take it. Come back with the man, and I'll give you a shilling. Come back in less than five minutes, and I'll give you half a crown!"

The boy was off like a shot.

"I'll send it to Bob Cratchit's!" whispered Scrooge, rubbing his hands and laughing. "He won't know who sent it, and it's twice the size of Tiny Tim!"

When the turkey arrived, it was so enormous that it could never have stood upon its legs, so Scrooge paid for a cab to carry it to Camden Town. He paid the poulterer, he paid for the cab, and he paid the boy, and he laughed until he cried.

Then he dressed himself all in his best and went out into the streets. The people were pouring out, as he had seen them with the Ghost of Christmas Present. Walking with his hands behind him, Scrooge looked at everyone with a delighted smile. He looked so irresistibly pleasant that three or four good-humoured fellows said, "Good morning, sir! A merry Christmas to you!" Scrooge said often afterwards that, of all the happy sounds he had ever heard, those were the happiest in his ears.

He had not gone far when he saw one of the gentlemen who had come to his office the day before to collect money for the poor.

"My dear sir," said Scrooge, taking the old gentleman by both his hands. "I hope you were successful yesterday, and a merry Christmas to you, sir!"

"Mr Scrooge?"

"Yes," said Scrooge. "That is my name, and I fear it may not be pleasant to you. Allow me to apologise. And will you have the goodness..." Here Scrooge whispered in his ear.

"Lord bless me!" cried the gentleman, as if his breath had been taken away. "My dear Mr Scrooge, are you serious?"

"If you please," said Scrooge, "and not a penny less. A great many back payments are included in it, I assure you. Will you come and see me?"

"I will!" cried the old gentleman. And it was clear that he meant to do it.

Scrooge went to church, and walked about the streets. He watched the people hurrying here and there, patted children on the head, questioned beggars and looked down into the kitchens of houses. He had never dreamed that any walk, that anything, could give him so much happiness.

In the afternoon, he turned his steps towards his nephew's house. He passed the door a dozen times before he had the courage to go up and knock. But he made a dash, and did it.

"Is your master at home, my dear?" Scrooge asked the girl who opened it.

He turned the handle gently and put his face round the door. Fred and his wife were looking at the table, which was laid for dinner.

"Fred!" said Scrooge.

Dear heart alive, how his niece by marriage jumped!

"Why, bless my soul!" cried Fred. "Who's that?"

"It's I, your Uncle Scrooge, and I have come to dinner. Will you let me in, Fred?"

Let him in! It is lucky that Fred didn't shake his arm off. Scrooge felt at home in five minutes, and nothing could be warmer. His niece looked just as she had looked in the Spirit's vision, and so did the plump sister, and so did everyone when they came. Wonderful party, wonderful games, wonderful happiness!

Chapter 11: Scrooge Keeps His Promise

But he was early at the office next morning. Oh, he was early there! If he could only be there first, and catch Bob Cratchit coming late! That was the thing he had set his heart upon.

And he did it, yes, he did! The clock struck nine, and there was no Bob. A quarter past, and still no Bob. He was a full eighteen and a half minutes late. Scrooge sat with his door wide open, so that he might see him come in.

Bob's hat was off before he opened the door, and his scarf too. He was on his stool in a moment, writing away with his pen as if he was trying to catch up with nine o'clock.

"Hallo!" growled Scrooge, in his usual voice, as near as he could make it. "What do you mean by coming here at this time of day? Step this way, sir, if you please."

"It's only once a year, sir," pleaded Bob, appearing from the cupboard. "It shall not be repeated. I was making rather merry yesterday, sir."

"Now, I'll tell you what, my friend," said Scrooge. "I am not going to stand this sort of thing any longer. And therefore," he continued, jumping from his stool and giving Bob such a dig in the ribs that he staggered back into the cupboard again, "and therefore I am about to raise your salary!"

Bob trembled and moved a little nearer to the ruler. For a moment he thought of knocking Scrooge down with it, holding him, and calling to the people in the street for help and a straitjacket.

"A merry Christmas, Bob!" said Scrooge, with a seriousness that could not be mistaken, as he patted him on the back. "A merrier Christmas, Bob, my good fellow, than I have given you for many a year! I'll raise your salary and try to help your struggling family. We will discuss your affairs this very afternoon, over a bowl of hot Christmas punch, Bob! Make up the fires, and buy another coal box before you put another dot on an i, Bob Cratchit!"

Scrooge was better than his word, and he did it all, and infinitely more. To Tiny Tim, who did not die, he was a second father. He became as good a friend, as good a master and as good a man as the good old city knew, or any other good old city, town or village in the good old world.

Some people laughed to see the change in him, but he let them laugh, and little cared. He was wise enough to know that nothing good ever happened on this globe at which some people did not have their fill of laughter at the beginning. His own heart laughed, and that was quite enough for him.

He had no further contact with Spirits, but lived happily ever afterwards. It was always said of him that he knew how to keep Christmas well, if any man alive possessed that knowledge. May that be truly said of us, and all of us! And so, as Tiny Tim observed, God bless us, every one!

The End
