Chapter 1: Stolen from the South

Buck did not read the newspapers. If he had read them, he would have known that trouble was coming. It was coming for every strong dog with warm, long hair, from the coast of Puget Sound to San Diego. Men had found gold in the far north, in a cold land called the Klondike. Thousands of men were rushing there, and they all wanted dogs. They wanted big, heavy dogs with strong muscles and thick fur to protect them from the frost.

Buck lived in a big house in the sunny Santa Clara Valley in California. It was called Judge Miller's place. The house stood back from the road, half hidden among the trees. Around it there were wide green lawns, stables, long rows of vines and a swimming pool. It was a beautiful place, and Buck ruled over all of it.

He had been born there, and he had lived there for the four years of his life.

But Buck was neither a house dog nor a yard dog. The whole place was his. He swam in the pool with the Judge's sons and went hunting with them. He walked with the Judge's daughters in the cool early morning. On winter nights he lay at the Judge's feet in front of the fire. He carried the Judge's grandsons on his back and rolled them in the grass. He watched over them when they ran to the fountain in the stable yard.

His father, Elvis, had been a huge St Bernard and the Judge's best friend. Buck hoped to follow in his father's footsteps. He was not as big as his father, because his mother, Shep, had been a Scotch shepherd dog. Still, he weighed one hundred and forty pounds. Good food and the respect of everyone around him had made him proud, like a country gentleman. Hunting and swimming had kept him from growing fat, and his muscles were hard.

That was Buck in the autumn of 1897, when the Klondike gold rush began. Buck did not know about it. And he did not know that Manuel, one of the gardener's helpers, was a dangerous man to trust.

Manuel had one bad habit. He loved to play the Chinese lottery. But a gardener's helper earns very little, and Manuel had a wife and many children to feed.

One evening the Judge was at a meeting, and his sons were busy with a sports club. Nobody saw Manuel and Buck leave together through the orchard. Buck thought they were only going for a walk. They walked to a little railway station called College Park. Only one man was waiting there. He talked with Manuel for a while, and money passed between their hands.

"You might wrap up the goods before you deliver them," the stranger said roughly.

Manuel put a thick rope around Buck's neck, under his collar.

"Twist it, and you'll choke him plenty," said Manuel, and the stranger made a sound that meant yes.

Buck accepted the rope quietly. It was a new thing, but he had learned to trust the men he knew. He believed that they were wiser than he was. But when Manuel gave the end of the rope to the stranger, Buck growled. He only meant to show that he was not happy. In his pride, he believed that a warning was enough. To his surprise, the rope tightened around his neck and stopped his breath.

Buck jumped at the man in a sudden fury, but the man caught him by the throat and threw him onto his back. Then the rope tightened even more. Buck fought wildly. His tongue hung out of his mouth and his great chest struggled for air. Never in all his life had anyone treated him so badly. Never had he been so angry. Then his strength left him, his eyes closed, and he knew nothing more.

When he woke up, he felt pain in his throat and tongue. He was lying in a small dark space that was shaking and moving. He heard the cry of a train whistle at a crossing, and he understood where he was. He had often travelled with the Judge, and he knew the feeling of a baggage car.

He opened his eyes, which were full of the anger of a kidnapped king. The man tried to grab him by the throat, but Buck was too quick for him. His jaws closed on the man's hand, and they did not open again until Buck lost his senses once more.

"Yes, he has fits," the man told the baggage man, who had come to see what the noise was. He hid his bleeding hand. "I'm taking him to San Francisco for the boss. There's a dog doctor there who thinks he can cure him."

That night, in a small room behind a bar on the San Francisco waterfront, the man complained loudly about the trip.

"All I get is fifty dollars for it," he said. "I wouldn't do it again for a thousand in cash."

His hand was wrapped in a bloody cloth, and his right trouser leg was torn from knee to ankle.

Buck was half dead with pain, but he tried to face the men who were torturing him. Again and again they threw him down and choked him. At last they filed the heavy brass collar from his neck. Then they took off the rope and pushed him into a wooden box like a cage.

He lay there for the rest of the long night, full of anger and wounded pride. He could not understand what it all meant. What did these strange men want with him? Why were they keeping him in this narrow box? Several times during the night he jumped to his feet when the door opened. He hoped to see the Judge, or at least the boys. But each time it was the fat face of the bar owner, who looked in at him by the weak light of a candle. And each time the happy bark in Buck's throat changed into a wild growl.

Chapter 2: The Man in the Red Sweater

In the morning four men came and picked up the box. They were rough, dirty men in torn clothes, and Buck immediately decided that they were enemies. He growled and attacked them through the wooden bars. The men only laughed and poked sticks at him, so Buck lay down quietly and waited.

For two days and two nights the box travelled across the country by train. During all that time Buck did not eat or drink. He was so angry that he refused to accept anything from anyone. The men on the train laughed at him, barked like dogs and flapped their arms like birds. His throat and tongue were dry and swollen, and his eyes turned red with anger. He had changed into a raging monster that even the Judge would not have recognised.

At last the box was carried into a small yard with high walls in Seattle. A stout man in a red sweater signed the driver's book and took the box. Buck guessed that this man was his next torturer, and he threw himself savagely against the bars. The man smiled darkly and brought a small axe and a heavy wooden club.

The man began to break open the box with the axe. The four men who had carried it climbed quickly onto the high wall to watch the show from a safe place.

"Now, you red-eyed devil," the man said, when he had made a hole big enough for Buck's body. At the same time he dropped the axe and took the club in his right hand.

Buck really looked like a red-eyed devil as he prepared to jump. His hair stood up, his mouth was full of foam, and a mad light shone in his eyes. He launched his hundred and forty pounds of fury straight at the man. In mid-air, just as his jaws were about to close, he received a terrible blow. His teeth crashed together, his body turned over, and he fell to the ground on his back.

He had never been hit with a club in his life, and he did not understand. With a cry that was half bark and half scream, he got up and jumped again. Again the club smashed him to the ground. This time he knew it was the club, but his madness did not let him stop. A dozen times he attacked, and a dozen times the club broke the attack.

At last he stood there, unsteady and confused, with blood running from his nose, mouth and ears. Then the man came forward and calmly hit him on the nose. All the pain that he had felt before was nothing compared with this. With a roar like a lion, Buck threw himself at the man once more. But the man caught him under the jaw, swung him round in the air and brought him down head first onto the ground. Then the man gave the final blow, and Buck fell, completely unconscious.

When Buck opened his eyes, he lay where he had fallen and watched the man in the red sweater.

"Answers to the name of Buck," the man said, reading the bar owner's letter. "Well, Buck, my boy, we've had our little fight. The best thing now is to forget it. You've learned your place, and I know mine. Be a good dog and all will go well. Be a bad dog, and I'll beat the life out of you. Understand?"

As he spoke, he gently patted the head that he had beaten so mercilessly. Buck's hair stood up at his touch, but he did not resist. When the man brought him water, he drank eagerly. Later he ate a generous meal of raw meat, piece by piece, from the man's hand.

He was beaten, he knew that, but he was not broken. He had learned a lesson that he would never forget: a man with a club was a master who must be obeyed. It was his introduction to the law of the club. Life had shown him a harder side, and he faced it with all the cunning that was slowly waking up inside him.

One day a thin little man arrived who spoke English with a strong French accent.

"Sacredam!" he cried, when his eyes fell on Buck. "That is one fine dog! How much?"

"Three hundred, and it's a present at that price," replied the man in the red sweater. "It's government money, Perrault, so you can't complain."

Perrault smiled. He carried letters and official papers for the Canadian government, and he needed the best dogs to carry them fast. He knew dogs well, and he recognised that Buck was one in ten thousand.

A little later Buck and Curly, a friendly Newfoundland, were led away by the little man. From the deck of the ship Narwhal, Buck watched the city of Seattle disappear behind him. It was the last time he saw the warm South.

On the ship Perrault gave the two dogs to a dark-faced giant called François. Both men were French-Canadians, and both were a new kind of men for Buck. He did not love them, but he soon respected them. They were fair and calm in giving justice, and they knew dogs too well to be fooled by them.

There were two other dogs on the ship. One was a big, snow-white dog from Spitsbergen called Spitz. He was friendly in a dishonest way: he smiled into your face while he planned some trick. At the first meal he stole Buck's food. Before Buck could punish him, François's whip flew through the air and hit the thief first. Buck decided that François was fair, and his respect grew.

The other dog was Dave, a gloomy animal who only wanted to be left alone. He ate, slept, yawned and did not care about anything.

Day after day the ship went north, and every day the weather grew colder. At last, one morning, the engines stopped, and the ship was full of excitement. François tied the dogs and led them onto the deck. At the first step onto the cold surface, Buck's feet sank into a white soft thing very like mud. He jumped back with a snort. More of this white stuff was falling through the air. He shook himself, but more fell on him. He smelled it curiously, then licked some up with his tongue. It bit like fire, and the next moment it was gone. He tried again, with the same result. The men watching him laughed loudly, and he felt ashamed without knowing why. It was his first snow.

Chapter 3: The Law of Club and Fang

Buck's first day on the beach at Dyea was like a nightmare. He had been pulled out of the heart of civilisation and thrown into the heart of something wild and ancient. Here there was no peace, no rest and not a moment of safety. These dogs and men were not town dogs and town men. They were savages, and they knew only the law of club and fang.

He had never seen dogs fight like these wolfish creatures, and his first experience taught him a lesson he never forgot. Curly, in her friendly way, went up to a husky dog the size of a large wolf. There was no warning. The husky jumped in like a flash, his teeth closed with a metallic sound, and he jumped away again. Curly's face was torn open from eye to jaw.

That was the wolf's way of fighting: strike and jump away. But there was more to it than that. Thirty or forty huskies ran to the spot and made a silent, eager circle around the two fighters. Curly rushed at her enemy, and he struck again and jumped away. When she rushed a second time, he met her with his chest in a strange way and knocked her off her feet. She never got up again. This was what the silent huskies had been waiting for. They closed in on her, snarling and yelping, and she disappeared under a mass of bodies.

It was so sudden and so unexpected that Buck was shocked. He saw Spitz run out his red tongue in a kind of laugh. Then he saw François swing an axe into the crowd of dogs. Three men with clubs helped him to drive them away. Two minutes after Curly had fallen, the last of her attackers had been chased off. But she lay there in the bloody, trampled snow, dead, and the dark-faced François stood over her, swearing horribly.

The scene often came back to Buck to trouble him in his sleep. So that was the way things were: there was no fair play, and once you were down, that was the end of you. Well, he would make sure that he never went down. Spitz laughed again, and from that moment Buck hated him with a deep and bitter hatred.

Before he had recovered from the shock of Curly's death, he received another shock. François fastened him into a harness of straps and buckles. It was like the harnesses he had seen on horses at home. And like the horses, he was made to work. He had to pull François on a sled to the forest at the edge of the valley and return with a load of firewood.

His pride was badly hurt, but he was too wise to rebel. He decided to do his best, even though everything was new and strange. François was strict. He demanded instant obedience, and his whip made sure that he got it. Dave, who was an experienced wheel dog, bit Buck's back legs whenever he made a mistake. Spitz was the leader, and he growled sharp warnings or threw his weight against the traces to push Buck into the right path. Buck learned quickly. Before they returned to camp, he knew enough to stop at "Ho" and to go ahead at "Mush". He also knew how to swing wide on the bends and keep clear of the wheel dog when the loaded sled ran downhill.

"Three very good dogs," François told Perrault. "That Buck, he pulls like the devil. I teach him quick as anything."

By the afternoon Perrault, who was in a hurry to be on the trail, returned with two more dogs. Their names were Billee and Joe. They were brothers, and true huskies both, but they were as different as day and night. Billee was too good-natured. Joe was sour and always snarling. Then came Sol-leks, an old husky with only one eye. He had a long, thin face and many battle scars. Like Dave, he asked for nothing and gave nothing. He did not like anyone to come near him on his blind side.

That night Buck faced a great problem: where should he sleep? The tent was lit by a candle and looked warm and inviting. But when he entered, Perrault and François shouted at him and threw cooking pots at him. He ran out into the cold again. A bitter wind was blowing, and it bit his wounded shoulder. He lay down on the snow and tried to sleep, but the frost soon drove him up again, shivering.

He wandered sadly through the camp, looking for the other dogs, but to his surprise they had all disappeared. Suddenly the snow gave way under his front legs, and he sank down. Something moved under his feet. He jumped back, growling and afraid. But a friendly little yelp calmed him, and he went back to look. A warm smell rose to his nose. There, curled up under the snow in a snug ball, lay Billee. He whined and wagged his tail to show that he meant no harm.

So that was how they did it. Buck chose a spot, and with much fuss and wasted effort he dug a hole for himself. In a moment the heat from his body filled the small space, and he was asleep. The day had been long and difficult, and he slept soundly, although he growled and fought in his bad dreams.

He did not open his eyes until the noises of the waking camp woke him. At first he did not know where he was. It had snowed during the night, and he was completely buried. The snow walls pressed on him from every side, and a great fear rose through him. It was the fear of the wild animal for the trap. With a ferocious snarl, he burst straight up into the blinding daylight. The snow flew around him in a cloud. Then he saw the white camp spread out before him, and he remembered everything.

Chapter 4: A Fight in the Night

That first day they climbed up the Dyea Canyon and towards the frozen lakes beyond it. Day after day the team travelled across mountains and through dark forests, past great glaciers and snowdrifts hundreds of feet deep. Perrault was always in a hurry, and he was proud of his knowledge of ice.

Buck worked in the traces from dark morning to dark night. They usually made camp after dark, ate their bit of fish and crawled to sleep in the snow. Buck was always terribly hungry. His ration was a pound and a half of dried salmon a day, and it seemed to disappear inside him. The other dogs received only a pound, but they had been born to this life, and they managed to stay in good condition.

Buck soon lost the good manners of his old life. He was a dainty eater, and he found that the other dogs finished first and then stole his unfinished ration. So he learned to eat as fast as they did. Then, one day, he saw Pike, a new dog, steal a slice of bacon when Perrault's back was turned. The next day Buck did the same thing and got away with the whole piece. There was great anger in camp, but nobody suspected Buck. Instead, Dub, a clumsy dog who was always getting caught, was punished for it.

This first theft showed that Buck could survive in the hostile Northland. In the South, under the law of love, it was right to respect property and other people's feelings. But in the Northland, under the law of club and fang, a dog who followed such rules was a fool. Buck did not reason all this out. He was simply fit, and without thinking he changed to the new life.

His muscles became as hard as iron, and he grew used to all ordinary pain. He could eat anything, however disgusting it was. His sight and smell became remarkably keen, and his hearing grew so sharp that even in his sleep he heard the faintest sound. He learned to bite the ice out of his toes with his teeth.

And he learned more than that. Old instincts that had been sleeping for many generations came alive again. In a strange way he remembered the wild dogs of long ago, running through the ancient forests and killing their meat as they ran down. On cold, still nights he pointed his nose at a star and howled long and wolf-like. Then it was his ancestors, dead and dust, who were howling through him.

But the dominant primitive beast was strong in Buck, and it grew stronger under the hard conditions of trail life. He did not look for a fight with Spitz. He was too busy adapting to his new life. Spitz, however, saw that Buck was a dangerous rival. He never missed a chance to show his teeth, and he tried to start the fight that could only end in the death of one of them.

It almost happened early in the trip, at Lake Laberge. That night the team made camp on the shore in a terrible snowstorm. Buck had made a warm nest close to a rock and was unwilling to leave it when François gave out the fish. When he had finished eating and returned to his nest, he found Spitz lying in it. Until then Buck had avoided trouble, but this was too much. The beast inside him roared. He sprang upon Spitz with a fury that surprised them both.

"Ah-ah!" François shouted to Buck. "Give it to him, by God! Give it to him, the dirty thief!"

Spitz was equally willing. The two dogs circled, looking for the best moment to attack. But at that moment something unexpected happened, and their struggle for leadership was postponed.

Suddenly Perrault shouted a curse, and they heard the sound of a club striking a bony body. A cry of pain followed. The camp was suddenly full of creeping, furry forms. There were eighty or a hundred starving huskies from some native village. They had smelled the camp from far away. They were so thin that their bones almost broke through their skin, and their eyes blazed with hunger. The two men attacked them with clubs, but the animals did not care. They were crazy with the smell of food. One had found the box of supplies, and they fought over the bread and the bacon even while the clubs fell on them.

The team dogs jumped out of their snow nests, only to be attacked by the fierce invaders. Buck had never seen dogs like these. He was attacked by three huskies at once, and in a moment his head and shoulders were cut and bleeding. Buck caught a husky by the throat and tasted warm blood in his mouth, and it made him even fiercer. At the same moment he felt teeth in his own throat. It was Spitz, attacking treacherously from the side.

Billee, terrified into courage, sprang through the circle of attackers and ran away over the ice. Pike and Dub followed him, and then the rest of the team. As Buck gathered himself to follow, he saw Spitz rush at him, trying to knock him over. Once off his feet, under that mass of huskies, there would be no hope for him. But Buck held his ground against Spitz's charge and then joined the flight out on the lake.

At daybreak the dogs returned carefully to the camp. The attackers had gone, and half the food supply had gone with them. The huskies had even eaten a pair of Perrault's moose-skin boots and two feet of François's whip.

"Ah, my friends," François said softly to the dogs. "Maybe those bites make you go mad. Maybe all of you go mad, by God! What do you think, Perrault?"

Perrault shook his head doubtfully. There were still four hundred miles of trail between him and Dawson, and he could not afford to have madness among his dogs.

Chapter 5: Who Will Be the Leader?

The trail to Dawson was long and dangerous. One morning, at Pelly, Dolly, a new dog who had been badly bitten by the wild huskies, suddenly went mad. She gave a long, terrible wolf howl that made every dog's hair stand up with fear. Then she jumped straight at Buck. He had never seen a mad dog, but he understood the danger and ran away in panic. Dolly followed him, foaming at the mouth, only one jump behind. Buck ran through the woods, crossed the frozen river and came back to the camp. Behind him he heard François call, and he turned towards the voice. As Buck passed him, the man lifted the axe, and it crashed down on mad Dolly's head.

Buck fell against the sled, too exhausted to move. This was Spitz's chance. He jumped on Buck and twice sank his teeth deep into his helpless enemy. Then François's whip came down, and Buck had the satisfaction of watching Spitz receive the worst whipping of his life.

"One devil, that Spitz," Perrault said. "One day he kills that Buck."

"That Buck is two devils," François answered. "All the time I watch him, and I know for sure. One fine day he gets very mad and chews that Spitz all up and spits him out on the snow. Sure. I know."

From then on it was war between them. Spitz, as leader of the team, felt that his position was threatened by this strange Southland dog. Buck wanted to be leader too. He wanted it because it was his nature, and because he had been gripped by that strange pride of the trail. It was the pride that made dogs work until their last breath and broke their hearts if they were taken out of the harness.

Buck openly began to challenge Spitz's authority. He came between Spitz and the dogs who should have been punished. One night there was a heavy snowfall, and Pike, who was lazy, stayed hidden in his nest under a foot of snow. When Spitz finally found him and rushed to punish him, Buck jumped between them. François, laughing, punished Buck with the whip, and Spitz punished the guilty Pike. But after that the other dogs became less and less obedient, and the team never again worked in harmony.

But the opportunity did not come until they reached Dawson and left it again. One night, after supper, Dub found a snowshoe rabbit near the camp. He tried to catch it and missed. In a second the whole team was after the rabbit, with Buck at the front. There were fifty huskies from a nearby police camp, and they joined the hunt too. The rabbit raced down the frozen river, and the dogs followed. The ancient instinct to hunt had been awakened in Buck, and he ran under the pale moonlight, crying with joy.

But Spitz, cold and calculating, left the pack and cut across a narrow piece of land where the river curved. When the rabbit came round the bend, Spitz landed on it from the bank. Buck heard the rabbit scream like a human being. At that sound the pack behind Buck howled with delight.

Buck did not howl, and he did not stop either. He drove straight into Spitz, shoulder to shoulder, so hard that he missed the throat. They rolled over and over in the powdery snow. Spitz got up almost as though he had not been knocked down, cut Buck's shoulder and jumped clear. Buck knew it at once. The time had come. It was to the death.

They circled around, snarling, with their ears laid back and their eyes watching for an advantage. The scene seemed strangely familiar to Buck: the white woods, the cold moonlight and the excitement of battle. The other dogs had finished the rabbit, and now they waited in a silent circle with shining eyes. Their breath rose slowly in the frosty air. To them it was not a new or strange scene. It was the way things had always been.

Spitz was an experienced fighter. He had fought all kinds of dogs and won. He never attacked in anger, and he never forgot that his enemy wanted the same thing as he did. Again and again Buck tried to reach the white dog's throat, but each time his teeth met Spitz's teeth. Then Buck tried to knock Spitz down with his shoulder, but each time his own shoulder was cut by Spitz's teeth.

The fight became desperate for Buck, who was bleeding badly and breathing hard. The silent circle of wolfish dogs waited to finish whichever dog fell first. Once Buck went over, and the circle moved forward. But he got up again almost in the air, and the circle sank back.

But Buck possessed a quality that made him great: imagination. He fought by instinct, but he could also fight with his head. He rushed as though he wanted to use his shoulder trick again. At the last moment he dropped low to the snow, and his teeth closed on Spitz's left front leg. There was a crunch of breaking bone. Three times Buck tried to knock him over, and then he repeated the trick and broke the right front leg.

Spitz struggled madly to keep standing, but it was hopeless. He saw the silent circle closing in, as he had seen similar circles close in on beaten enemies in the past. Only this time he was the one who was beaten.

There was no hope for him, because Buck was without mercy, and mercy was a thing for gentler places. He rushed in for the final time. The dark circle became a single spot on the moonlit snow as Spitz disappeared from sight. Buck stood and looked on. He was the successful champion, the dominant primitive beast who had made his kill and found it good.

Chapter 6: The New Leader

"What did I say? I spoke true when I said Buck is two devils."

François said this the next morning, when he discovered that Spitz was missing and Buck was covered with wounds. He took Buck to the fire and showed them to Perrault.

"That Spitz fought like hell," said Perrault, as he examined the cuts.

"And that Buck fought like two hells," was François's answer. "And now we make good time. No more Spitz, no more trouble, sure."

While Perrault packed the camp equipment and loaded the sled, François prepared to harness the dogs. Buck walked straight to the place where Spitz used to stand as leader. But François did not notice him, and he brought Sol-leks to the position. In his opinion, Sol-leks was the best lead dog left. Buck jumped on Sol-leks in a fury, drove him back and stood in his place.

"Look at him!" cried François, slapping his thighs with amusement. "Look at that Buck. He killed that Spitz, and now he thinks he takes the job."

He dragged Buck away by the neck and put Sol-leks back. The old dog did not like it at all, and he showed clearly that he was afraid of Buck. But François was stubborn, and when he turned his back, Buck again pushed Sol-leks out. This time François came back with a heavy club in his hand.

Buck remembered the man in the red sweater, and he moved away slowly. He did not attack when Sol-leks was brought forward again. But he circled just out of reach of the club, snarling with bitterness and anger. He was not trying to get away. He was simply waiting for the leadership that was his by right. He had earned it, and he would not be content with less.

For an hour the men chased him round the camp, throwing clubs at him. At last Perrault looked at his watch and swore. They should have been on the trail an hour before. François scratched his head, grinned at Perrault, and Perrault shrugged his shoulders. They had been defeated. François went up to where Sol-leks stood and called to Buck. Buck laughed as dogs laugh, but he still kept his distance. Only when Sol-leks had been taken out and returned to his old place did Buck trot in. Laughing in triumph, he swung round into position at the head of the team.

"By God!" François said, as the team dashed out onto the river trail. "I never see such a dog as that Buck! He's worth one thousand dollars, by God! Eh? What do you say, Perrault?"

Buck had quickly taken up the duties of leadership. When judgement was required, and quick thinking and quick action, he showed himself even superior to Spitz. He made the lazy Pike pull harder than he had ever pulled in his life. On the first night in camp, he punished the sour Joe so thoroughly that Joe stopped snapping and began to whine for mercy. The whole team found its old unity again. At the Rink Rapids two new huskies, Teek and Koona, joined them, and the speed with which Buck trained them amazed François.

That season they made a record run. They reached Skaguay in fourteen days, averaging forty miles a day.

Then official orders came, and François called Buck to him, threw his arms around him and wept over him. That was the last of François and Perrault. Like other men before them, they passed out of Buck's life forever.

A Scotsman took charge of Buck and his companions. Together with a dozen other dog teams, they started back over the long trail to Dawson. It was no light running now, and there were no records to beat. It was heavy work each day, with a heavy load behind them. This was the mail train, carrying letters from the world to the men who searched for gold under the shadow of the Pole.

Sometimes, as Buck lay by the fire after his fish, he dreamed of Judge Miller's big house in the sunny valley. But more often he remembered the man in the red sweater, the death of Curly and the great fight with Spitz. And sometimes, in his half-sleep by the fire, he seemed to see another fire and another man. This man was shorter, with long arms and hairy skin, and he wore animal skins. He did not stand straight, and he looked fearfully into the darkness, where there were glowing eyes. These were memories of an ancient world, from a time long before Buck was born.

When they arrived in Dawson, the dogs were tired and thin. They should have had a week or ten days of rest, but in two days they started back down the Yukon with letters for the outside world. The dogs were tired, the drivers were angry, and to make things worse, it snowed every day.

Dave suffered most of all, because something had gone wrong inside him. He became more gloomy and irritable than ever, and at night he cried out with pain. The drivers examined him, but they could find no broken bones. By the time they reached Cassiar Bar, he was so weak that he fell several times in the traces. The Scotsman stopped the team and took him out of the harness. He wanted to let Dave run free behind the sled.

But Dave was proud. Sick as he was, he hated to see another dog do his work. He ran beside the team, crying, and tried to push the other dog away from his place. When the sled stopped, he struggled back to his old position and stood there. The driver was moved by this, and he put him back in the harness. Dave pulled proudly again, although he cried out with pain many times.

The next morning he was too weak to travel, but he tried to crawl towards his place at the sled. The last they saw of him, he lay gasping in the snow, looking after them. The Scotsman walked slowly back to the camp they had just left, while the men waited in silence. A revolver shot rang out, and the man came back quickly. The whips cracked, the bells rang cheerfully, and the sleds moved along the trail. But Buck knew, and every dog knew, what had happened behind the trees by the river.

Chapter 7: Three Strangers from the South

Thirty days after leaving Dawson, the Salt Water Mail, with Buck and his team at the front, arrived at Skaguay. The dogs were in a terrible condition. Buck's hundred and forty pounds had gone down to a hundred and fifteen. Pike, who had often pretended to have a bad leg, was now really limping. Their feet were sore, and they had no strength left in their bodies.

"Mush on, poor sore feet," the driver encouraged them as they went down the main street. "This is the end. Then we'll get one long rest. Eh? For sure. One long rest."

The drivers expected a long stay, and the dogs desperately needed it. But there were too many letters waiting in Skaguay, from the wives and families of the men in the Klondike. Fresh dogs would take the place of the tired ones, and the useless dogs were to be sold.

Three days later two men from the States came and bought them for almost nothing. The men called each other "Hal" and "Charles". Charles was a middle-aged man with weak, watery eyes and a moustache that tried to look fierce. Hal was a young man of nineteen or twenty. He carried a big revolver and a hunting knife on a belt that was full of cartridges. Neither of them belonged in the North, and nobody could understand why they had come.

Buck heard the money pass and understood that the Scotsman and the mail drivers were leaving his life forever. When he and the team were driven to the new owners' camp, he saw a terrible mess. The tent was half up, the dishes were unwashed, and everything was in disorder. He also saw a woman. The men called her Mercedes. She was Charles's wife and Hal's sister. It was a nice family party.

Buck watched anxiously as they began to pack the sled, working hard but without knowing what they were doing. They rolled the tent into an awkward bundle three times larger than necessary. They packed the tin dishes unwashed. Mercedes kept getting in the way and giving advice.

Three men from a neighbouring tent came out and watched, grinning and winking at one another.

"You've got a real big load there," said one of them. "It isn't my business, but I wouldn't take that tent along if I were you."

"Impossible!" cried Mercedes, throwing up her hands in dismay. "However could I manage without a tent?"

"It's springtime, and you won't get any more cold weather," the man replied.

She shook her head firmly, and Charles and Hal put the last bits on top of the mountainous load.

"And of course the dogs can pull that thing all day," said another man.

"Certainly," said Hal, with cold politeness, and he took hold of the pole with one hand and swung the whip with the other. "Mush!" he shouted. "Mush on there!"

The dogs threw themselves against the breast bands and pulled hard for a few moments. Then they relaxed. They were unable to move the sled.

"The lazy brutes, I'll show them," Hal cried, preparing to hit them with the whip.

But Mercedes interfered. "Oh, Hal, you mustn't," she cried. "The poor dears! Now promise me you won't be harsh with them for the rest of the trip, or I won't go a step."

"A lot you know about dogs," her brother answered. "They're lazy, I tell you, and you've got to whip them to get anything out of them. Ask any of those men."

"They're weak as water, if you want to know," came the reply from one of the men. "They're completely worn out, that's what's the matter. They need a rest."

Again Hal's whip fell upon the dogs. They threw themselves against the breast bands, dug their feet into the packed snow and put all their strength into it. But the sled held as though it were an anchor. After two efforts, they stood still, breathing hard. The whip was whistling savagely when once more Mercedes interfered. She dropped to her knees in front of Buck, with tears in her eyes, and put her arms around his neck.

"You poor, poor dears," she cried. "Why don't you pull hard? Then you wouldn't be whipped."

One of the watching men finally spoke up. He explained that the runners of the sled were frozen to the snow. If they broke it out, the dogs could pull it. Hal followed this advice, and the sled finally moved forward, with Buck and his comrades struggling under the rain of blows.

A hundred yards ahead, the path turned and sloped steeply into the main street. The top-heavy sled turned over in the bend and spilled half its load onto the ground. The dogs, angry at the way they had been treated, ran down the street with the lighter sled bumping behind them. The whole town laughed as the dogs scattered packages, dishes and blankets along the main street of Skaguay.

Kind-hearted citizens caught the dogs and gave the newcomers some honest advice. If they wanted to reach Dawson, they should carry half the load and have twice the dogs. Hal and his family listened unwillingly. They threw out half of their things, but Mercedes cried when her clothes were thrown away. Then Charles and Hal went out and bought six more dogs. With the six original dogs and two huskies from the record trip, the team now had fourteen.

But the new dogs did not know anything about pulling a sled. Fourteen dogs need a great deal of food. Hal had calculated it carefully with a pencil: so many dogs, so many days, so many pounds of food. It was all very simple on paper. But the North was not paper, and the trail would teach them a hard lesson.

Chapter 8: John Thornton

From the beginning the journey went badly, because the three strangers did not know how to make camp. Half the night passed before they were ready to sleep, and half the morning before they were ready to start. They quarrelled about everything. The dog food was being used too quickly, and Hal decided to give the dogs smaller rations. Mercedes, meanwhile, secretly stole fish from the sacks and fed the dogs she liked best. But it was not food that Buck and the huskies needed. It was rest. They pulled the heavy load very slowly, and every day their strength grew less.

The six new dogs were the first to die. They had no experience of hard work and little food. Then the old team dogs began to go one by one. Dub, the clumsy thief, was the first. Billee, the good-natured, fell in the traces and could not get up, and Hal killed him with the axe, because he had no other way. Next went Koona. Only Buck, Pike, Joe, Teek and old Sol-leks were left, still faithful to the toil of trace and trail.

They came at last to John Thornton's camp at the mouth of the White River. When they stopped, the dogs fell down as though they had all been struck dead. Mercedes dried her eyes and looked at John Thornton. Charles sat on a log to rest. Hal did the talking. John Thornton was finishing an axe handle that he had made, and he went on with his work while he listened. He gave short answers and offered advice only when it was asked for. He knew this kind of people, and he was sure that his advice would not be followed.

"They told us up there that the trail was breaking up," Hal said. "They said we couldn't make it to White River, and here we are."

"And they told you true," John Thornton answered. "The ice is likely to break at any moment. Only fools, with the blind luck of fools, could have made it. I tell you straight, I wouldn't risk my life on that ice for all the gold in Alaska."

"That's because you're not a fool, I suppose," said Hal. "All the same, we'll go on to Dawson." He uncoiled his whip. "Get up there, Buck! Hi! Get up there! Mush on!"

Thornton went on working. He knew it was useless to get between a fool and his foolishness.

The team did not get up at the command, because it had long since reached the stage where blows were required to rouse it. The whip flashed out, here and there, on its cruel work. John Thornton pressed his lips together. Sol-leks, Teek and the others got up slowly, one by one. Buck made no effort. He lay quietly where he had fallen. The whip bit into him again and again, but he neither whined nor struggled. Several times Thornton started to speak, but each time he changed his mind.

This was the first time that Buck had failed, and it drove Hal into a rage. He changed the whip for a club. Buck still refused to move. Like his mates, he was barely able to get up, but unlike them, he had decided not to get up. He had a strange feeling of disaster. He had felt the thin, rotten ice under his feet all day. He sensed that a terrible danger was waiting for him on the frozen river ahead.

And then, suddenly, without warning, John Thornton sprang upon the man who held the club. He gave a cry that was like the cry of an animal. Hal was thrown backward, as though a falling tree had hit him. Mercedes screamed. Charles looked on sadly, wiped his watery eyes, but did not get up because of his stiff legs.

John Thornton stood over Buck. He was shaking with anger and could not speak.

"If you strike that dog again, I'll kill you," he managed to say at last, in a choking voice.

"It's my dog," Hal replied, wiping the blood from his mouth as he came back. "Get out of my way, or I'll fix you. I'm going to Dawson."

Thornton stood between him and Buck and did not move. Hal drew his long hunting knife. Mercedes screamed and laughed wildly. Thornton struck Hal's hand with the axe handle and knocked the knife to the ground. Then he picked it up himself, and with two strokes he cut Buck free from the traces.

Hal had no fight left in him, and besides, his hands were full with his sister, and Buck was too nearly dead to be of further use in pulling. A few minutes later they pulled out from the bank and down the river. Buck heard them go and raised his head to see. Pike was leading, Sol-leks was at the back, and between them were Joe and Teek. They were limping and staggering. Mercedes was riding on the loaded sled. Hal guided at the pole, and Charles stumbled along at the rear.

As Buck watched them, Thornton knelt beside him. With rough, kind hands he searched for broken bones. By the time his search had shown nothing more than many bruises and a state of terrible starvation, the sled was a quarter of a mile away. Dog and man watched it crawling along over the ice. Suddenly they saw its back end drop down, as into a hole, and the pole, with Hal clinging to it, jump into the air. Mercedes's scream came to their ears. They saw Charles turn and make one step to run back. Then a whole section of ice gave way, and dogs and humans disappeared. A yawning hole was all that could be seen. The bottom had dropped out of the trail.

John Thornton and Buck looked at each other.

"You poor devil," said John Thornton, and Buck licked his hand.

Chapter 9: For the Love of a Man

John Thornton had frozen his feet the previous December, and his partners had made him comfortable and left him to recover. They had gone up the river to cut logs for a raft, and they would return later to take him to Dawson. He was still limping slightly when he rescued Buck, but with the warm weather even the limp disappeared. Lying by the river bank through the long spring days, Buck slowly recovered his strength as well. He watched the running water, listened lazily to the songs of birds, and felt his muscles grow strong again.

For the first time in his life, Buck experienced love: genuine, passionate love. He had never known this at Judge Miller's. With the Judge's sons, hunting and walking, it had been a working partnership. With the Judge himself, it had been a dignified friendship. But love that was feverish and burning, love that was adoration and madness, it had taken John Thornton to awaken.

This man had saved his life, which was something, but he was also the ideal master. Other men looked after their dogs from a sense of duty and business. Thornton looked after his dogs as if they were his own children, because he could not help it. He had a way of taking Buck's head roughly between his hands and shaking it back and forth. At the same time he called him bad names, which to Buck were words of love. Buck knew no greater joy than that rough embrace.

Buck's love was expressed in a strange way. He would often seize Thornton's hand in his mouth and close his teeth on it so firmly that the marks remained for some time. Thornton understood that this pretended bite was a caress. For long hours Buck would lie at Thornton's feet, eager and alert, looking up into his face and studying every expression. He did not want to let Thornton out of his sight. He had lost so many masters in the North that he was afraid this one would disappear as well.

Thornton's partners, Hans and Pete, arrived on the raft they had been building. Buck accepted them, but he did not love them. He loved only Thornton, and for him he would do anything.

Late that year, in a bar in Circle City, a bad-tempered man called Black Burton started a fight with a newcomer. Thornton stepped between them good-naturedly, and Burton struck him. Buck, who had been lying in a corner with his head on his paws, rose into the air with a roar and went for the man's throat. The men pulled him away, but Burton's throat was badly torn. A meeting of miners decided that the dog had good reason to attack, and Buck was set free. From that day his name spread through every camp in Alaska.

Later, in the autumn, Buck saved Thornton's life in a different way. The three partners were guiding a long, narrow boat down a dangerous stretch of rapids on the Forty Mile Creek. Suddenly the boat turned over, and Thornton was thrown into the wild water. He was carried down towards the worst part of the rapids, where the water ran like a mill race. Buck sprang in at once. At the end of three hundred yards, in a mad swirl of water, he reached Thornton. Thornton grabbed his tail, and Buck headed for the bank, swimming with all his splendid strength. But the current was too strong. Thornton was thrown against a rock and was able to cling to its slippery top.

"Go, Buck! Go!" he shouted above the roar of the water.

Buck obeyed, and Hans and Pete pulled him out, half drowned. Then they tied a rope to his neck and shoulders and put him back into the river. The first time he missed his master, but the second time he swam straight to him. Thornton put his arms around the dog's shaggy neck, and Hans and Pete pulled them both through the foaming water to the bank. They were half dead, bruised and choking, but they were alive.

That winter, at Dawson, Buck performed another great act, one that was perhaps less heroic but made his name even more famous. In a bar, men were boasting about their favourite dogs. One man said that his dog could start a sled with five hundred pounds and walk off with it. Another said six hundred, and a third, seven hundred.

"Pooh! Pooh!" said John Thornton. "Buck can start a thousand pounds."

"And break it out of the snow? And walk off with it for a hundred yards?" demanded Matthewson, a rich mining king.

"And break it out, and walk off with it for a hundred yards," John Thornton said coolly.

"Well," said Matthewson slowly, so that everyone could hear, "I've got a thousand dollars that says he can't. And there it is." He threw a sack of gold dust the size of a sausage onto the bar.

Nobody spoke, and Thornton felt his face grow warm, because his tongue had tricked him. He did not know whether Buck could start a thousand pounds. Half a ton! And he did not have a thousand dollars. Neither did Hans or Pete.

"I've got a sled standing outside now, with twenty fifty-pound sacks of flour on it," Matthewson continued. "So don't let that stop you."

Thornton did not reply. He looked at the faces around him as if he had lost the power to think. Then he saw Jim O'Brien, an old friend.

"Can you lend me a thousand?" he asked, almost in a whisper.

"Sure," answered O'Brien, putting a heavy sack beside Matthewson's. "Though I don't have much faith, John, that the beast can do the trick."

The whole bar emptied into the street to see the test. Several hundred men, wrapped in furs, gathered round the sled. It had been standing for a couple of hours in the intense cold, sixty below zero, and the runners had frozen fast to the hard snow. Men offered odds of two to one that Buck could not even move it.

Thornton knelt down by Buck's side. He took his head in both hands and put his cheek against the dog's cheek. He did not shake him playfully, as he usually did, or murmur soft curses of love.

"As you love me, Buck. As you love me," he whispered.

Buck whined with suppressed eagerness. Thornton got up and stepped back.

"Now, Buck," he said.

Buck tightened the traces, then let them go slack a few inches. "Gee!" Thornton's voice rang out sharply in the tense silence. Buck swung to the right, ending the movement in a sudden jerk. The load trembled, and from under the runners came a crisp crackling. "Haw!" Thornton commanded. Buck repeated the manoeuvre, this time to the left. The crackling turned into a snapping, and the runners slipped several inches to the side. The sled was broken out. The men were holding their breath.

"Now, MUSH!"

Thornton's command cracked out like a pistol shot. Buck threw himself forward, gathering himself together in a tremendous effort. His whole body was tight with muscles that rolled and knotted under the silky fur. His great chest was low to the ground, his head forward and down, and his feet were flying madly. The sled swayed and trembled and half started forward. Then it moved, in a series of small jerks, and then it kept moving, faster and faster, until it was moving steadily. Men gasped and began to breathe again. When Buck passed the pile of firewood that marked the end of the hundred yards, the crowd burst into a roar of cheering.

Thornton fell on his knees beside Buck. Head was against head, and he was shaking him back and forth. Those who hurried up heard him cursing Buck, and he cursed him long and passionately, and softly and lovingly.

"Sir! Sir!" stammered a rich man from the Skookum Benches. "I'll give you a thousand for him, sir, a thousand, sir... twelve hundred, sir."

Thornton rose to his feet. His eyes were wet, and the tears were streaming openly down his cheeks.

"Sir," he said to the rich man, "no, sir. You can go to hell, sir. It's the best I can do for you, sir."

Buck seized Thornton's hand in his teeth. Thornton shook him back and forth. As though moved by a common impulse, the crowd stepped back respectfully, and they were not again careless enough to interrupt.

Chapter 10: The Call in the Forest

When Buck won sixteen hundred dollars in five minutes for John Thornton, he made it possible for his master to pay certain debts. He also allowed him to travel with his partners into the East, after a fabulous lost mine.

So John Thornton, Hans and Pete, with Buck and half a dozen other dogs, set out into an unknown land. John Thornton asked little of men or nature and was not afraid of the wild. With a handful of salt and a rifle, he could disappear into the wilderness and live wherever he pleased, for as long as he pleased. Like the native hunters, he hunted his dinner in the course of the day's travel.

To Buck this was a life of endless delight: hunting, fishing and wandering through strange places. For weeks they travelled steadily, and for weeks they camped here and there while the dogs rested and the men burned holes through the frozen earth, looking for gold. A summer came and went, and another winter. In the spring they found, not the lost mine, but a wide valley where the gold showed like yellow butter in the bottom of the washing pan. They searched no further. Each day they worked, they earned thousands of dollars in clean gold dust and nuggets. They put the gold into moose-hide bags, fifty pounds to the bag, and piled them like firewood outside their cabin.

There was nothing for the dogs to do except haul in the meat that Thornton killed now and again. Buck spent long hours thinking by the fire. The vision of the short, hairy man came to him more frequently now. And there was something else. Deep in the forest a call was sounding. It filled him with a great unrest and strange desires. Sometimes he followed the call into the forest, searching for it as though it were a real thing. He would push his nose into the cool wood moss and lie for whole days, hidden, watching and listening to everything that moved around him.

One night he woke up suddenly, with his nose and ears alert. From the forest came the call, more distinctly than ever before: a long howl, like the howls of husky dogs, yet different. He ran silently through the sleeping camp and into the woods. In an open place among the trees he saw a long, thin timber wolf, sitting with its nose pointed to the sky.

Buck walked into the open, half crouching, every movement full of a strange mixture of threat and friendship. But the wolf ran away. Buck followed, and at last he trapped it at the end of a narrow stream. The wolf turned and snarled, its teeth clipping together rapidly, ready to defend itself. Buck did not attack. He circled round it, making friendly advances. The wolf was suspicious and afraid, because Buck was three times its weight. Several times it escaped, and several times Buck caught up with it. At last, when it understood that Buck meant no harm, it sniffed noses with him. Then they became friendly and played together in the nervous, half shy way that wild animals have.

After some time the wolf started off at an easy run, inviting Buck to come. They ran side by side through the twilight, up the stream and over the mountains to a land of great forests and many streams. Hour after hour they ran, and the sun rose higher, and the day grew warm. Buck was wildly happy. He knew that he was at last answering the call, running with his wood brother towards the place from which the call surely came. Old memories were coming to him quickly. He had done this thing before, somewhere in that other dimly remembered world.

They stopped by a stream to drink, and as he stopped, Buck remembered John Thornton. He sat down. The wolf started on towards the place the call came from, then returned to him, sniffing noses and trying to encourage him. But Buck turned round and started slowly on the way back. For an hour the wild brother ran beside him, whining softly. Then he sat down, pointed his nose upward and howled. It was a sad howl, and as Buck continued steadily on his way, he heard it grow faint and fainter until it was lost in the distance.

John Thornton was eating dinner when Buck burst into the camp and jumped upon him in a frenzy of affection. For two days and nights Buck never left camp, nor let Thornton out of his sight. But after two days the call in the forest began to sound more powerfully than ever. Buck's restlessness came back, and he was haunted by memories of the wild brother and the run side by side through the wide forest.

Once again he began to wander in the woods, but the wild brother came no more. Sometimes he stayed away for three or four days at a time. He fished for salmon in the streams and killed a large black bear that had been blinded by mosquitoes. He was a killer now, a creature that lived on other living things, alone, by his own strength, surviving in a hostile environment where only the strong survived. He was so splendid and so proud that, when Thornton and the others watched him leave the camp, they said: "Never was there such a dog."

In the autumn the moose appeared in great numbers, moving slowly down to spend the winter in the lower valleys. Buck chose a great bull, more than six feet tall, with a terrible temper and an arrow wound in his side. For four days Buck stayed close to him, never giving him a moment's rest. He separated him from the herd, kept him from eating the leaves of young trees and prevented him from drinking at the streams. Day by day the great bull grew weaker. At the end of the fourth day Buck pulled the huge animal down. For a day and a night he stayed by the kill, eating and sleeping. Then, rested and strong, he turned his face towards the camp and John Thornton.

Chapter 11: The Ghost Dog

Buck travelled home with a long, easy run, hour after hour, never losing his way. But as he got nearer, he felt a growing sense of danger. The air of the forest was full of a strange new life. The birds talked of it, the squirrels chattered about it, and the very wind whispered of it. Three miles from camp he came upon a fresh trail that made the hair on his neck rise. It led straight towards the camp and John Thornton. Buck hurried on, swiftly and silently.

He found one of the sled dogs lying dead in the trail, with an arrow through his body. A hundred yards further on he found another, dying. Then he found Hans, lying on his face with arrows in his back. From the direction of the camp came the faint sound of many voices, rising and falling in a chant. Buck crawled to the edge of the clearing. There he saw the ruined cabin, and men of the Yeehat people moving among the ruins. They had come to the valley that was their own land, and the strangers had paid with their lives.

Buck gave a terrible roar of fury. For the last time in his life he allowed passion to take the place of cunning and reason. It was because of his great love for John Thornton that he lost his head.

The Yeehats did not know what had hit them. Buck rushed into the camp like a storm, moving with such incredible speed that their arrows could not reach him. He sprang at the nearest man and tore at his throat. He jumped from one to another, so quickly that he seemed to be everywhere at the same moment. In panic the Yeehats fled into the woods, shouting that the Evil Spirit had come. Buck chased them through the trees, and then he returned to the silent, ruined camp.

He found Pete where he had been killed in his blankets, in the first moment of surprise. Thornton's desperate struggle was written in the earth, and Buck followed every detail of it with his nose to the edge of a deep pool. At the edge, head and front feet in the water, lay Skeet, a faithful little dog who had stayed with him until the end. The pool itself was muddy and coloured with mud from the washing boxes. It effectively hid what it contained, and it contained John Thornton. Buck followed his trail into the water, and from it no trail led away.

All day Buck stayed by the pool or wandered restlessly around the camp. He knew that death was the end of all movement, and he knew that John Thornton was dead. It left a great emptiness in him, an emptiness that ached and ached, and that food could not fill. At times, when he stopped to look at the bodies of the Yeehats, he forgot the pain. He felt a great pride in himself, a pride greater than any he had experienced. He had killed man, the noblest game of all, and he had killed in the face of the law of club and fang. From now on he would not be afraid of men, except when they carried arrows, spears and clubs.

Night came, and a full moon rose high over the trees. As he sat by the pool in the moonlight, Buck became aware of a new life stirring in the forest. He stood up, listening and smelling. From far away came a faint, sharp howl, followed by a chorus of similar howls. As the moments passed, the howls grew closer and louder. Again Buck knew them as things heard in that other world that lived in his memory. He walked to the centre of the open space and listened. It was the call, the call of many notes, sounding more powerfully than ever before. And as never before, he was ready to obey. John Thornton was dead. The last tie was broken. Man and the claims of man no longer bound him.

The wolf pack poured into the clearing like a silver flood in the moonlight. In the centre stood Buck, motionless as a statue, waiting for them. They were frightened by his size and stillness, and for a moment nobody moved. Then the boldest one leaped straight at him. Like a flash, Buck struck and broke its neck. Then he stood without moving, as before. Three others tried it, one after another, and each one fell back with blood streaming from a torn throat or shoulder.

Then the whole pack came forward in a confused crowd. Buck backed up against a high bank of earth, where they could attack him only from the front. He fought so well that, after half an hour, the wolves drew back, defeated. Their tongues hung out, and their white teeth shone cruelly in the white moonlight.

Then a wolf, long and thin and grey, came forward carefully, in a friendly manner. Buck recognised the wild brother with whom he had run for a night and a day. He was whining softly, and when Buck whined too, they touched noses.

Then an old wolf, thin and battle-scarred, came forward. Buck lifted his lip to snarl, but instead he sniffed noses with him. The old wolf sat down, pointed his nose at the moon and broke out into the long wolf howl. The others sat down and howled too. And now the call came to Buck in unmistakable accents. He also sat down and howled. When this was over, he came out of his corner, and the pack crowded around him, sniffing in a half-friendly and half-savage way. The leaders lifted the yelp of the pack and sprang away into the woods. The wolves swung in behind, yelping in chorus. And Buck ran with them, side by side with the wild brother, yelping as he ran.

And here the story of Buck may well end. Not many years passed before the Yeehats noticed a change in the breed of timber wolves. Some were seen with brown on their heads and noses, and with a white stripe down the middle of the chest. But even more remarkable, the Yeehats tell of a Ghost Dog that runs at the head of the pack. They are afraid of this Ghost Dog, because it is more cunning than they are. It steals from their camps in hard winters, robs their traps and kills their dogs, and it fears none of their bravest hunters.

And there is a valley where the Yeehats never go. In the summers there is one visitor, however, to that valley. It is a great, gloriously coated wolf, like and yet unlike all other wolves. He comes alone from the smiling timber land and walks down into an open space among the trees. Here a yellow stream flows from rotted moose-hide sacks and sinks into the ground, with long grasses growing through it. Here he thinks for a time, howling once, long and sadly, before he leaves.

But he is not always alone. When the long winter nights come on, the wolves follow their meat into the lower valleys. Then he may be seen running at the head of the pack through the pale moonlight. His huge body leaps high above his companions, and his great throat sings a song of the younger world, which is the song of the pack.

The End
