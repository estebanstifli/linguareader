Chapter 1: Mrs Rachel Lynde Is Surprised

Mrs Rachel Lynde lived just where the main road of Avonlea dipped down into a little hollow. A small brook ran past her house, and nothing in the village escaped her eyes. If a stranger walked along the road, Mrs Lynde knew it. If somebody bought a new hat, Mrs Lynde knew it before the end of the week. She was a busy, helpful woman, and she had opinions about everything.

One afternoon in early June, she was sitting at her kitchen window, knitting a cotton quilt. Suddenly she saw something very strange. Matthew Cuthbert was driving past in his buggy, and he was wearing his best suit and a white collar.

Matthew was a shy man of sixty who hated meeting people. He almost never left his farm at Green Gables, and he never, ever wore a white collar on a weekday.

"Now where is Matthew Cuthbert going?" Mrs Lynde asked herself. "And why is he wearing his best clothes? If he were going to town for seeds, he would wear his old coat. Something has happened, and I must find out what it is."

So after tea she put on her shawl and walked up the long red lane to Green Gables. The house stood far back from the road, among apple trees and tall white birches. Matthew and his sister Marilla had lived there all their lives. Neither of them had ever married.

Marilla was in the kitchen. She was a tall, thin woman with grey hair twisted into a hard little knot, and she did not smile very often. The kitchen was clean, cold and perfectly tidy, just like Marilla herself.

"Good evening, Rachel," she said. "Sit down. How are you all?"

"We're all well," said Mrs Lynde. "But I was afraid that you were ill, Marilla. I saw Matthew driving away in his best suit, and I thought he might be going for the doctor."

Marilla's lips moved a little, as if she wanted to laugh. She had expected this visit.

"Oh no, I'm quite well," she said. "Matthew has gone to Bright River station. We're getting a little boy from an orphan asylum in Nova Scotia, and he's coming on the train tonight."

If Marilla had said that Matthew had gone to meet a kangaroo, Mrs Lynde could not have been more astonished. For a moment she could not speak at all.

"An orphan boy!" she cried at last. "Marilla Cuthbert, are you serious?"

"Of course I am," said Marilla calmly. "Matthew is getting old, and his heart troubles him. We need a boy to help him on the farm. Mrs Alexander Spencer is getting a little girl from the asylum, so we asked her to bring us a boy of ten or eleven. He'll be old enough to do some work, and young enough to learn our ways."

"Well, Marilla, I'll tell you plainly what I think," said Mrs Lynde. "You're making a big mistake. You don't know anything about this child. You're bringing a strange boy into your house, and you don't know what he's like or who his parents were. Only last week I read in the newspaper about an orphan boy who set fire to a farmhouse. And there was another one who put poison in the well."

"We aren't getting a girl," said Marilla, as if that answered everything. "I would never take a girl. But a boy is a different matter. Of course there are risks, Rachel. There are risks in everything we do."

Mrs Lynde shook her head. She stayed a little longer, and then she hurried away to tell the news to everybody she knew.

"Well, I'm sorry for that poor boy," she said to herself as she walked home. "Matthew and Marilla don't know anything about children. They'll expect him to be wiser and steadier than his own grandfather. It seems unnatural somehow."

But if Mrs Rachel had seen who was waiting at Bright River station at that moment, she would have been even more surprised.

Chapter 2: Matthew Cuthbert Is Surprised

Matthew enjoyed the drive to Bright River. The road ran between farms and woods, and the wild plum trees were white with flowers. The air was sweet with the smell of apple blossom, and birds were singing everywhere. Matthew liked the drive because he did not have to talk to anybody on the way.

He was afraid of all women except Marilla and Mrs Rachel. He had a feeling that they were secretly laughing at him, and perhaps sometimes they were, because he was an odd-looking man. He had long grey hair, a soft brown beard and round shoulders. But he had the kindest heart in Avonlea.

When he arrived at the station, the train had already gone. The platform was empty except for one person: a girl was sitting on a pile of wooden boards at the far end. Matthew walked past her as quickly as possible, without looking at her face.

"Has the half-past-five train come in?" he asked the stationmaster.

"It came and went half an hour ago," the man said. "But there was a passenger left for you. A little girl. She's sitting out there. I asked her to wait in the ladies' room, but she said she'd rather stay outside. She said there was more scope for imagination there. She's a strange one, I can tell you."

"But I'm not expecting a girl," said Matthew. "It's a boy I've come for. Mrs Spencer was going to bring him from Nova Scotia."

The stationmaster whistled. "Well, Mrs Spencer got off the train with that girl and gave her to me. She said you were going to collect her. That's all I know. I haven't got any orphan boys hidden here."

He walked off, leaving poor Matthew alone. It would be easier, Matthew thought, to walk into a lion's cage than to speak to that strange girl. But he had to do it.

She was about eleven years old, and she was wearing a short, tight, ugly dress of yellowish-grey cotton. Under a faded brown hat, her hair hung down her back in two thick plaits. It was very red. Her face was small, white and thin, with a lot of freckles, and her large eyes looked green in some lights and grey in others. Her mouth was wide and sweet, and her chin was pointed.

As soon as she saw him coming, she stood up. She held a shabby old bag in one hand, and she held out the other hand to him.

"I suppose you are Mr Matthew Cuthbert of Green Gables," she said in a clear, sweet voice. "I'm very glad to see you. I was beginning to be afraid that you weren't coming. I had decided that if you didn't come tonight, I would climb up into that big wild cherry tree and stay there all night. I wouldn't be a bit afraid. It would be lovely to sleep in a wild cherry tree all white with blossom in the moonlight, don't you think? You could imagine you were living in marble halls."

Matthew took the thin little hand in his own. In that moment he decided what to do. He could not tell this child that there had been a mistake. He would take her home, and Marilla could tell her. She could not be left at Bright River anyway.

"I'm sorry I was late," he said shyly. "Come along. The horse is over there. Give me your bag."

"Oh, I can carry it," she said cheerfully. "It isn't heavy. I've got all my worldly goods in it, but it isn't heavy. And if you don't carry it in a certain way, the handle comes off. So I'd better keep it, because I know exactly how to do it."

As soon as they were in the buggy, she began to talk, and she did not stop for the whole journey.

"Oh, it seems so wonderful that I'm going to live with you and belong to you," she said. "I've never belonged to anybody, not really. The asylum was the worst. I was only there four months, but that was enough. It's not that the people were bad. They were very kind, really. But there's so little scope for the imagination in an asylum. Only the other orphans. Do you mind if I talk? People are always telling me that I talk too much."

"Oh, you can talk as much as you like," said Matthew. "I don't mind."

And to his own surprise, he found that he was enjoying himself. He did not need to say anything. He only needed to listen.

They drove round a curve in the road, and the girl gave a little cry. In front of them was an avenue of apple trees, and their branches met high above the road like a roof of snowy white flowers. Below them the air was full of a purple light. The child sat in silence with her hands clasped and her face lifted towards the white splendour above.

She did not speak again for three whole miles. Then they came to a pond that lay in a valley, shining with all the colours of the sunset.

"What is that pond called?" she asked.

"That's Barry's pond," said Matthew.

"Oh, I don't like that name," she said. "I shall call it the Lake of Shining Waters. Yes, that's the right name for it. And that lovely road with the trees, I shall call it the White Way of Delight. When I find a name that suits a place exactly, it gives me a thrill. Do things ever give you a thrill, Mr Cuthbert?"

Matthew thought hard. "Well, it always gives me a kind of thrill to see those ugly white grubs that the farmer digs up in the cucumber beds. I hate the look of them."

"Oh, I don't think that's exactly the same kind of thrill," she said politely.

At last they climbed a hill, and she saw a grey-roofed house far away, half hidden among the trees. Above it a single star was shining in the evening sky.

"That's it, isn't it?" she said, pointing. "That's Green Gables. I knew it the moment I saw it. Oh, I'm so happy! I've been pinching myself all day to make sure I'm awake. But now I know it's real, and we're nearly home."

Matthew moved uneasily on the seat. He was glad that Marilla, and not he, would have to tell this child that the home she longed for was not going to be hers after all.

Chapter 3: Not a Boy

Marilla came quickly to the door when she heard the buggy. But when her eyes fell on the odd little figure in the stiff, ugly dress, with the long red plaits and the shining eyes, she stopped in amazement.

"Matthew Cuthbert, who is that?" she cried. "Where is the boy?"

"There wasn't any boy," said Matthew miserably. "There was only her."

"No boy! But there must have been a boy," said Marilla. "We sent a message to Mrs Spencer to bring us a boy."

"Well, she didn't. She brought her. I couldn't leave her at the station, Marilla, whatever the mistake was."

The child had been listening, and her face changed. The joy went out of it. Suddenly she dropped her precious bag and took a step forward with her hands clasped.

"You don't want me!" she cried. "You don't want me because I'm not a boy! I might have expected it. Nobody ever did want me. I might have known that it was all too beautiful to last. Oh, what shall I do? I'm going to burst into tears!"

And she did. She sat down on a chair by the table, put her face in her arms and cried as if her heart was broken. Matthew and Marilla looked at each other over her head. Neither of them knew what to do.

"Well, well, there's no need to cry so much," said Marilla at last.

"Yes, there is need!" The child raised her head and showed a face wet with tears. "You would cry too if you were an orphan. Imagine arriving at a place that you thought was going to be home, and then discovering that they didn't want you because you weren't a boy. Oh, this is the most tragic thing that has ever happened to me!"

Something like a smile appeared on Marilla's face.

"Well, don't cry any more. We aren't going to turn you out tonight. You'll have to stay here until we find out what happened. What's your name?"

The child hesitated. "Will you please call me Cordelia?"

"Call you Cordelia? Is that your name?"

"No, it's not exactly my name, but I would love to be called Cordelia. It's such a perfectly elegant name."

"I don't know what you mean. If Cordelia isn't your name, what is?"

"Anne Shirley," said the girl sadly. "But please call me Cordelia. It can't matter much to you what you call me if I'm only going to be here a little while, can it? And Anne is such an unromantic name."

"Nonsense," said Marilla. "Anne is a real, good, sensible name. You don't need to be ashamed of it."

"Oh, I'm not ashamed of it," said Anne. "I just like Cordelia better. But if you call me Anne, please call me Anne with an E. A-n-n-e looks so much more distinguished than A-n-n. If you'll only call me Anne spelled with an E, I'll try to forgive you for not calling me Cordelia."

"Very well, then, Anne spelled with an E," said Marilla, and she could not hide her smile now.

At supper Anne could not eat a thing. She was in the depths of despair, she explained. Then Marilla took her upstairs to a small white room under the eastern gable. It was very clean and very bare, and its emptiness made Anne shiver. She undressed quickly, jumped into bed and pulled the blankets over her head.

Downstairs Marilla began to wash the dishes.

"Well, she must go back to the asylum," she said. "Mrs Spencer made the mistake, so she must take the girl back."

"Yes, I suppose so," said Matthew slowly.

"You suppose so! Don't you know it?"

"Well, she's a really nice little thing, Marilla. It's kind of a pity to send her back when she's so set on staying here."

"Matthew Cuthbert, you don't mean to say that you think we should keep her! What good would she be to us?"

Matthew was silent for a moment. Then he said something very surprising.

"We might be some good to her."

The next afternoon Marilla drove Anne to Mrs Spencer's house at White Sands. On the way Anne told her about her life. Her parents had been teachers, and they had both died of fever when she was a baby. Then poor people had taken her in, and she had looked after their many children until the father died. After that she had lived with another family who had three sets of twins. She had worked for them until she was sent to the asylum.

"Were those women kind to you?" asked Marilla.

Anne's face turned red. "Oh, they meant to be. I know they meant to be just as kind as possible. And when people mean to be good to you, you don't mind very much when they're not, always."

Marilla asked no more questions. But a pity she had never felt before was beginning to stir in her heart.

At Mrs Spencer's house they had a piece of news. A woman called Mrs Peter Blewett was looking for a girl to help with her large family of children, and she happened to be visiting that very afternoon. Marilla knew her reputation. She had a sharp tongue and a bad temper, and her servant girls never stayed long.

Mrs Blewett looked Anne up and down with her small, sharp eyes.

"She's very thin," she said. "But the thin ones are often the strongest. I'll take her, if you like. You'll have to earn your keep, girl, and no nonsense about it. My baby is very cross, and I'm tired of looking after him."

Anne's face went white. She looked at Marilla like a little animal caught in a trap.

Marilla hesitated. She thought of Matthew's face that morning when she had left. She knew very well that she could not give a child, even this child, to a woman like Mrs Blewett.

"Well, I don't know," she said slowly. "I haven't really decided yet. Matthew and I might keep her after all. I'll take her home again and talk to my brother. If we decide not to keep her, we'll send her to you tomorrow."

On the way home Anne sat perfectly still.

"Did you really say that perhaps you would let me stay at Green Gables?" she whispered at last.

"I have not decided yet," said Marilla. "You had better not get too excited about it."

When Matthew heard the story, his face lit up with quiet joy.

"Well, now, Marilla," he said. "I'd hate to see her go to Mrs Blewett's."

"I've never brought up a child, and especially not a girl," said Marilla. "I'll probably make a terrible mess of it. But I'll do my best. So, Matthew, she can stay."

The next morning Marilla called Anne and told her the news. The child burst into tears again, but this time they were happy ones.

"Oh, I'll try so hard to be good," she promised. "But you'll have to be patient with me. I have so many faults. I forget things, and I imagine things, and I talk too much. But I'll try, really I will."

Chapter 4: Mrs Rachel Lynde Is Horrified

Before long Marilla discovered that Anne had never said a prayer before going to sleep. That shocked her very much.

"You must say your prayers every night, Anne," she said firmly.

"Oh, of course, if you want me to," Anne said. "But I've never said any. Mrs Thomas told me that God made my hair red on purpose, and since then I haven't cared about Him. But I don't mind trying. What shall I say?"

Marilla told her to kneel down and to thank God for her blessings and ask Him for the things she wanted. Anne knelt, thought for a moment, and then she began.

"Gracious heavenly Father, I thank Thee for the White Way of Delight and the Lake of Shining Waters and Bonny and the Snow Queen. Bonny is my geranium and the Snow Queen is the cherry tree outside my window. That's all the blessings I can think of right now. As for the things I want, there are so many that it would take a long time to name them all. So I'll only mention the two most important. Please let me stay at Green Gables, and please let me be good-looking when I grow up. I remain, Yours respectfully, Anne Shirley."

Marilla nearly fainted. It was clear that the child's religious education had to begin at once.

For two weeks Anne was happy. She explored every field and every path of Green Gables. She found a brook below the farm, a wood of firs and maples, and a little bridge over a stream. She made friends with every tree. She talked to Matthew in the barn and asked Marilla a hundred questions while she washed the dishes. Marilla often told her to stop talking, but Anne could never stop for very long.

Then Mrs Rachel Lynde came to inspect the orphan.

"Well, they didn't pick you for your looks, that's sure," said Mrs Rachel at once. "She's terribly skinny and plain, Marilla. Come here, child, and let me look at you. Did anyone ever see so many freckles? And hair as red as carrots!"

Anne crossed the kitchen in one jump and stood in front of Mrs Lynde. Her face was red with anger, her lips were trembling and her whole thin body was shaking.

"I hate you!" she cried, stamping her foot on the floor. "I hate you, I hate you, I hate you! How dare you call me skinny and ugly? How dare you say I'm freckled and red-headed? You are a rude, impolite, unfeeling woman!"

"Anne!" cried Marilla.

But Anne did not stop. "How would you like it if somebody said that you were fat and clumsy and probably hadn't a spark of imagination in you? I don't care if I do hurt your feelings! You have hurt mine worse than they were ever hurt before. And I'll never forgive you for it, never, never!"

Stamp! Stamp!

"Did anybody ever see such a temper!" cried Mrs Rachel.

"Anne, go to your room and stay there until I come up," said Marilla, when she could speak.

Anne ran upstairs, and the door of her room shut with a bang that shook the house.

"Well, I don't envy you the job of bringing that up, Marilla," said Mrs Lynde.

"You shouldn't have criticised her looks, Rachel," Marilla said, to her own surprise.

Mrs Rachel stood up with great dignity and went home. Marilla went upstairs slowly. She did not know what she should do. But she knew that Anne had to apologise.

She found Anne lying face down on the bed, crying bitterly.

"Anne, you must go to Mrs Lynde and tell her that you are very sorry for your rude behaviour."

"I can never do that," said Anne. "You can shut me up in a dark, damp prison full of snakes and toads, and feed me only on bread and water, and I won't complain. But I can't ask Mrs Lynde to forgive me."

"Then you'll stay in this room until you're willing to do it," said Marilla.

The next day passed, and Anne stayed in her room. She hardly ate anything. In the evening, while Marilla was out milking the cows, Matthew crept quietly up the stairs. He had not been in that room for years.

"Anne," he whispered. "How are you getting on?"

Anne smiled weakly. "Oh, pretty well. I imagine a lot, and that helps to pass the time. Of course, it's rather lonely."

"Well, now, Anne, don't you think you'd better do it and get it over with?" said Matthew. "Marilla's a terribly determined woman. Just smooth it over, so to speak."

"Do you mean apologise to Mrs Lynde?"

"Yes, apologise, that's the word. It's so lonely downstairs without you."

Anne thought for a moment. "Well, I'd do anything for you, Matthew," she said. "I'm not angry any more. I'm only ashamed of myself. If you really want me to, I'll do it."

"Well now, of course I do. But don't tell Marilla I said anything. She told me not to interfere."

So that evening, Anne and Marilla walked down the lane to Mrs Lynde's house. Anne was very quiet at first. But by the time they arrived, she was walking with her head up and a strange light in her eyes. Suddenly she fell on her knees in front of the astonished Mrs Rachel and held out her hands.

"Oh, Mrs Lynde, I am so extremely sorry," she said, and her voice trembled. "I could never express all my sorrow, no, not if I used a whole dictionary. I behaved terribly to you, and I've disgraced my dear friends Matthew and Marilla, who have let me stay at Green Gables although I'm not a boy. Everything you said was true. My hair is red, and I'm freckled and skinny and ugly. Oh, Mrs Lynde, please, please forgive me. If you refuse, it will be a lifelong sorrow to me."

Marilla stared at her. Anne was clearly enjoying her apology very much.

"There, there, get up, child," said Mrs Lynde warmly. "Of course I forgive you. I was a little too hard on you, anyway. But I'm such an honest person. You mustn't mind me, that's all. And your hair might turn a nice auburn colour when you grow up. I knew a girl at school whose hair was every bit as red as yours, and when she grew up it became a really handsome auburn."

"Oh, Mrs Lynde!" Anne stood up and took a long breath. "You have given me hope. I shall always feel that you are a benefactor."

On the way home Marilla was silent, but she was smiling in the darkness.

"I feel so happy," said Anne. "I'll try to control my temper. But it isn't easy when people make fun of my hair. Do you think my hair really will be auburn when I grow up?"

"I think you should worry less about your hair and more about your behaviour," said Marilla. But when Anne slipped her small hand into Marilla's hard old one, something warm and sweet rose in the older woman's heart.

Chapter 5: A Bosom Friend and a Lost Brooch

Not far from Green Gables, on the other side of the brook, lived the Barry family, and they had a daughter called Diana who was about the same age as Anne. Anne had dreamed of a real friend all her life. In the asylum and in the houses where she had worked, her only friends had been imaginary ones. There was a girl who lived in the glass of a bookcase door, and an echo that answered her from a green valley.

"Oh, Marilla," she said, the evening before her visit to the Barrys, "what if she doesn't like me? It would be the most tragic disappointment of my life."

"Don't get so excited," said Marilla. "And don't use such long words. It sounds very strange in a little girl."

Diana turned out to be a pretty girl with black hair, black eyes and rosy cheeks, and she had a merry smile that she had probably inherited from her father. While the two mothers talked in the parlour, the girls went out into the garden. They stood shyly among the tiger lilies for a while, looking at each other. Then Anne took a deep breath.

"Oh, Diana," she said, almost in a whisper, "do you think you can like me a little, enough to be my bosom friend?"

Diana laughed, because she always laughed before she spoke. "Why, I guess so," she said. "I'm very glad you've come to live at Green Gables. It will be fun to have somebody to play with. There isn't any other girl who lives near enough."

"Will you swear to be my friend for ever and ever?" Anne asked eagerly.

Diana looked shocked. "Swearing is dreadfully wicked," she said.

"Oh no, not my kind of swearing. There are two kinds, you know. My kind just means making a solemn promise."

So they held hands over the garden path. Anne said the words very seriously: "I solemnly swear to be faithful to my bosom friend, Diana Barry, as long as the sun and moon shall endure." Then Diana repeated the oath with a laugh, and their friendship began.

All that summer the two girls played together in the woods and fields between their homes. They gave romantic names to everything they found. A small path through the trees became Lovers' Lane. A little circle of birch trees became the playhouse called Idlewild, where they kept broken bits of china and pretended they were precious treasures.

In July, the Sunday school announced a picnic by the Lake of Shining Waters, and there was going to be ice cream. Anne had never tasted ice cream in her life, and she could hardly think about anything else.

Then, one afternoon, something terrible happened. Marilla owned an amethyst brooch that had belonged to her mother. It was her most treasured possession, and she always wore it to church. One day she noticed that it was missing from its usual place on her pincushion.

"Anne, have you seen my amethyst brooch?" she asked. "I put it on my pincushion yesterday, and I can't find it anywhere."

"Yes, I saw it this afternoon when you were out," said Anne slowly. "I went into your room and I pinned it on my dress, just to see how it would look. But I put it back on the pincushion, I'm sure I did."

Marilla searched the whole house, but the brooch was nowhere to be found. She was convinced that Anne had taken it and lost it, and that she was telling a lie. She sent the child to her room and told her she would stay there until she confessed. Anne cried and protested that she was telling the truth, but Marilla did not believe her.

The day of the picnic came closer and closer. On the morning of the picnic, Anne called Marilla to her room and made a confession in a voice like a girl reciting a lesson.

"I took the amethyst brooch," she said. "I wanted to wear it to Idlewild and pretend I was the Lady Cordelia Fitzgerald. But on the way home I was crossing the bridge over the Lake of Shining Waters, and I took it off to look at it again. It slipped through my fingers, and it went down, down, down, all purply sparkling, and sank for ever. That's the best I can do at confessing, Marilla. Now may I go to the picnic, please?"

Marilla was furious. "Picnic indeed! You'll go to no picnic today, Anne Shirley. That's your punishment. And it isn't half bad enough for what you've done."

Anne flung herself on the bed and cried as if her world had ended. She had only confessed because she thought it would let her go to the picnic, and now she had lost everything.

But that afternoon, when Marilla took out her best black lace shawl to mend it, something glittered among the threads. It was the amethyst brooch, hanging by its pin from a corner of the lace. Marilla suddenly remembered that she had taken off the brooch on the day it was lost and laid it on top of the shawl for a moment.

She went upstairs with the brooch in her hand. "Anne Shirley, what does this mean? I found my brooch hanging on my black lace shawl. Why did you tell me that you had lost it in the lake?"

"Well, you said you'd keep me here until I confessed," said Anne wearily. "So I decided to confess, because I wanted to go to the picnic. I invented the confession last night after I went to bed, and I made it as interesting as I could."

Marilla wanted to laugh, but her conscience stopped her. It was she who had been wrong.

"Anne, you do beat everything," she said. "But I was wrong, and I see that now. I shouldn't have doubted your word when you'd never told me a lie. Of course, it wasn't right to confess to something you hadn't done. But I drove you to it. So if you'll forgive me, I'll forgive you. And now get yourself ready for the picnic."

Anne flew to the window and back. "Oh, Marilla, isn't it too late?"

"No, it's only two o'clock. They won't have started tea yet."

That night Anne came home in a state of perfect happiness. "Oh, Marilla, I've had a scrumptious time," she said. "We had a row on the lake, and Jane Andrews nearly fell overboard, and I ate ice cream. Words fail me to describe that ice cream. Marilla, I assure you it was sublime."

Chapter 6: Carrots

In September, Anne began going to the village school with Diana. It was a low white building beside the road, and the children sat in pairs at old desks covered in carved initials. Anne shared a desk with Diana, and she was soon happy there. She learned quickly, and her imagination made her lessons much more interesting than the teacher, Mr Phillips, ever did.

For the first three weeks, a boy called Gilbert Blythe was away visiting his cousins in New Brunswick. When he came back, all the girls became excited.

"He's awfully handsome," whispered Diana. "And he teases the girls terribly. He just torments our lives out."

Gilbert was a tall boy with curly brown hair, mischievous hazel eyes and a teasing smile. He was used to being popular, and he was used to having every girl look at him. But Anne Shirley did not look at him at all. She was gazing dreamily out of the window at the Lake of Shining Waters, lost in some romantic adventure of her own.

Gilbert was not pleased. He was determined to make that red-haired girl look at him. So he reached across the aisle and picked up the end of one of her long red plaits. He held it out at arm's length and said in a loud whisper that the whole room could hear:

"Carrots! Carrots!"

Then Anne looked at him with a vengeance.

She jumped to her feet, and her eyes flashed with angry tears. "You mean, hateful boy!" she cried. "How dare you!"

And then, before anybody could stop her, she brought her heavy slate down on Gilbert's head with a crack. The slate broke in two pieces, though Gilbert's head was not damaged at all.

The class gasped with horrified delight. Mr Phillips came down the aisle and laid his hand heavily on Anne's shoulder.

"Anne Shirley, what does this mean?" he asked angrily.

Anne did not answer. She would not tell the whole school that she had been called Carrots. It was Gilbert who spoke up bravely.

"It was my fault, Mr Phillips. I teased her."

But Mr Phillips did not listen. He made Anne stand in front of the blackboard for the rest of the afternoon, and he wrote above her head in large letters: Ann Shirley has a very bad temper. Ann Shirley must learn to control her temper. He even spelled her name without an E, which made it much worse.

When school was over, Gilbert tried to apologise. "I'm awfully sorry I made fun of your hair, Anne," he whispered. "Honestly I am. Don't be angry for keeps."

Anne walked past him without a word or a look. "Oh, how could you, Anne?" Diana breathed as they went down the road. But Anne had made up her mind. She would never, ever forgive Gilbert Blythe.

The next day, things became even worse. The children often spent their lunch break in a pine wood on a hill near the school, and they ran back when they saw Mr Phillips returning. Anne, who had been dreaming among the trees, arrived last. Mr Phillips was in a bad mood, and he decided to punish her in front of everyone.

"Since you seem to enjoy the company of the boys so much, Anne, you may sit with Gilbert Blythe this afternoon," he said.

Anne went white. She picked up her books and sat down next to Gilbert. Then she put her head on the desk and kept it there for the rest of the lesson. When school ended, she took every one of her books, pens and pencils out of her desk and carried them home.

"I'm not going back to school any more," she told Marilla. "I've been insulted, and I won't go."

Marilla was wise enough not to argue. She went down to see Mrs Lynde, who had brought up ten children and had strong opinions about education.

"Let her stay at home for a while," advised Mrs Rachel. "She'll get tired of it, and then she'll go back by herself. That Mr Phillips is no good as a teacher anyway. He spends all his time looking at the older girls and paying no attention to the little ones."

So Anne stayed at home and did her lessons by herself, until Diana's loneliness and her own curiosity were stronger than her pride. She went back to school a few weeks later. She made friends with all the other girls and did splendidly in her lessons. But she continued to ignore Gilbert completely, as if he did not exist.

Secretly, however, she was competing with him in every subject. Gilbert was the cleverest boy in the school, and Anne was determined to beat him. Sometimes he was at the top of the class in spelling, and sometimes she was. Sometimes they were equal. It was the most exciting race in Avonlea, though only one of them would admit it.

Chapter 7: Diana Is Invited to Tea

One Saturday in October, when the maples were red and the birches were golden, Marilla had to go to a meeting in Carmody. Before she left, she gave Anne permission to invite Diana to tea.

"You can give her some fruit cake and cherry jam," she said. "And there's a bottle of raspberry cordial on the second shelf of the sitting-room cupboard. You and Diana can have some in the afternoon if you like."

Anne was delighted. She and Diana were going to have tea like grown-up ladies. When Diana arrived in her second-best dress, the two girls shook hands politely and asked about each other's families, as if they had never met before. Then they went out to the orchard to eat apples, and they enjoyed themselves so much that they forgot to behave like ladies.

When they came back, Anne went to the cupboard to look for the raspberry cordial. She found a bottle on the top shelf, poured a large glass for Diana and handed it to her.

"Help yourself, Diana," she said. "I don't think I'll have any just now. I don't feel like it after all those apples."

Diana drank the glass and said it was the nicest raspberry cordial she had ever tasted, much nicer than Mrs Lynde's. So she drank a second glass, and then a third. While she was drinking, Anne talked about a sad story she was reading, and she didn't notice anything strange.

Suddenly Diana stood up, and she didn't look well at all.

"I'm awfully sick," she said in a thick voice. "I must go home right now."

"But you can't go home without your tea!" cried Anne. "Lie down on the sofa for a while, and you'll be better."

"I must go home," repeated Diana stupidly, and there was nothing Anne could say to stop her. Diana walked home with a strange, unsteady step, and Anne cried all the way back to Green Gables.

The next day Mrs Barry came to see Marilla. She was a woman with strong principles and very little sense of humour, and she was extremely angry. Diana had come home drunk, she said. Anne had given her currant wine on purpose.

Marilla hurried to the sitting-room cupboard. There on the shelf was the bottle of raspberry cordial, untouched. The bottle Anne had found was Marilla's own homemade currant wine, which was well known in Avonlea for being very strong.

When she understood what had happened, Marilla could not help laughing. But Mrs Barry did not think it was funny at all. She told Anne that she was a wicked girl and that Diana would never be allowed to play with her again.

"Oh, Marilla," sobbed Anne that evening. "I've lost my bosom friend for ever. Mrs Barry won't believe it was an accident. And Diana and I swore to be faithful to each other until the end of time."

All that winter the two friends could only wave to each other from a distance and exchange sad little notes through the other girls at school. Anne grew thin and quiet, and even Marilla, who was not easily moved, felt sorry for her.

Then, one cold evening in January, Diana came running up the path to Green Gables through the snow. Her face was white with fear. Her mother and father had gone to a political meeting in town, and her little sister, Minnie May, was terribly ill.

"Oh, Anne, please come quickly," she cried. "Minnie May is awfully sick. Young Mary Joe, who's looking after us, says it's croup, and she doesn't know what to do. Father and Mother are away, and there's nobody to go for the doctor."

Anne didn't hesitate for a moment. While Matthew put on his coat and went to fetch the doctor from Carmody, Anne took a bottle of medicine from the kitchen cupboard and ran across the frozen fields with Diana.

"Don't cry, Diana," she said. "I know exactly what to do for croup. You forget that Mrs Hammond had three sets of twins. When you look after three sets of twins, you naturally get a lot of experience with croup."

Minnie May was three years old, and she was lying on the kitchen sofa. She was hot and restless, and her rough breathing could be heard all over the house. Anne went to work at once. She told Mary Joe to put more wood on the fire and boil a large pot of water. Then she gave Minnie May dose after dose of the medicine and sat by her side for hours, never losing her calm.

It was three o'clock in the morning when Matthew arrived with the doctor. By then Minnie May was sleeping peacefully. The danger was over.

When the doctor had heard the whole story, he went to see Mr and Mrs Barry.

"That little red-headed girl they have over at Cuthbert's is as smart as they make them," he said. "I tell you she saved that baby's life, because it would have been too late by the time I got there. She seems to have a skill and presence of mind that are perfectly wonderful in a child of her age."

That afternoon Mrs Barry came to Green Gables with tears in her eyes. She told Anne that she was sorry, that she knew now that the currant wine had been an accident, and that Diana could be her friend again. Anne flew over to Orchard Slope, as the Barrys' farm was called, and the two friends were happily united.

"I'm the happiest girl on Prince Edward Island," Anne told Marilla that night. "And I'll always be extremely careful which bottle I take out of a cupboard."

Chapter 8: Puffed Sleeves and Green Hair

Christmas was coming, and Matthew had noticed something. He had been sitting in the kitchen one evening when a group of girls came in to see Anne. They all wore pretty, bright dresses with large puffed sleeves, which was the fashion that year. Anne stood among them in her plain, dark, straight-sleeved dress, and Matthew realised that she looked different.

Marilla always made Anne's clothes simple and practical. She believed that pretty dresses only encouraged vanity, and she disapproved of puffed sleeves very strongly. So Matthew decided to buy Anne a dress himself.

This was a heroic decision for a man who was afraid of women. He went into a shop in Carmody, but the young lady behind the counter frightened him so much that he lost his head. He left with a garden rake and twenty pounds of brown sugar instead of the cloth he had come for. In the end he asked Mrs Lynde for help, and she was delighted to make the dress for him.

On Christmas morning, Anne came down to the kitchen and found Matthew holding out a dress shyly. It was made of soft brown silk with a pretty skirt, little frills and lace at the neck. And the sleeves were the most beautiful part of all. They had two enormous puffs, divided by rows of brown silk ribbon.

Anne looked at it in silence. Then her eyes filled with tears.

"Why, Anne, don't you like it?" asked Matthew anxiously.

"Like it! Oh, Matthew!" Anne clasped her hands. "Matthew, it's perfectly exquisite. Oh, I can never thank you enough. Look at those sleeves! It seems to me this must be a happy dream."

She wore the dress that same evening to a Christmas concert in the village hall, where she recited a poem. Everyone said she did it beautifully, and Matthew was the proudest man in Avonlea.

The following spring, however, Anne's vanity got her into trouble. A travelling salesman came to the door of Green Gables while Marilla was away. He was selling all kinds of things, and among them was a bottle of hair dye. He promised Anne that it would turn any hair a beautiful, glossy raven black, and that it would never wash out.

Anne could not resist. She had always hated her red hair more than anything in the world. She bought the bottle with her own small savings and used the whole of it that very evening, following the instructions carefully.

When Marilla came home, she found Anne lying on her bed with her face buried in the pillow.

"Anne, what on earth is the matter? Are you sick?"

"Look at my hair, Marilla," whispered Anne, and she sat up slowly.

Marilla lifted the candle and looked. Then she gasped. Anne's hair was green. It was not even a nice green. It was a strange, dull, bronzy green, with streaks of the original red here and there to make it look even worse. Marilla had never seen anything so grotesque in her life.

"Anne Shirley, what have you done to your hair?"

"I dyed it," said Anne miserably. "I bought some dye from a salesman. He said it would make my hair a beautiful raven black. I know it was wicked of me, but I thought it would be worth it to be rid of my red hair. Oh, Marilla, I'm the most unhappy girl on Prince Edward Island."

They washed the hair with soap and water for a whole week, but the green did not come out. Anne stayed at home and refused to see anybody except Diana, who promised to keep the terrible secret. In the end, Marilla took the scissors and cut it all off. There was no other way.

Anne cried when she looked in the mirror afterwards. Her hair was now so short that the boys at school would certainly make fun of her. But then she wiped her eyes and turned the mirror to the wall.

"I'll never look at myself again until my hair grows," she said. "And I'll never try to be beautiful again. Perhaps this is the right punishment for my vanity. It gives me something to think about."

When she went back to school, everyone was amazed by her short hair, and she did not tell anybody the real reason. Josie Pye, who never missed an opportunity to be unkind, said that Anne looked like a perfect scarecrow. But Anne only gave her a proud look and said nothing at all.

"I didn't say anything back," she told Marilla that evening. "I just gave her one scornful look and forgave her. It makes you feel very virtuous when you forgive people, doesn't it? I'm going to devote all my energies to being good after this. But of course, it's easier to be good when your hair is a nice colour."

Chapter 9: An Unfortunate Lily Maid

The years passed quickly at Green Gables, and Anne grew taller every season. Her hair grew back thick and long, and Diana insisted that it was darker than before. Mr Phillips left the school, and a new teacher arrived: Miss Muriel Stacy, a young woman with a warm, bright smile and a wonderful talent for making children want to learn. Anne adored her from the first day. Miss Stacy took her pupils into the woods to study ferns and birds, encouraged them to write their own stories. She also organised a concert at which Anne recited a dramatic poem that made half the audience cry.

There were other adventures too. One night Anne and Diana jumped onto the spare-room bed at Orchard Slope for fun. Unfortunately, they landed on top of Diana's great-aunt Josephine Barry, an old lady from Charlottetown who was visiting unexpectedly. Miss Barry was furious at first. But Anne went to apologise and explained the whole affair so amusingly that the old lady laughed until she cried. After that Miss Barry became one of Anne's most devoted friends, and she invited both girls to stay at her elegant house in town.

In the summer when Anne was thirteen, she and her friends decided to act out a scene from a poem by Tennyson that they had studied at school. It told the story of Elaine, the lily maid, who died of a broken heart and whose body floated down the river to King Arthur's court in a black boat.

"Of course you must be Elaine, Anne," said Ruby Gillis. "I'd be too frightened to float down there alone. I'd die of fright."

"But a red-haired person can't be a lily maid," Anne protested sadly.

"Your hair is ever so much darker than it used to be," said Diana loyally. "And when you lie down, nobody will see it."

So Anne lay down in the bottom of Mr Barry's old flat boat, which was tied at the edge of the pond. The girls covered her with an old black shawl and placed a single blue iris in her folded hands. Then they pushed the boat out into the stream and ran through the woods to meet it at the bridge below, where the river flowed into the pond.

For a few minutes Anne enjoyed the romance of her situation very much. Then she felt something cold and wet under her back. She sat up quickly and saw that water was pouring through a large hole in the bottom of the boat. The boat was sinking, and she was much too far from the bank to reach it.

Anne did not scream, although she was terribly frightened. She remembered that the boat would soon pass under the bridge, close to one of the old wooden piles that supported it. As it drifted past, she grabbed the pile and climbed onto it, and there she stayed, clinging to the slippery wood with her feet just above the water.

The other girls saw the boat drift past the bridge and sink in the middle of the pond. They thought that Anne had sunk with it. Screaming, they ran off through the woods to find help, and they did not even see her.

The minutes passed slowly. Anne's arms began to ache, and she started to wonder how long she could hold on. Then, just when she thought she could not hold on any longer, Gilbert Blythe came rowing under the bridge in Harmon Andrews' little boat.

He looked up and saw her. His surprise was enormous, but he did not laugh at her.

"Anne Shirley! How on earth did you get there?" he exclaimed. Without waiting for an answer, he pulled close to the pile and held out his hand.

Anne had no choice. She took his hand, climbed down into the boat and sat in the back, wet and angry, holding the dripping shawl. It was extremely difficult to be dignified under those circumstances.

"What has happened, Anne?" asked Gilbert, picking up his oars.

"We were playing Elaine," said Anne coldly, without even looking at her rescuer. "I had to drift down to Camelot in the boat. But the boat began to leak, and I climbed out onto the pile. The girls went for help. Will you be kind enough to row me to the landing?"

Gilbert rowed her to the landing, and Anne jumped out without accepting his hand.

"I'm very much obliged to you," she said proudly as she turned away. But Gilbert jumped out of the boat too and put his hand on her arm.

"Anne," he said quickly, "look here. Can't we be good friends? I'm awfully sorry that I made fun of your hair that time. I only meant it as a joke. Besides, it was so long ago. I think your hair is awfully pretty now, honestly I do. Let's be friends."

For a moment Anne hesitated. There was a strange new feeling in her heart, and Gilbert's kind hazel eyes were looking at her in a very pleasant way. But then she remembered that afternoon at school, the word Carrots and the laughter of the whole class. Her old anger was stronger than her new feeling.

"No," she said coldly. "I shall never be friends with you, Gilbert Blythe. And I don't want to be!"

"All right!" Gilbert jumped into his boat with an angry red face. "I'll never ask you to be friends again, Anne Shirley. And I don't care either!"

He rowed away quickly. Anne walked up the path with her head held high, but she felt an odd sort of regret. She almost wished she had answered him differently. Then she met Diana and Jane, who had been running in a panic towards Orchard Slope, and they threw their arms around her and cried with relief.

That evening Anne told the story to Marilla and Matthew with great seriousness.

"I think I'm cured of being romantic, Marilla," she said. "I've made a lot of mistakes since I came to Green Gables, but each one has cured me of something. The amethyst brooch cured me of meddling with things that didn't belong to me. The green hair cured me of vanity. And today's mistake is going to cure me of being too romantic. I've come to the conclusion that it's no use trying to be romantic in Avonlea."

"I hope so," said Marilla doubtfully. But when Anne had gone upstairs, Matthew laid a shy hand on his sister's arm.

"Don't give up all your romance, Anne," he whispered to the empty doorway. "A little of it is a good thing. Not too much, of course. But keep a little of it, Anne, keep a little of it."

Chapter 10: The Queen's Class

That autumn Miss Stacy formed a special class of her best pupils who wanted to study for the entrance examination to Queen's Academy. This was the college in Charlottetown where young people trained to become teachers. Anne joined the class, and so did Diana's friends Jane Andrews, Ruby Gillis and Josie Pye. Gilbert Blythe joined it too. Diana did not, because her parents did not intend to send her to Queen's, and Anne felt as though a part of her own life had been taken away.

For two years the Queen's class worked hard. Anne studied history, English and literature with pleasure, but she suffered terribly over geometry. She and Gilbert were fierce rivals now, but they were silent ones. Gilbert never spoke to her except when it was necessary, and he never showed that he noticed her. Anne told herself that she did not care in the least, although she knew that it was not true.

Meanwhile, Anne was changing. One evening Marilla looked across the kitchen and realised that the thin little orphan who had arrived at Green Gables was gone. In her place was a tall, slender girl of fifteen with serious grey eyes, a thoughtful face and thick, beautifully shining auburn hair. Mrs Lynde, as she had once predicted, could hardly believe her eyes.

"The child has grown up," said Marilla to Matthew, with a strange sadness in her voice. "She's taller than I am, and she doesn't talk half as much as she used to. I miss her chatter, though I never thought I would."

The day of the entrance examination came in June. Anne went to Charlottetown with the others and spent three long days writing papers in a crowded hall. She was confident about English and history, but geometry terrified her, and she came home sure she had failed.

"If I don't pass, I'll never be able to show my face in Avonlea again," she told Diana. "And if Gilbert Blythe comes out ahead of me, I'll feel even worse."

The results were published in the newspaper three weeks later. Anne waited for them in agony. One evening Diana came racing up the hill from the post office, waving the paper over her head.

"Anne, you've passed!" she shouted. "You've passed the very first! You and Gilbert both, you're tied! But your name is first on the list. Oh, I'm so proud of you!"

Anne took the paper with shaking hands. It was true. Out of two hundred students, she and Gilbert shared first place.

At Green Gables, Matthew was so happy that he could hardly speak. "Well now, I always said it," he declared. "I knew you could beat them all easily."

So in September Anne went to Queen's Academy in Charlottetown. She was lonely at first, and on the first evening she sat in her small boarding-house room and cried for Green Gables. But she soon made new friends and threw herself into her studies with all her energy.

Most students needed two years to get their teacher's licence, but Anne decided to do it in one. So did Gilbert. They never spoke to each other at Queen's, but everyone knew about their rivalry. There was also a scholarship called the Avery prize, which would give the best student in English four years at Redmond College, a real university. Anne wanted it more than anything.

At the end of the year Anne won the Avery scholarship, and Gilbert won the gold medal for the best student overall. On the day of the closing ceremony, when Anne read her essay in front of the whole college, Matthew and Marilla sat in the audience. Matthew's eyes were shining, and even Marilla could not hide her pride.

"I guess she's better than any boy, Marilla," whispered Matthew. "I'm glad we kept her."

"It's not the first time you've said so," said Marilla. But she was smiling.

Chapter 11: The Bend in the Road

When Anne came home to Green Gables that summer, she noticed that Matthew looked old and tired. His heart had been giving him trouble for some time. Marilla had been suffering from terrible headaches too, and her eyes were getting worse.

"Matthew, you shouldn't work so hard," Anne said one evening as they walked home together from the fields. "You must rest more."

"Well, I can't," said Matthew, opening the gate. "I'm getting old, Anne, and I keep forgetting it. Well, well, I've always worked hard, and I'd rather fall in harness."

"If I'd been the boy you sent for," said Anne sadly, "I'd be able to help you so much now. I wish I'd been a boy, just for that."

"Well now, I'd rather have you than a dozen boys, Anne," said Matthew, patting her hand. "Just mind you that, rather than a dozen boys. It wasn't a boy that took the Avery scholarship, was it? It was a girl, my girl, my girl that I'm proud of."

He smiled his shy smile at her. Anne kept the memory of that smile all her life, because it was the last time she ever walked with him.

The next morning a newspaper arrived with terrible news. The Abbey Bank, where Matthew and Marilla had kept all their savings, had failed, and their money was lost. Matthew read the newspaper in the hall, and his face went grey. A moment later he fell to the floor. Anne and Marilla ran to him, but it was too late. He died at once, without any pain, and the doctor said that the shock had been too much for his heart.

The whole of Avonlea came to the funeral. Anne could not cry at first. She sat by the window of her room that night and stared out into the darkness. Then she remembered his face at the gate the evening before, and his gentle words, "my girl that I'm proud of." At last the tears came, and she cried as if her heart would break.

Marilla came in quietly and held her.

"There, there, don't cry, dearie," she said. "It can't bring him back. I know I've been hard on you sometimes, Anne. But you mustn't think that I didn't love you as much as Matthew did. It's never been easy for me to say things out of my heart. But I love you as dearly as if you were my own flesh and blood. You've been my joy and comfort ever since you came to Green Gables."

In the weeks after the funeral, Marilla went to see a doctor about her eyes. The news was very bad. If she did not stop reading and sewing completely and rest her eyes, she would be blind in six months. With Matthew gone and the money lost, she could not keep the farm. She decided that she would have to sell Green Gables.

Anne listened to all this in silence. That night she sat alone at her window for a long time, and in the morning she had made a decision.

"You're not going to sell Green Gables," she told Marilla firmly. "I'm not going to Redmond. I'm going to stay here with you."

"Not going to Redmond! But Anne, what about your scholarship? You worked so hard for it."

"I'm going to teach instead. I've applied for the school at Carmody, and I'll come home every weekend. We'll rent the fields to Mr Barry, and I'll read to you in the evenings and keep you cheerful. You won't be left here alone, Marilla. I couldn't bear it."

Marilla protested. She said she could not allow Anne to give up everything for her sake. But Anne only laughed.

"I'm not giving up anything," she said. "Nothing could be worse than giving up Green Gables. I've thought it all out. I can study at home and take college courses by myself. Oh, I have dozens of plans, Marilla. When I left Queen's, my future seemed to stretch out before me like a straight road. Now there's a bend in it. I don't know what lies around the bend, but I'm going to believe that the best does."

When Mrs Lynde came to visit, she brought more surprising news. Gilbert Blythe had been offered the job of teacher at the Avonlea school. But when he heard about Anne's decision, he had asked the school trustees to give it to Anne instead. That way she could live at home with Marilla every day. He had taken the school at White Sands, where he would have to pay for his board and lodging.

Anne was deeply moved. She knew how much Gilbert needed to save money for his own university studies.

The next evening she went to put flowers on Matthew's grave, and on the way home she met Gilbert coming out of the Blythes' gate. He stopped when he saw her and raised his cap politely, and he would have passed by in silence if Anne had not stopped him and held out her hand.

"Gilbert," she said, and her cheeks were pink, "I want to thank you for giving up the school for me. It was very good of you, and I want you to know that I appreciate it."

Gilbert took her hand eagerly. "It wasn't particularly good of me at all, Anne. I was pleased to be able to do you some small service. Are we going to be friends after this? Have you really forgiven me for that old mistake?"

Anne laughed and tried to take her hand away, but he didn't let go.

"I forgave you that day by the pond, although I didn't know it," she said. "What a stubborn little goose I was. I've been sorry ever since."

"We're going to be the best of friends," said Gilbert happily. "We were born to be good friends, Anne. You've been fighting against destiny for long enough. I know we can help each other in many ways. You're going to keep up your studies, aren't you? So am I. Come on, I'm going to walk home with you."

Marilla looked at Anne curiously when she came into the kitchen half an hour later.

"Who was that who came up the lane with you, Anne?"

"Gilbert Blythe," said Anne, annoyed to find herself blushing. "We've decided to be friends. We were talking at the gate for half an hour."

"I didn't think you and Gilbert Blythe were such good friends that you'd stand for half an hour at the gate talking to him," said Marilla, with a dry smile.

"We haven't been, but we're going to be now," said Anne. "And there were five years of lost conversation to make up for."

That night Anne sat for a long time at her window. The wind was purring softly in the cherry branches, and the smell of mint came up to her from the garden. The stars were shining over the pointed firs in the hollow, and Diana's light was gleaming through the gap in the trees, just as it had done in the old days.

Anne's horizon had become narrower since the night she had come home from Queen's. But she knew that flowers of quiet happiness would bloom along her road. The joys of honest work, worthy ambition and true friendship were hers, and nothing could take away her gift for dreams.

"God's in His heaven, all's right with the world," whispered Anne softly.

The End
