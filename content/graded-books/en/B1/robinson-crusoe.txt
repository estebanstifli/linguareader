Chapter 1: A Boy Who Wanted the Sea

I was born in the year 1632, in the city of York, in the north of England. My father was a merchant from Germany who had come to England and done well. My mother came from a good English family called Robinson, so they gave me the name Robinson Crusoe.

I was the third son, and my father wanted me to study law, but I had only one thought in my head. I wanted to go to sea, and I spent hours at the window, dreaming of ships, storms and strange countries.

One morning my father called me into his room. He was old and ill, and he spoke to me very seriously.

"Robinson," he said, "why do you want to leave your home and your country? You have everything here. You will never be rich, but you will never be poor. The middle way of life is the happiest one. Kings envy it, and poor men dream of it."

"But, Father, I want to see the world," I said.

"If you go to sea, you will be sorry," he answered, and tears ran down his face. "And one day, when there is nobody to help you, you will remember my words."

I was moved, and for a few days I promised myself that I would stay. But the feeling did not last. A year later, on the first of September 1651, I was in the port of Hull. A friend of mine was sailing to London on his father's ship. "Come with us," he said. "It will cost you nothing." I did not ask my parents or even send them a letter, and I simply got on the ship.

As soon as we left the river, the wind began to blow and the sea rose. I had never been on a ship before, and I was terribly sick and terribly afraid. Every wave seemed ready to swallow us. "If God lets me live," I said to myself, "I will go straight home to my father and never leave him again."

The next day the wind dropped, the sun came out, and the sea was as smooth as glass. My friend laughed at me. "Were you frightened by that little bit of wind?" he asked. We drank a bowl of punch together, and I forgot all my promises.

A few days later, near the town of Yarmouth, a real storm came. This time even the sailors were pale, because the ship began to fill with water, and the captain fired guns to ask for help. At last a small boat from another ship came close, and we jumped into it. We were hardly a few minutes away when our ship went down. I watched it sink, and my heart was like a stone.

When we reached the land, my friend's father spoke to me. "Young man," he said, "you should never go to sea again. This is a sign from heaven. Go home." I did not listen, because I was ashamed to go home and I thought that people would laugh at me, so I went to London instead.

In London I met the captain of a ship that was sailing to Guinea, on the coast of Africa. He was an honest man who liked me, and I went with him, taking some cheap goods to sell. On that voyage I learned a little about the sea, and I came back with nearly three hundred pounds in gold. I thought that I would soon be a rich man.

Sadly, my good captain died soon after we returned. I decided to make the same voyage again on the same ship. That was the worst decision of my young life.

Near the Canary Islands, a pirate ship from the port of Sallee, in North Africa, began to follow us. We fought for hours, but they were too many for us, and after they had killed three of our men, the rest of us were taken prisoner. I was given as a slave to the pirate captain himself.

For two years I worked in his house and his garden, and although I was not beaten, I was not free, so every night I thought about escape. My master often went fishing in a small boat, and he took me with him, because I was good at catching fish. With us came a Moorish boy called Xury and an older man.

One day the master sent the three of us out alone to catch fish for his guests. He had put food, water and some guns in the boat. This was my chance, and when we were far from the shore, I came up behind the older man and pushed him into the sea. He was a strong swimmer, and I did not want to kill him. "Swim back to the land," I called. "I will not hurt you if you leave us alone." He swam away towards the shore.

Then I turned to the boy. "Xury," I said, "will you come with me and be faithful? If you will, I will be good to you." He smiled and promised. So we sailed away to the south, along the coast of Africa.

Chapter 2: The Shipwreck

Xury and I travelled for many days along that wild coast. At night we heard terrible animals roaring on the shore, and we did not dare to land. When we needed water, Xury bravely went to look for it while I watched with the gun. Once I shot a huge lion that was sleeping on the beach. Another time we met some local people who were kind to us. They brought us meat and water, and we gave them the skin of a leopard that I had killed.

At last, near the Cape Verde Islands, a Portuguese ship saw our little boat and picked us up. The captain was a generous man who bought my boat from me and gave me a fair price for everything. He also wanted Xury to work on his ship. I did not like the idea, but Xury agreed, and the captain promised to set him free after ten years. I have often thought since then that I should never have let him go. He had been a true friend to me.

The ship took me to Brazil, where I bought some land and began to grow sugar and tobacco. I worked hard, and after four years my plantation was doing well, and I had neighbours, friends and a good future. My father would have said that I had found the middle way of life at last.

But I was not satisfied. Some of the other planters wanted to send a ship to Africa to bring back enslaved people to work on their land. They asked me to go with the ship. I am ashamed to write it now, but I said yes. I wanted to become rich more quickly. I believe that heaven was already watching.

We sailed on the first of September 1659, exactly eight years after I had run away from home. For twelve days the weather was fine. Then a terrible hurricane came out of the south-east. For twelve days the wind drove us where it wanted, and we could do nothing. One man died of fever, and two more were washed into the sea.

On the morning of the thirteenth day, one of the sailors shouted, "Land!" At almost the same moment, the ship hit a sandbank and stopped. The sea broke over us so violently that we thought we would all die in minutes.

We had one small boat left. The eleven of us climbed into it and rowed towards the land. But it was not a safe beach. It was a coast of rocks, and the waves were as high as mountains. About a mile from the shore, a huge wave came behind us. It turned the boat over, and in a moment we were all in the water.

I cannot describe what I felt. The wave carried me a long way towards the shore. Then it went back and left me on the sand, half dead, with my mouth full of sea water. I stood up and ran, but another wave, higher than a house, came after me. It buried me deep under the water. I held my breath and let it carry me. When my head came out, I took a breath, and then the water covered me again.

The last wave threw me against a rock so hard that I nearly lost my senses. I held on to the rock with all my strength until the water went back. Then I ran again, climbed up the rocks and threw myself down on the grass, safe at last.

I looked up at the sky and thanked God that I was alive. Then I looked around for my companions. There was nobody. I never saw any of them again, except three of their hats, one cap and two shoes that were not a pair.

When I looked out to sea, the ship was lying so far away that I could hardly see it. "How was it possible for me to reach the shore?" I asked myself.

Then a new fear came over me. I was wet, I had no other clothes, and I had nothing to eat or drink. I had no weapon except a knife, a pipe and a little tobacco in a box. Night was coming, and I did not know what wild animals lived in this place.

I walked a little way from the beach and found some fresh water. I drank it gratefully, and then I put a little tobacco in my mouth to stop my hunger. I cut a short, thick stick for my protection. Then I climbed into a large tree with thick branches and made myself as comfortable as I could. I was so tired that I fell asleep immediately, and I slept better than I have ever slept in a bed.

Chapter 3: Treasures from the Ship

When I woke up, it was broad day, the storm had ended, and the sea was calm. To my great surprise, I saw that the ship had been lifted off the sandbank by the high tide in the night. It now lay only about a mile from the shore, still standing upright.

I wished with all my heart that we had stayed on board. If we had stayed, we would all be alive. That thought made me cry, but crying would not feed me, so I decided to go out to the ship.

When the tide was low, I took off most of my clothes and swam to it. The difficult part was climbing up, because the sides were high and smooth. At last I saw a rope hanging down, and I pulled myself up. The ship was full of water at the bottom, but the back part was dry, and so was the food. I was very hungry, so I filled my pockets with biscuits and ate them as I walked around. I also found some rum and drank a little to give me strength.

Now I needed a boat to carry things to the shore. There were several pieces of wood on the ship, and some spare masts. I threw them into the sea, tied them together with ropes and made a raft. It was strong enough to carry me and quite a lot of goods.

First I opened three sailors' boxes and filled them with bread, rice, three Dutch cheeses, some dried goat's meat and a little corn. While I was working, the tide began to rise. My coat, shirt and jacket, which I had left on the sand, floated away. So I looked for clothes on the ship and found plenty. But there were things I needed far more. I found the carpenter's box of tools, which was worth more to me than a ship full of gold. I also found two good guns, two pistols, some powder and a bag of shot.

With great difficulty, I steered the heavy raft towards a small river. Once it nearly turned over and threw everything into the water. But at last I brought it into a little bay and waited for the tide to go down. Then I carried all my goods onto the land.

Next I wanted to know where I was. I took a gun and climbed a hill not far away, and from the top I saw my fate clearly: I was on an island. There was no other land near, except some rocks and two small islands far to the west. I saw no houses, no smoke and no people. There were many birds, and I shot one, but its meat was bad.

That night I built a little wall of boxes and boards around me and slept on the beach, and although I was afraid, nothing came near me.

For the next thirteen days I went back to the ship again and again, and each time I brought something useful. I brought sails, ropes, nails, a grindstone, iron bars, barrels of powder, more guns, blankets, mattresses and many other things. I made a tent from the sails to keep my goods dry. Inside it I put my bed, and I slept with two pistols beside my head and a gun next to me.

On my last visit, I found a drawer with some money in it: gold and silver coins from Europe and Brazil. I laughed out loud.

"Oh, money!" I said. "What use are you to me now? You are not worth picking up from the floor. One of these knives is worth more than all of you." But after a moment I changed my mind and took the money with me anyway.

While I was on the ship, the sky grew dark and the wind began to rise. I quickly swam back to the shore. The storm blew all that night, and in the morning, when I looked out to sea, the ship had gone. I was not too sad, because I had taken almost everything that was useful. Now I was truly alone.

My next task was to find a safe place to live. I did not want to stay on the beach, where wild animals or men might find me. After searching for some time, I found a small flat area of grass on the side of a hill. Behind it, the rock went straight up like the wall of a house, and there was a hollow in it, like the door of a cave. The place looked out over the sea, so I could see any ship that passed. It was in the shade in the afternoon, and there was fresh water nearby.

Here I decided to build my home.

Chapter 4: A Home, a Calendar and a Journal

Before I put up my tent, I drew a half circle on the grass in front of the hollow in the rock. Along this line I hammered two rows of strong wooden posts deep into the ground, so close together that not even a small animal could get through. It was terribly hard work. I had to cut the trees in the woods, carry them back on my shoulders and push them into the earth one by one. Some days I managed only one post. When the fence was finished, I made no door in it. Instead, I used a short ladder to climb over the top, and I pulled the ladder in after me. Inside, I felt as safe as a king in his castle.

Behind the tent I began to dig into the rock. The stone was soft, and with my iron tools I slowly made a cave. I used the earth and stones that I took out to raise the ground inside the fence. This cave became my cellar, where I kept my powder, my food and my most precious tools.

One day, while I was working, a sudden storm broke over the island. A flash of lightning lit up the sky, and immediately a terrible thought came into my head. "My powder!" I cried. If the lightning hit it, all my powder could explode in a single moment, and with it my only way of hunting and defending myself. So I spent the next two weeks making small bags and boxes. I divided the powder into a hundred little parcels and hid them in different holes in the rocks, far away from each other.

After about ten or twelve days on the island, I realised that I would soon lose all sense of time. I would forget the days of the week, and even forget which day was Sunday. So I found a large post and cut these words into it with my knife: "I came on shore here on the 30th of September, 1659." I put the post in the ground at the place where I had first landed. Every day I cut a small mark on its side. Every seventh mark was longer than the others, and on the first day of each month I cut a mark that was longer still. This was my calendar.

Among the things that I had brought from the ship were pens, ink and paper, so I decided to keep a journal. At first I wrote about my troubles, and the pages were full of tears. I was a prisoner, and the sea was my prison wall. Then one evening I sat down and tried to look at my situation honestly, like a merchant who checks his accounts. I divided the page into two columns. On one side I wrote the evil, and on the other side the good.

"I am alone on a terrible island, with no hope of rescue," I wrote on the evil side. On the good side I wrote, "But I am alive, and all my companions are drowned."

"I am separated from the whole world," I wrote. "But I am not starving, and I have food and water."

"I have no clothes," I wrote. "But the climate is so hot that I hardly need any."

"I have nobody to speak to," I wrote. "But God sent the ship near enough to the shore for me to take all these useful things."

When I read the two columns, I understood something important. There is no situation so miserable that there is nothing good in it. From that day I tried to stop complaining and to make my life as comfortable as I could.

Here are a few entries from my journal of those first months, copied exactly as I wrote them.

October 1. In the morning I was surprised to see that the ship had floated nearer to the shore with the high tide. This gave me some comfort, but it also renewed my grief for my lost companions, who would certainly have survived if we had remained on board.

November 4. This morning I began to organise my time. In the early morning I go out with my gun for two or three hours, unless it is raining. Then I work until about eleven o'clock, and afterwards I eat whatever provisions I have. From twelve until two I lie down and sleep, because the temperature is extremely high. In the evening I return to work.

November 17. Today I began to dig my cave deeper into the rock. I discovered that I needed three essential instruments: a pickaxe, a shovel and a wheelbarrow. I used the iron bars instead of a pickaxe, although they were rather heavy. A shovel was more difficult. Eventually I made one from an extremely hard type of wood, which the Brazilians call ironwood. It took me four days, and it was not a beautiful object, but it was useful.

December 10. I thought my cave was finished, when suddenly a great quantity of earth fell down from the roof. If I had been standing underneath it, I would never have needed a gravedigger. I spent many days repairing the damage, and I put up wooden posts to support the ceiling.

After a time my ink began to run out, and I wrote only the most important events. But I continued the journal for many years.

I was not completely alone, in fact. The ship's dog had swum ashore after me, and he was a loyal friend for many years. He could not talk, but he followed me everywhere and slept at my feet. Two of the ship's cats had come too, and later they had so many kittens that I had to chase most of them away.

I soon discovered that there were wild goats on the island. They were very shy and ran away as soon as they saw me, so they were difficult to hunt. However, I noticed that when they were up on the rocks, they did not look down at me. If I came from above, they did not see me. After that I shot several, and their meat kept me alive. Once I killed a mother goat, and her little kid followed me home. I tried to tame it, but it would not eat, and in the end I had to kill it too. It made me sad, and I began to think about catching living goats instead of shooting them.

Little by little I became a carpenter. I had never used tools before, but I learned that a man can do almost anything if he has patience. I made a table and a chair, and I put up shelves in the cave for my tools, my nails and my iron. Making a single board took me days. I had to cut down a whole tree and then chop away at both sides until it was flat and thin enough. It was a slow and wasteful method, but I had nothing except time.

At night I needed light. I kept the fat of the goats that I killed, and in a little dish of clay I made a lamp with a piece of string. It did not give much light, but it was better than darkness.

Chapter 5: The Earthquake and the Fever

One day, while I was looking through my things, I found a small bag. It had once held corn for the chickens on the ship, but now there was nothing in it except dust and the rats had eaten everything else. I needed the bag, so I shook the dust out onto the ground beside my fence, and I forgot about it.

About a month later I noticed some green plants growing in that place. I thought they were some kind of local grass. But a few weeks afterwards, I was astonished to see ears of English barley among them, and a few stalks of rice. At first I believed that God had made them grow by a miracle, just for me, and tears came into my eyes. Then I remembered the bag of chicken food, and my religious feelings disappeared almost as quickly as they had come. I am ashamed of that now. It was still a kind of miracle that the rats had left those few seeds, and that I had thrown them into a shady place where they could grow. If I had thrown them anywhere else, the sun would have burned them.

I kept every grain carefully. It took me four years before I had enough barley and rice to make bread, but I never again had to live without it.

On the 17th of April, I had a terrible fright. I was working at the entrance to my cave when suddenly the earth and the rock above me began to move. Great stones fell from the top of the hill, and the ground shook so violently that I thought the whole world was ending. I was so frightened that I ran out of the fence and stood in the open, feeling sick. The sea itself seemed to rise and boil. After the third shock, everything became calm again, and then a heavy storm of wind and rain followed.

It was an earthquake. From then on I was afraid to sleep under the rock, in case another shock buried me alive. For a while I planned to build a new home in the open, but it would have taken months, and I was not brave enough to leave my safe fence. In the end I stayed.

A few days later, the storm had thrown the remains of the ship much closer to the shore. I spent weeks breaking it up and carrying away wood, iron and lead. It was hard work, and I think it made my body weak. Soon afterwards, I became very ill.

I had a high fever. For days I shook with cold and then burned with heat. I could not stand up or go out for water. I lay alone in my tent with a terrible pain in my head, and I was sure that I would die there, with nobody to help me. During the fever I had a frightening dream. I saw a man coming down from a black cloud, surrounded by fire. He came towards me with a spear in his hand and said, "Since all these things have not brought you to repentance, now you shall die." I woke up shouting.

When the fever let me go for a few hours, I lay thinking about my life. I remembered my father's tears and his words: "One day, when there is nobody to help you, you will remember my words." Now his words had come true. I had never once thanked God for anything. I had lived like an animal, thinking only about food and comfort.

Then I remembered something. The Brazilians often used tobacco as a medicine, and I had a roll of it in one of the sailors' boxes. When I opened the box, I also found a Bible that I had taken from the ship and never opened. I took both out. I chewed some tobacco, then I soaked some more in rum and drank it, although it nearly choked me. Before I went to sleep, I opened the Bible, and the first words that I read were these: "Call on me in the day of trouble, and I will deliver you."

That night, for the first time in my life, I prayed. Then I slept so deeply that I believe I did not wake up for a day and a night, because when I checked my calendar later, I had lost a day.

When I woke up, the fever had gone. I was weak, but I was hungry, and I knew that I would live. From that time I read the Bible every morning and every evening. I began to feel that I was not a prisoner, but a man who had been given a second chance. My island was the same island, but I looked at it with different eyes.

When I was strong again, I decided to explore the island more completely. I followed the little river inland. After a few miles, I came to a beautiful green valley full of flowers, with melons growing on the ground and grapes hanging from the trees. Further on I found lemons, oranges and sugar canes. The water was fresh and cool, and the grass was soft. It looked like a garden that somebody had planted.

I stayed there that night, sleeping in a tree. In the morning I walked through the valley and felt a secret pleasure. This was all mine. I was the king and lord of the whole country, and nobody could take it from me.

I carried home as many grapes as I could. I hung the rest in the sun to dry, and they became excellent raisins, which I later ate through the whole of the rainy season. I liked the valley so much that I built a second little home there, with a fence around it, a shelter of branches and a bed. I called it my country house. From then on I often spent a few nights there, especially in the dry season.

On my way back one day, I caught a young parrot. I knocked it down with a stick, carried it home and taught it to talk. It took me years, but in the end it learned to say my name.

Chapter 6: The Great Canoe

The years passed. I divided my time between my work, my prayers and my walks. I learned that the island had two rainy seasons and two dry seasons every year, and I planned my farming according to them. The first time I planted my barley and rice, I did it in the dry season, and almost nothing grew. After that mistake I always planted just before the rains, and the harvest was good.

Making bread was a long adventure. I had no plough, so I dug the soil with a heavy wooden spade. When the barley was growing, the goats and the hares came to eat it, so I had to build a fence around the field. When it was nearly ripe, the birds came in hundreds. I shot three of them and hung them up on posts, like criminals, as a warning to the others. It worked. Not a single bird came near my field again.

Then I needed something to cut the barley, something to grind it, an oven to bake it, and pots to keep it in. I made a sickle from one of the swords that I had brought from the ship. I made a kind of stone bowl to crush the grain. I found clay near the river and tried to make pots, but for months they broke or fell apart. Many were so ugly that I laughed at them. One day I found that a broken piece of pot had become hard and red in my fire. So I learned to burn my pots in a hot fire, and at last I had pots that could hold water and stand over the flames. I have never been so happy about anything as small as an earthen pot.

All these tasks took a long time. When I think about it now, I am surprised at my own patience. A loaf of bread, which in England a man can buy for a penny, cost me almost a year of work.

All the time, I dreamed of the land that I could see on clear days, far away to the west. "Perhaps it is part of America," I thought. "Perhaps there are Spanish or Portuguese settlements there. If I had a boat, I could escape." I went to look at the ship's boat, which still lay on the beach, upside down. But it was far too heavy for one man to turn over, and after weeks of work I gave up.

Then I had another idea. The people of America make canoes from a single tree, so why could I not do the same? I found a magnificent cedar tree in the woods and began to work. It took me twenty days to cut it down and another fourteen to remove the branches. Then I spent nearly three months shaping the outside and hollowing out the inside with my hammer and chisel. When I had finished, it was a beautiful canoe, big enough for twenty-six men.

There was only one problem, and I had never once thought about it. The canoe was a hundred yards from the water, and I could not move it even an inch. I tried to dig a canal from the sea to the boat, but I calculated that the work would take ten or twelve years. At last, with great sadness, I gave up the project. I had learned a painful lesson. It is foolish to begin a piece of work before you have counted the cost and measured your strength.

Later I made a second, smaller canoe, this time close to a little stream so that I could float it down to the sea. I put up a mast and a sail made from a piece of the ship's sails, and I made an umbrella out of goatskin to protect me from the sun. I had already made a hat, a jacket and trousers out of goatskin, too. If any Englishman had met me at that time, dressed in skins, with a gun on my shoulder and an umbrella over my head, he would have run away or died laughing.

In the sixth year of my reign, as I liked to call it, I decided to sail around the island. Everything went well at first. But on the east side there was a long line of rocks going out into the sea, and beyond them I found a powerful current. It caught my little boat and began to carry me out to the open sea, away from the island. I rowed with all my strength, but it was useless. I thought I was lost.

How differently I saw my island now! I looked back at it and it seemed like paradise. "Oh, happy desert!" I cried. "I shall never see you again!" I would have given anything to be back in my lonely home.

Then, near evening, a light wind rose from the south-east. I put up my sail and steered towards the edge of the current. At last the boat came out of it into calmer water, and after many hours I reached the shore. I fell on my knees and thanked God. I left the canoe in a little bay and walked home across the island, exhausted.

I went to my country house and fell asleep. Suddenly, a voice woke me. "Robin, Robin, Robin Crusoe! Poor Robin Crusoe! Where are you, Robin Crusoe? Where have you been?"

I jumped up in terror. Then I saw my parrot, Poll, sitting on the fence. It had flown there from my other home and was repeating the words that I had taught it so many times. I laughed until I cried, and Poll came and sat on my thumb as if nothing strange had happened.

After this adventure I did not try to sail around the island again for a long time. Instead I worked to make my life safer. My powder was getting low, so I decided to catch goats alive. I dug pits in places where they walked, covered them with branches and put barley on top. Soon I had a few young goats, and I built a field with a strong hedge around it. Within a year or two I had a whole flock. I had milk, butter and cheese, and meat whenever I wanted it, without firing a single shot.

Sometimes I sat down to dinner like a king. Poll was my favourite, the only one who could speak to me. My old dog sat on my right, and two cats sat on either side of the table, waiting for a piece of meat from my hand. I had a whole royal family.

In those quiet years I often reflected on the strange contradictions of my situation. In England I had been a restless young man, always dissatisfied, always imagining that happiness was waiting somewhere else. Now I lived on a solitary island, with no society and no possessions except the ones I had created with my own hands. Yet here I discovered a peaceful kind of contentment that I had never experienced before. I had no enemies, no ambitions and no temptations. There was nobody to envy and nobody to impress. My only conversations were with God, with my animals and with myself, and I gradually realised that these conversations were more valuable than all the gossip of London.

Chapter 7: The Footprint

I had been on the island for about fifteen years. I was used to my life now, and I was almost content. Then, one day around noon, everything changed.

I was walking along the beach towards my canoe when I suddenly stopped. There, in the sand, was the print of a man's naked foot. It was very clear: the toes, the heel, every part of it. I stood as if I had been struck by lightning. I listened and looked around, but I saw nothing and heard nothing. I went up a small hill to look further, and then down to the sea, but there was only that single footprint. There were no other marks around it, and I could not understand how it had got there.

I ran home like a frightened rabbit. Every bush and every tree seemed to be a man. I looked behind me every few steps. When I reached my fence, I did not even remember whether I had climbed over it with the ladder or come in through the hole in the rock. I did not sleep that night. My head was full of terrible ideas. Had the devil made the print? Were there savages on the island? I had read that the people on the mainland sometimes came across the sea in canoes. Perhaps they had seen my boat and would come back with others to find me and eat me.

For three days I did not leave my castle. Then I became so hungry, and my goats needed milking so badly, that I had to go out. Little by little I grew a bit braver. I even went back to measure the footprint with my own foot. It was much bigger than mine. So it was not my own, as I had half hoped. Someone had really been there.

How strange human nature is! For fifteen years I had prayed for company and dreamed of seeing another human face. Now the mere sign of a man made me shake with fear.

After that day, I made my home much stronger. I built a second wall outside the first one, and I planted thousands of young trees in front of it. In a few years they became a thick wood, so that no one could see that a man lived there. I placed seven guns in the wall, ready to fire. I divided my goats into small groups and hid them in different valleys, so that a stranger would not find all of them together.

Two years later, I made a horrible discovery. I was exploring the west side of the island, which I rarely visited, when I came down to a beach. The sand was covered with human bones, skulls, hands and feet. There was a circle of earth where a fire had been made. I understood at once. The savages from the mainland came to this beach to hold their terrible feasts. They brought their prisoners and killed them and ate them here.

I turned away and was sick. Then I ran home and thanked God that I had been born in a different part of the world. I also thanked Him that I had lived on this island for so many years without meeting these people. Now I understood why I had never seen them: they always came to the far side, where I seldom went.

For a long time I was full of anger. I made plans to hide near the beach with all my guns and attack them the next time they came. I went up the hill every morning for two or three months to look for canoes, but I never saw any. During those days I had time to think more calmly. "Who made me their judge?" I asked myself. "These people have done nothing to me. In their own eyes, perhaps, they are doing nothing wrong. It is the custom of their nation. If I attack them, I am no better than the Spanish soldiers who killed thousands of people in America." So I decided not to attack them, unless they attacked me first.

I became very careful. I stopped using my gun, in case the noise was heard. I was afraid of making fires, because the smoke could be seen from far away. So I learned to make charcoal, which burns without smoke, the way people do in England.

While I was cutting wood for the charcoal, I found a hidden cave behind some thick bushes. Its entrance was so low that I had to crawl inside. Suddenly I saw two shining eyes in the dark. I jumped back in fear. Then I took a burning stick and went in again. On the floor lay a very old goat, dying of old age. I laughed at myself. I left the poor animal in peace, and when it died I buried it. The cave was dry and large, and I decided that it would be my secret hiding place if the savages ever came looking for me.

Chapter 8: A Man Called Friday

In the twenty-third year of my residence on the island, early one December morning, I saw the light of a fire on the shore, about two miles from my castle. It was not on the far side of the island this time, but on my side. I hurried home, pulled up my ladder and loaded all my guns. Then I climbed carefully to the top of the hill with my telescope and lay flat on the ground.

There were nine naked men sitting around a small fire. Their canoes lay on the beach. They were dancing and celebrating, and I did not need to ask myself what kind of meal they were preparing. I stayed hidden, trembling, until the tide turned and they paddled away. When they had gone, I went down to the beach. The horrible remains of their feast lay on the sand, and my anger returned. But I still kept my promise not to attack them without reason.

A year later, on a stormy night, I heard the sound of a gun from the sea. A ship was in trouble. I lit a great fire on the hill so that the sailors would see it, and I heard several more shots. In the morning I saw the wreck of a ship on the rocks to the south. It was a Spanish ship. I took my canoe out to it, but not a single person was alive on board. There was only a dog, which was so happy to see me that it jumped straight into my boat. I brought back some useful things: bottles of wine, shirts, handkerchiefs, a few pots and a chest of money. But I would have given all of it to have one living person to talk to.

After this, my desire to escape became stronger than ever. One night I had a dream. I saw eleven canoes arriving with prisoners, and one of the prisoners escaped and ran towards my wood to hide. In my dream I showed myself to him and helped him, and he became my companion. "Now," I thought in my dream, "I may venture to the mainland, because this man will be my guide." Then I woke up and was terribly disappointed.

Eighteen months later, my dream came true in a way.

One morning I saw five canoes on my side of the island. There were at least thirty men, and they had already lit a fire. Through my telescope I watched them drag two prisoners out of the boats. They struck one of them on the head with a wooden club, and he fell. The other prisoner stood waiting for his turn. Suddenly, seeing that the guards were busy, he started to run. He ran along the sand with incredible speed, straight towards my part of the island.

Three men ran after him. Between them and my castle there was a small river, and the escaping prisoner swam across it in about thirty strokes. Two of the men followed him into the water, but the third could not swim and went back.

This was my chance, and I took it without hesitation. I ran down the hill with two guns and placed myself between the prisoner and the men who were chasing him. I shouted to the prisoner. He looked at me in terror, more frightened of me than of his enemies. I knocked the first man down with the end of my gun. The second man raised his bow to shoot an arrow at me, so I had to fire. He fell dead.

The poor prisoner stood still, shaking. He had never heard a gun before. I made signs for him to come to me, and he came slowly, stopping every few steps. At last he knelt down in front of me, kissed the ground, and placed my foot on his head. I understood that this was a sign of thanks, but I did not like it. I lifted him up and smiled at him, trying to show him that he was not my servant.

Then the man whom I had knocked down began to recover. My new companion made signs that he wanted my sword. I gave it to him, and before I could stop him, he killed his enemy with a single blow. Afterwards he buried both bodies in the sand, so that the others would not find them.

I took him to my secret cave, gave him bread, raisins and water, and showed him a bed of straw. He lay down and fell asleep immediately.

While he was sleeping, I looked at him carefully. He was a tall, handsome young man of about twenty-six, strong and well built, with a kind and intelligent face. His hair was long and black, and his skin was a deep olive brown. His teeth were as white as ivory.

When he woke up, I tried to ask him his name. He told me, but it was long and full of sounds that my English tongue could not repeat. He laughed at my attempts. In the end I told him that I would call him Friday, because I had met him on a Friday. He repeated the word happily, as if it were a good joke. Then I pointed to my chest and said, "Robinson." He said it perfectly the first time, and I realised that this was the first time in twenty-five years that another human being had spoken my name.

Chapter 9: Two Teachers

I had always imagined that, if I ever found a companion, I would teach him everything. I did not expect that he would teach me just as much.

Friday learned English with astonishing speed. Within a few weeks he understood most of what I said, and within a few months we could talk about almost anything. In return, he taught me words in his own language. He also showed me how much I still did not know about the island where I had lived for a quarter of a century. He knew which plants could heal a wound and which roots were good to eat. He could catch fish with his bare hands, and he could move through the woods so silently that the goats never heard him coming.

One day I took him out to show him my gun. I shot a young goat, and he jumped and shook all over. He tore open his own shirt to see if he was wounded. He thought that the gun could kill anyone, anywhere, just by being pointed at them. For days he would not touch it, and when I was not looking, I saw him talking to it seriously, asking it politely not to kill him. Later I showed him how it worked, and he became an excellent shot.

I made him a jacket and trousers of goatskin and a cap of hare's skin, and I built him a little hut between my two walls. At first I took the ladder inside with me at night, and I kept the guns beside my bed. I was ashamed of these fears very soon. Friday was the most honest and loyal friend that any man could wish for. He would have given his life for me, and, as I discovered later, so would I for him.

We spoke about everything. I told him about England, about ships and cities, about horses and books. He told me about his nation and his family, especially his father, whom he loved deeply. He explained that his people lived on the large land that I could see to the west, and that the savage feasts were part of their wars. Prisoners taken in battle were eaten by the winners. "My people do this," he said honestly. "Your people do not eat prisoners. But do they not kill them?" I had no good answer.

I told him about God, and he listened carefully. Then he asked me questions that I found very difficult. "If God is so strong," he said, "why does he not kill the devil, so that people stop doing bad things?" I thought for a long time before answering, and even then I was not sure that my answer was good. I realised that I had been a careless Christian, and that this young man, who had never seen a Bible, could teach me to think more deeply about my own beliefs.

He also asked me why I had not tried to leave the island. One day, standing on the hill, he looked towards the west and suddenly began to jump and dance. "Oh, joy!" he cried. "There is my country! There are my people!"

His face shone with such happiness that I felt a little jealous. "Friday," I asked, "would you like to go back there?"

"Yes," he answered. "I would be very glad to be in my own country."

"And would you become a savage again and eat men?" I asked.

He shook his head seriously. "No. Friday would tell them to live good, to eat bread and goat's milk, not men."

"But they would kill you," I said.

"No," he said. "They would not kill me. They would love to learn."

Then he told me something that made my heart beat faster. On his land, he said, there were seventeen white men with beards who had come in a broken ship. They lived safely with his people. I realised that they must be Spanish or Portuguese sailors, perhaps from the ship that I had found on the rocks.

"Will you come with me?" Friday asked.

"How could I go?" I said. "They would eat me."

"No," he replied firmly. "Friday would tell them you saved his life. They would love you."

From then on, we worked together on a plan. We found a great tree near the water and made a canoe from it, which took us about a month. Friday was far better than me at choosing the wood and at shaping a boat. This time I had learned my lesson: we built it close enough to the water to launch it. I made a mast and a sail, and I taught Friday how to steer. We planned to leave when the rainy season ended.

Chapter 10: The Rescue

I was now in the twenty-seventh year of my life on the island. One morning I sent Friday down to the beach to look for a turtle. He came running back a few minutes later as if he were flying, his face full of fear.

"Oh, Robinson!" he cried. "Bad! Over there, one, two, three canoes!"

I ran up the hill with my telescope. There were twenty-one men on the beach with three prisoners, and they had come to celebrate another feast. I asked Friday if he would fight beside me.

"I will die if you tell me to die," he said.

"I do not want you to die," I replied. "I want us both to live."

I gave him a pistol, three guns and a hatchet, and I took the rest. We went silently through the wood until we came to the edge of the trees, very close to the beach. Through the leaves I saw that one of the prisoners was a white man, with a beard, lying tied up on the sand. Two of the savages were already bending down to untie his feet.

There was no time to lose. "Now, Friday," I whispered. "Do exactly what I do." We fired together. Several men fell, and the others jumped up in panic, not knowing where the terrible noise had come from. We fired again, and then I ran out of the trees, shouting as loudly as I could, and Friday ran beside me.

I cut the ropes of the bearded prisoner and asked him in Portuguese who he was. He answered in Latin that he was a Christian, and then in Spanish that he was from Spain. He was so weak that he could hardly stand. I gave him a pistol and a sword, and he fought with great courage.

The fight did not last long. A few of the savages escaped in a canoe and paddled away as fast as they could. Friday wanted to follow them, so we ran to another canoe. To our surprise, lying in the bottom of it, there was another prisoner, tied hand and foot, almost dead with fear. I cut his ropes and gave him a little rum. Friday looked at him, and suddenly he cried out. He kissed the old man, hugged him, cried, laughed, sang and danced.

It was his father.

I cannot describe the joy of that young man. He sat beside his father for hours, holding his head against his chest, rubbing his arms and legs, which were stiff from the ropes. He ran home to fetch water and bread for him, and then he ran back again, so fast that he seemed to fly. Watching them, I understood that love between a father and a son was the same in every nation on earth. I thought about my own father, whom I had left without a word, and my eyes filled with tears.

We carried the two weak men back home on our shoulders and in the canoe. I built a tent for them, gave them blankets, and Friday and I cooked a goat for dinner. Now my island had four people. I sometimes smiled and thought of myself as a king with three subjects. But I knew that it was only a joke. Friday was my friend, his father was his father, and the Spaniard was a gentleman as proud and as free as I was. In my little country, I decided, every person would be free to follow his own religion.

When the Spaniard was stronger, we had long conversations. He told me that he and sixteen of his countrymen had been living for years among Friday's people. They had lost their ship and were very poor, with no tools and no way of escaping. "If you would help them," he said, "they would all come here and work with you to build a ship. Every one of them would thank you for the rest of his life."

I was cautious. "What if they arrive here and take my island, or send me to the prisons of the Spanish king?" I asked.

He promised honestly that they would not. He offered to go himself, with Friday's father, to bring them, and to make every man swear an oath of loyalty before they came.

But first we needed food for so many people. So for a whole season all four of us worked together. We planted much more barley and rice than usual, we caught and tamed more goats, and we dried grapes in large quantities. Friday and his father worked harder than anybody. At last, in October, we gave the Spaniard and Friday's father a canoe, food, guns and powder, and they sailed away towards the west. They promised to come back as soon as possible, and Friday and I watched until their sail disappeared.

Chapter 11: The Ship and the Return

Eight days later, early in the morning, Friday ran into my room. "They are coming! They are coming!" he shouted.

I jumped up and ran to the hill. But it was not the canoe. About a league out to sea there was an English ship at anchor, and a boat was coming towards the shore. My first feeling was joy. My second feeling was suspicion. Why would an English ship come to this lonely part of the world?

I watched from the trees. There were eleven men in the boat, and three of them were prisoners with their hands tied. The others were waving their arms and pushing them roughly. "Robinson," whispered Friday, "they are going to eat them!"

"No, Friday," I said. "I am afraid they will murder them, but they will not eat them."

The sailors left the three prisoners on the beach and wandered off into the woods. It was very hot, and soon most of them lay down to sleep under the trees. When it was safe, I walked up quietly behind the three prisoners. "Gentlemen," I said in English, "do not be afraid. Perhaps you have a friend here when you least expect one."

They jumped up and stared at me, with my goatskin clothes, my enormous hat, my beard and all my guns. One of them took off his hat to me. "Are you a man or an angel from heaven?" he asked.

"I am an Englishman," I replied, "and I am ready to help you. What has happened?"

He told me that he was the captain of the ship. His crew had mutinied against him, and they had brought him and two loyal men here to leave them to die on this island. "Two of the mutineers are very bad men," he said. "The rest were led by them, and I believe they would come back to their duty if they could."

"I will help you," I said, "on two conditions. First, while you are on my island, you will follow my orders. Second, if we take back the ship, you will carry me and my friend to England for nothing."

He agreed immediately and shook my hand. I gave the three of them guns. When the mutineers woke up, we attacked them. The two leaders were killed, and the others threw down their weapons and begged for mercy. The captain forgave them on the condition that they would help him.

Later, the men on the ship sent another boat to look for their friends. Friday and I played a clever trick on them. We hid in the trees and shouted, then ran from one place to another, calling and answering, so that they followed our voices into the woods until they were completely lost. When night came, they were tired and frightened, and the captain told them that the governor of the island had a large army and would hang them all. They surrendered without a fight. The captain thought that I was the governor, and I did not correct him, because the joke was useful.

That night the captain and his loyal men rowed out to the ship and took it back. At dawn, I heard seven guns from the sea. It was our signal. The ship was ours. The captain came ashore, embraced me, and pointed to the ship. "My dear friend and deliverer," he said, "there is your ship. She is yours, and so are we."

I nearly fainted. After twenty-eight years, I was free.

There was one difficult decision left. Five of the worst mutineers would be hanged if we took them to England. I offered them a choice: they could come with us and face the law, or they could stay on the island. They chose to stay. I showed them my castle, my fields, my goats and my country house. I explained how I made bread, cheese and pots, and I told them about the seventeen Spaniards who would soon arrive, asking them to treat them well. I left them my guns, my tools and my journal, which explained everything.

On the 19th of December 1686, I went on board with Friday. I took my goatskin hat, my umbrella and my parrot, and the money that I had found in the first shipwreck, which had waited for me all those years. I had been on the island for twenty-eight years, two months and nineteen days.

When we reached England, I found that my parents had died long before, although two sisters were still alive. Nobody remembered me. I felt like a stranger in my own country. Then I remembered my plantation in Brazil. I travelled to Lisbon, the capital of Portugal, to discover what had happened to it. There, by an extraordinary coincidence, I found my old friend, the Portuguese captain who had rescued me from the African coast so many years before. He was elderly now and no longer went to sea, but he recognised me immediately and embraced me with tears in his eyes.

He explained that, after I disappeared, my partners in Brazil had continued to manage the plantation. Officially, the government had considered me dead, and part of the profits had been given to a monastery and to the royal treasury. "But you are alive," said the captain, "and the law will return everything to you." He helped me to write letters and prepare the necessary documents. Some months later a ship arrived from Brazil with letters from my partners, together with chests of gold, sugar and tobacco. My plantation had become one of the most valuable in the region. Suddenly I was a rich man, and I was so overwhelmed by the news that I became quite ill and needed a doctor.

I did not forget the people who had helped me. I gave the old captain a generous pension for the rest of his life, and I sent money to my sisters in England.

I sold the plantation and settled in England. Friday came with me everywhere. He travelled through France and Spain with me, and once, in the snowy mountains between those countries, he saved us all from a pack of hungry wolves with his calm courage. He saw snow for the first time and touched it with wonder, laughing like a child. Many times I asked him whether he wanted to go back to his own people. Each time he smiled and said, "Where Robinson goes, Friday goes."

Years later, I sailed back to see my island. The Spaniards and the English sailors had quarrelled, made peace, married women from the mainland and built a small town. My island had become a colony full of children, gardens and stories. They told me that people there still talked about the man in goatskin who had lived alone for twenty-eight years.

Sometimes, when I sit by my English fire, I think of my father's words. He was right that the middle way of life is the safest. But I would not change my story. On that island, alone with my thoughts, I learned patience, I learned to pray, and I learned that a man can rebuild his whole life with a few tools and a little hope. And from Friday I learned the most important lesson of all: that a stranger from the other side of the sea can become your best friend.

The End
