Chapter 1: The Old Sea Dog

My name is Jim Hawkins, and Squire Trelawney, Dr Livesey and the other gentlemen have asked me to write down the whole story of Treasure Island. I will tell it from the beginning to the end, and I will keep back only one thing: the place of the island. There is still treasure there that we never took.

When this story begins, I was only a boy, and my father kept an inn called the Admiral Benbow. It stood on the coast of the west of England, in a quiet bay, and the sea wind blew against its windows all year.

One cold morning, an old sailor came to our door. I remember him as if it were yesterday. He was tall, strong and brown from the sun. His hair hung behind him in a dirty tail, his hands were rough and scarred, and there was a white cut across one cheek. A man with a wheelbarrow followed him, carrying a heavy sea chest.

The old sailor looked around the bay and then up at our sign. He began to sing in a high, broken voice:

"Fifteen men on the dead man's chest, yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!"

Then he knocked on the door with a stick and asked my father for a glass of rum.

"This is a handy place," he said. "Do many people come here?"

"Very few," said my father.

"Then this is the place for me," he answered. He threw three or four gold coins on the table. "I'm a plain man. Rum and bacon and eggs is all I want. You can call me captain. When that money is finished, tell me."

He stayed with us for months. All day he walked along the cliffs with a brass telescope, and all evening he sat in the corner by the fire and drank rum and water. He hardly spoke to anyone, and every day, when he came back from his walk, he asked the same question: had any sailors passed along the road?

One day he took me aside. "Jim," he said, "I will give you a silver fourpenny on the first of every month. All you must do is watch for a seafaring man with one leg. If you see him, tell me at once."

I took the money, but the man with one leg filled my dreams. On stormy nights I saw him in a thousand forms. Sometimes his leg was cut off at the knee, sometimes at the hip. Sometimes he was a monster with one leg in the middle of his body, running after me over hedges and ditches.

The captain frightened everyone. When he drank too much rum, he told terrible stories about hanging men, storms at sea and wild islands in the Spanish Main. He made the whole room sing his song. If someone did not sing, or tried to leave, he hit the table with his hand and shouted until everyone was silent. My father said that the captain would ruin the inn. But the truth is that people secretly enjoyed it. The young men of the village called him a "real old sea dog" and came back every night to hear him.

The captain's money ended after a few months, but my father was too ill and too frightened to ask for more.

That winter my father grew very sick, and Dr Livesey came to see him one evening. When he had finished, he sat down in the parlour to smoke his pipe. He was a neat, clever man with a white wig and bright black eyes, and he was also a magistrate. The captain began his song, louder than ever: "Drink and the devil had done for the rest..." Then he hit the table for silence.

Everyone stopped talking except the doctor, who went on speaking quietly to a farmer.

"Silence, there!" shouted the captain.

"Were you speaking to me, sir?" said the doctor.

"I was," said the captain, with a terrible word.

"I have only one thing to say to you," the doctor answered calmly. "If you keep on drinking rum, the world will soon be free of a very dirty scoundrel."

The captain jumped up, pulled out a sailor's knife and held it ready, but the doctor did not even move.

"Put that knife away," he said, "or I promise you will hang at the next court."

For a long moment, they looked at each other. Then the captain put the knife away and sat down, growling like a beaten dog.

"I am a magistrate," the doctor added. "If I hear one complaint about you, I will have you hunted and thrown out of this country. Remember that."

Soon after that, a strange thing happened. It was a cold January morning, and the captain had gone out early when a pale man came into the inn. Two fingers were missing from his left hand. He did not look like a sailor, but there was something of the sea about him.

"Is my mate Bill here?" he asked, with an ugly smile. "He has a cut on one cheek."

"I don't know your mate Bill," I said. "This is for a man we call the captain."

"That's Bill," he said. "We'll wait for him together, sonny."

When the captain came in, the stranger called, "Bill!" The captain turned round, and all the colour left his face. He looked like a man who had seen a ghost.

"Black Dog!" he said.

They sent me away and talked in low voices, and then I heard shouting, a crash and the sound of steel. Black Dog ran out of the door with blood on his shoulder, and the captain ran after him with his sword. He struck once at the fleeing man, but he hit only our big signboard. You can still see the mark there today.

The captain came back inside, very pale. "Jim," he said, "rum!" Before I could bring it, he fell to the floor with a loud crash.

Dr Livesey arrived just in time to save him. "It is a stroke," he said. "I warned him. Another glass of rum will kill him."

Chapter 2: The Black Spot

The next day, the captain lay in his bed, weak and angry, and when I brought him his medicine, he called me to his side.

"Jim," he said, "you're the only one here who is worth anything, and you know I've always been good to you. That doctor says I mustn't drink, but I've lived on rum, and I'll die without it. Bring me just one glass, and I'll give you a golden guinea."

I brought him the glass, partly because I was afraid that his shouting would disturb my father, who was very ill in the room next door. He emptied it in one long swallow and lay back on the pillow.

"Listen to me carefully," he whispered. "If I can't get away soon, they'll give me the black spot. It's my old sea chest they want. Flint's men are after it, every one of them. I was Flint's first mate, and I'm the only one who knows the place, because he gave it to me when he was dying at Savannah. If they come, Jim, get on a horse and ride to that doctor. Tell him to bring the magistrates, and he'll catch all of Flint's crew, or all that's left of it."

"But what is the black spot, captain?" I asked.

"It's a summons, mate, a kind of warning. And above all, Jim, watch out for the seafaring man with one leg."

That same evening, to everyone's surprise, my poor father died. For several days I was so busy with the funeral, the neighbours and the work of the inn that I hardly had time to think about the captain.

On the afternoon after the funeral, I was standing sadly at the door, thinking of my father, when I heard a tapping sound on the frozen road. A blind man was slowly coming towards the inn, tapping in front of him with a stick. He was bent, as if with age or weakness. He wore a green shade over his eyes and nose and an enormous old sea cloak with a hood, which made him look completely deformed. I have never in my life seen a more frightening figure.

"Will any kind friend tell a poor blind man where he is at present?" he said in a strange, singing voice. "I lost the precious sight of my eyes in the service of my country."

"You are at the Admiral Benbow, Black Hill Cove, my good man," I answered.

"I hear a young voice," he said. "Will you give me your hand, my kind young friend, and lead me in?"

I held out my hand, and immediately the horrible creature gripped it like a vice. "Now, boy," he said, and his voice was suddenly cold and cruel, "take me in to the captain, or I'll break your arm."

I was so terrified that I obeyed him at once. When we entered the parlour, the captain looked up, and the effects of the rum disappeared from his face. He seemed too sick and too frightened to stand.

"Now, Bill, sit where you are," said the blind man. "If I can't see, I can hear a finger moving. Hold out your left hand. Boy, take his hand by the wrist and bring it near to my right."

We both did exactly what he said, and I saw him pass something from his hand into the captain's palm, which closed upon it immediately. "And now that's done," said the blind man. He let go of me suddenly and, with incredible speed and accuracy, tapped his way out of the inn and into the road.

The captain opened his hand and stared at a small round piece of paper, blackened on one side. "Ten o'clock!" he cried. "Six hours. We'll do them yet!" He jumped to his feet, but at that moment he put his hand to his throat, swayed for a second, and fell on his face. He was dead, killed by a second stroke.

I burst into tears. I had never liked the captain, although I had begun to pity him, but now I understood that my mother and I were alone and in great danger. The blind man's companions would certainly come at ten o'clock, so we ran to the village for help. Nobody was willing to come. Everyone had heard the terrible name of Captain Flint, and they were too frightened to fight his men. In the end, only one young man agreed to ride to Dr Livesey's house.

"Well," said my mother, "the captain owed us money, and I will not lose it. We'll go back and open his chest, and I'll take what belongs to us."

We hurried back through the cold, foggy darkness. I found the key on a string around the dead man's neck, and we opened the chest, which smelled strongly of tobacco and tar. Inside there were clothes, a pair of pistols, a compass, some old Spanish watches and a few curious shells from the West Indies. At the very bottom lay a bundle wrapped in oilcloth and a canvas bag of coins.

My mother began counting the money, but it was slow and difficult work, because the coins came from every country in the world. Being an honest woman, she was determined to take only the exact amount that we were owed.

Suddenly I heard a sound that froze my blood: the tap-tap-tapping of the blind man's stick on the frozen road. "Mother," I whispered, "take it all and let's go!"

"I'll take what I have," she said, jumping up, and I picked up the oilcloth bundle to square the account.

We ran out of the inn and down the road. When we reached the little bridge, my mother fainted, and I pulled her with great difficulty into the shadow underneath it. From there I watched seven or eight men come running up the road, with the blind man in the middle of them.

"Down with the door!" he cried. They broke into the inn, and soon one voice shouted, "Bill's dead!" and another, "Somebody has been at the chest! Flint's papers are gone!"

"It's those people from the inn," cried the blind man furiously. "That boy! I wish I had put his eyes out! Find them, you fools, scatter and find them!"

At that moment, however, we heard horses galloping up the road. The pirates ran in every direction, and the blind man, who was left alone in his panic, ran straight into the road and fell under the horses. He never moved again.

The riders were officers who had heard about a pirate ship in the bay. They had arrived too late to catch the pirates, but just in time to save us. I immediately asked them to take me to Dr Livesey, because I had the thing that Flint's men were looking for in my pocket.

Chapter 3: The Map and the Ship

Dr Livesey was having dinner with Squire Trelawney at the Hall. The squire was a big, red-faced gentleman with rough eyebrows and a quick temper. He listened to my story with enormous excitement, walking impatiently up and down the room.

"Flint!" he exclaimed. "He was the most bloodthirsty pirate who ever sailed. The Spaniards were so afraid of him that, I tell you, sir, I was sometimes proud that he was an Englishman."

"But did he have any money?" asked the doctor.

"Money! What do pirates sail for, if not for money?"

"Then we shall soon know," said the doctor calmly. "Jim, give me the packet."

He opened it carefully on the table. Inside there was a notebook with the captain's accounts, and also a sealed paper. When the doctor broke the seal, a map fell out. It was the map of an island, about nine miles long and five miles across, shaped like a fat dragon standing up. There were two harbours, several hills, and in the centre a taller hill called the Spyglass. Three red crosses had been added in ink, two in the northern part of the island and one in the south-west. Beside this last cross, in a small, neat hand, someone had written: "Bulk of treasure here."

On the back, there were some mysterious instructions:

"Tall tree, Spyglass shoulder, bearing a point to the N. of N.N.E. Skeleton Island E.S.E. and by E. Ten feet."

"Livesey," said the squire, "you will give up your medical practice at once. Tomorrow I'm leaving for Bristol, and in three weeks I'll have the best ship and the finest crew in England. Hawkins shall come as cabin boy, you shall be ship's doctor, and I'll be admiral. We'll find that treasure and roll in money for the rest of our lives!"

"Trelawney," said the doctor, "I'll go with you, and so will Jim. But there's one man I'm afraid of."

"And who's that?" cried the squire. "Name the dog, sir!"

"You," replied the doctor, "because you cannot hold your tongue. We are not the only people who know about this paper. The men who attacked the inn tonight will do anything to get it, so not one of us must say a word about it."

"Livesey," said the squire, "you are always right. I'll be as silent as the grave."

A few weeks later, a letter arrived from Bristol. The squire had bought a fine ship called the Hispaniola, and he had found an experienced captain named Smollett. By great luck, he had also met an old sailor with one leg who kept a public house called the Spy-glass. This man, Long John Silver, had found him a whole crew of tough old seamen, and he had agreed to come on the voyage as ship's cook. At the end, the letter added cheerfully that everyone in Bristol knew where we were going. My heart sank when I read it, because the doctor was not there to stop him.

I said a sad goodbye to my mother and travelled to Bristol by coach. The squire sent me with a note to Long John Silver's inn, and I was very nervous, because I remembered the captain's warning about a seafaring man with one leg. But as soon as I saw Silver, I felt better.

His left leg had been cut off close to the hip. He carried a crutch under his left arm, which he used with wonderful skill, jumping around on it like a bird. He was very tall and strong, with a face as big as a ham, plain and pale, but intelligent and smiling. He seemed in the best of spirits, whistling as he moved among the tables and joking with his customers. This was not a pirate, I said to myself. This was a good, friendly man.

But just as I came in, a customer jumped up and hurried out of the door, and I recognised that pale face at once. "Stop him!" I cried. "It's Black Dog!"

Silver sent a man after him and then turned to me, looking shocked. "Black Dog? I don't know any Black Dog. And he was sitting at my tables, drinking my rum!" When the man came back without Black Dog, Silver shook his head sadly. "What will Squire Trelawney think of me? A pirate in my own house! Come, Hawkins, we'll go and tell him together."

As we walked along the busy docks, he explained everything to me about the ships, their ropes and their sails. By the end of the walk, I was convinced that he was the best companion in the world.

The captain of the Hispaniola, however, was not happy at all. Captain Smollett was a sharp-looking man who seemed annoyed with everything and everyone on board.

"I don't like this voyage," he told the squire and the doctor in the cabin. "I don't like the men, and I don't like my first mate. I was hired to sail this ship, and now I discover that every sailor on board knows more than I do. They say that you're going after treasure. They even say that you have a map, with crosses to show where the treasure is."

"I never told that to a soul!" cried the squire.

"The men know it, sir," said the captain, and the doctor looked at the squire, because he was thinking of the letter.

"Very well," the captain continued. "If you won't turn back, then let me make some changes. Put the weapons and the powder near the cabin, not in the front of the ship, and give your own servants the cabins beside it. And keep the map secret from everyone, even from me."

The squire was offended, but the doctor agreed with every word. Early the next morning, the Hispaniola sailed out of Bristol harbour towards the open sea.

Chapter 4: What I Heard in the Apple Barrel

The voyage was long but pleasant, because the Hispaniola was an excellent ship and the crew worked well. Only the first mate, Mr Arrow, was a disappointment. He turned out to be a drunkard, and one dark and stormy night he disappeared completely. Nobody was very surprised, and everybody agreed that he must have fallen into the sea.

Long John Silver was the favourite of the whole crew, who called him Barbecue. He tied his crutch around his neck with a cord, so that he had both hands free for cooking in the galley. On the rolling deck he moved as easily as a man on dry land. He was always kind to me, and he always had something delicious for me when I visited him.

He kept a parrot in a cage in the corner of the galley. "This is Cap'n Flint," he told me proudly. "I've named her after the famous pirate." And the parrot screamed, "Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight!" again and again, until Silver threw a handkerchief over the cage.

"That bird is maybe two hundred years old, Hawkins," he said seriously. "She has sailed with the great pirate England, and she has seen more wickedness than the devil himself."

On deck, there was a big barrel of apples, and anyone could take one whenever he wanted. One evening, near the end of the voyage, I felt like eating an apple before going to bed. The barrel was almost empty, so I climbed right inside to reach the last one. It was dark and warm in there, and the gentle movement of the ship made me sleepy. I was nearly asleep when a heavy man sat down beside the barrel and leaned his back against it.

It was Silver, and before he had said a dozen words, I understood that the lives of all the honest men on board depended on me alone.

"No, not I," Silver was saying. "Flint was captain, and I was quartermaster, because of my wooden leg. I lost my leg in the same battle where old Pew lost his eyes."

He was talking to Dick, one of the youngest sailors on the ship. "Gentlemen of fortune," Silver went on, "live rough and risk the rope. But when a voyage is finished, they have hundreds of pounds in their pockets. I've put my money safely in different banks, so nobody suspects anything. I'm fifty years old now, and after this cruise I'll live like a gentleman."

"Well," said the young man, "I didn't like the idea at first, but I'm with you now, John."

"You're a brave lad, and intelligent too," said Silver, shaking his hand so hard that the whole barrel moved.

By now I understood everything. "Gentlemen of fortune" was simply another name for pirates, and Silver was persuading one more honest sailor to join his conspiracy. Soon a third man arrived. It was Israel Hands, the coxswain.

"How long are we going to wait, Barbecue?" asked Hands impatiently. "I've had enough of Captain Smollett and his orders."

"Until the last possible moment," said Silver. "The captain is a first-rate sailor, and he'll take us there safely. The squire and the doctor have the map, and they'll find the treasure for us. When we've got it on board, then we'll act. If I had my way, I'd let them bring it halfway home."

"And what will we do with them when we've finished?" asked Dick.

"That's what I like to hear!" said Silver. "That's business. Shall we leave them on the island, or shall we cut them down like pork? That's what Flint would do."

"Dead men don't bite," said Hands.

"Right you are," said Silver. "When the time comes, I want the squire for myself, and I'll wring his head off his body with these hands. Dick, jump up like a good lad and get me an apple."

You can imagine my terror. I wanted to jump out and run away, but my legs refused to move. Fortunately, Hands said, "Oh, don't bother with that rubbish, John. Let's have some rum instead." So Dick went to fetch the rum, and I was saved.

At that moment, the moon rose over the sea, and a voice shouted from the top of the mast: "Land ho!"

There was a great noise of running feet. I slipped out of the barrel, hid for a moment in the shadows and then joined the others at the front of the ship. Far away in the moonlight, we could see two low hills, with a third, taller hill rising behind them.

Captain Smollett called Silver to him. "Have you ever seen that land before?"

"I have, sir," said Silver. "I was there as cook on a trading ship. That little island is called Skeleton Island, and it was a favourite place for pirates once. The big hill in the middle is called the Spyglass."

The captain showed him a map of the island. Silver's eyes were burning with excitement, but I noticed his disappointment immediately, because it was only a copy, without the red crosses. The captain had wisely kept the real map secret.

As soon as I could, I went to Dr Livesey. "Doctor," I whispered, "call the captain and the squire down to the cabin and then send for me. I have terrible news."

A few minutes later, I told them everything I had heard, and they listened in silence.

"Captain," said the squire, "you were right, and I was wrong. I'm an ass, and I'm waiting for your orders."

"No more an ass than I am," said the captain. "I've never heard of a crew that planned a mutiny without showing any signs of it. But this crew has fooled me completely."

Then they counted the men they could trust. There were the squire's three servants, Hunter, Joyce and Redruth, and then the captain, the squire and the doctor, and finally me. That made seven, including a boy, against nineteen.

"We can't turn back now, so we must go on," said the captain. "And Hawkins can help us more than anyone. The men aren't afraid of him, and he notices everything."

I felt very small and very worried. I was only a boy, and now our lives depended on me.

Chapter 5: My Shore Adventure

When I came on deck the next morning, the island looked completely different. The wind had dropped during the night, and we were lying about half a mile from the shore. Grey-coloured woods covered a large part of the land, and above them rose tall, strange hills of naked rock. The waves were breaking with a loud, endless noise on the rocks. The smell of wet leaves and rotting trees came out to us across the water. In spite of the sunshine and the singing birds, my heart sank. From that first moment, I hated the very thought of Treasure Island.

The heat was terrible, and the men were in a dangerous mood. We had to pull the ship slowly into the anchorage behind Skeleton Island, and the sailors grumbled and complained continually over their work. Even the smallest order was obeyed with an angry look, and mutiny was hanging over us like a thundercloud.

Only Long John Silver seemed cheerful. He moved from group to group, giving useful advice and setting a good example. He sang so loudly and so often that he seemed to want to hide the bad temper of the others.

Captain Smollett called us into the cabin. "If I give another order," he said, "the whole crew will rise against us. Silver is as anxious as we are, because he wants to wait until the treasure is found. So let's give him the opportunity to calm them down. We'll give the men an afternoon on shore. If they all go, we'll fight for the ship. If none of them go, we'll defend the cabin. And if some of them go, Silver will bring them back as quiet as lambs."

At first the plan worked perfectly. When the men heard about the afternoon on shore, they cheered so loudly that the birds flew up from the trees. Six men stayed on the ship, and the other thirteen, including Silver, climbed into the two boats.

Then I had a foolish idea, the first of many in this story. I imagined that I would be more useful on the island than on the ship. So, just before the boats left, I slipped over the side and hid under the seat of the nearest one. Nobody noticed me, except one man, who said, "Is that you, Jim? Keep your head down."

But Silver, in the other boat, looked sharply across and called out, "Jim! Jim!" As soon as our boat touched the beach, I jumped out, ran into the trees and did not stop until I was far away.

I was alone on the island, and I felt the joy of an explorer. There were unfamiliar plants and colourful flowers all around me. I saw several snakes, and one of them raised its head from a rock and made a noise like a spinning top. I did not realise that it was a deadly rattlesnake.

Then I heard voices, and I hid quickly behind a thick tree. Silver was talking to Tom, one of the honest sailors.

"Tom," Silver was saying, "I'm warning you because I like you. If I didn't, do you think I'd be here? The others have gone too far now. Join us, or you're a dead man."

"Silver," said Tom, "you're old and you're honest, or at least people think so. And you have money, which most poor sailors don't. Will you let yourself be led away by that kind of dirty scum? Not you! As sure as God sees me, I'd rather lose my hand."

Suddenly, from far away across the marsh, there came a long, horrible scream. Tom jumped like a horse that has been hit. "John!" he cried. "What was that?"

"That?" said Silver, smiling. "That'll be Alan."

"Alan!" cried Tom. "Then rest his soul, because he was a true seaman. As for you, John Silver, you've been a mate of mine for a long time, but you're not my mate any more. Kill me too, if you can. I defy you."

He turned his back on Silver and began to walk away, but he did not get far. With a cry, Silver grabbed a branch, took the crutch from under his arm and threw it with all his strength. It hit poor Tom in the middle of his back, and he fell to the ground. Then Silver, as quick as a monkey, was on top of him, and he buried his knife twice in that helpless body.

The whole world seemed to turn round in front of my eyes. When I could see again, Silver was standing calmly over the body, wiping his knife on a handful of grass. Then he took a whistle from his pocket and blew it several times. I did not wait any longer. I crawled back through the bushes and then ran as fast as I could. I was certain that I would never see the ship again, and that the pirates would find me and murder me like Tom.

Chapter 6: The Man of the Island

As I ran, a stone fell from the steep side of a hill and rolled noisily down through the trees. I looked up and saw a figure jumping quickly behind a pine tree. It was dark and hairy, and I could not tell whether it was a bear, a monkey or a man. I stopped immediately, more terrified than ever, because there were murderers behind me and this mysterious creature in front of me.

Then I remembered that I was carrying a pistol, and my courage returned. I walked towards the figure, and it came out from behind the tree. To my astonishment, it fell on its knees and held out its hands to me, as if it were begging for mercy.

"Who are you?" I asked.

"Ben Gunn," he answered, and his voice sounded rough and awkward, like a rusty lock. "I'm poor Ben Gunn, I am, and I haven't spoken to a Christian for three years."

He was a white man like myself, but his skin was burnt almost black by the tropical sun, and even his lips were dark. His clothes were made of old pieces of sail and ship's cloth, held together with brass buttons, little sticks and pieces of string. Only his pale blue eyes looked strange and gentle in that dark, wild face.

"Three years!" I cried. "Were you shipwrecked?"

"No, mate," he said. "I was marooned. My shipmates left me here, all alone, with nothing but a musket and a few bullets. I've lived on goats and berries and oysters ever since. But many a long night I've dreamed of cheese, toasted mostly. You don't have a piece of cheese about you, do you?"

"If I ever get back on board," I promised, "you shall have cheese by the pound."

All this time he had been feeling the cloth of my jacket and touching my hands, with childish pleasure. Then, suddenly, his face became serious and suspicious. "Now tell me true," he said. "Is that Flint's ship out there in the anchorage?"

"It isn't Flint's ship, and Flint is dead," I said. "But some of Flint's men are on board, and that's worse luck for the rest of us."

"Not a man with one leg?" he gasped.

"Silver?" I asked, and when he heard the name, he trembled. "If you were sent by Long John," he whispered, "I'm as good as dead."

I decided to trust him, and I told him the whole story of our voyage and the dangerous position we were in. He listened with great attention, and when I had finished, he patted me on the head.

"You're a good lad, Jim," he said, "and you're all in a mess, aren't you? Well, you just put your trust in Ben Gunn. Do you think your squire would be generous to a man who helped him? Would he give him, let us say, one thousand pounds out of money that is as good as his own already?"

"I'm sure he would," I said. "The squire is a real gentleman, and he would take you home too."

"Ah," he said, looking very pleased, "then I'll tell you a story. I was on Flint's ship when he buried the treasure. He went ashore with six strong seamen, and he stayed there a whole week, while we waited on the old Walrus. Then one day he came back alone in the little boat, with a blue scarf around his head. The six men were all dead, killed and buried, and how he did it, not one of us on board could ever discover."

He was silent for a moment, as if he were seeing it all again. "Years later," he continued, "I was sailing on another ship, and we passed this island. I told my shipmates about the treasure, and we landed to look for it. We searched for twelve long days, and every day they spoke more angrily to me. In the end they gave me a musket, a spade and a pickaxe, and they told me to stay here and find Flint's money for myself. Then they sailed away and left me."

He looked at me with a strange, clever expression, closing one eye. "But now, Jim, Ben Gunn has a boat of his own, which he made with his own two hands, and he keeps it hidden under the white rock. And Ben Gunn is a rich man, Jim. Tell your squire that. Tell him I have reasons of my own, but that I'm a man to trust."

At that moment, the noise of a cannon shot rolled across the island like thunder, followed by the sharp sound of muskets.

"They have begun to fight!" I cried. "Follow me."

We ran through the woods towards the sound. Before long, I saw a British flag flying above the trees, from inside a strong wooden fence on the top of a small hill.

"There are your friends," said Ben Gunn. "If it were Silver, he'd fly the Jolly Roger, the black pirate flag. That's the stockade, the old fort that Flint built years ago. Go to them now, Jim. When you want Ben Gunn, you know where to find him."

Chapter 7: The Stockade

While I was having my adventures on the island, my friends on the ship had been extremely busy. Dr Livesey told me the whole story afterwards, and I will tell it here exactly as he told it to me.

When the two boats went ashore, the doctor, the captain and the squire sat together in the cabin and discussed what they should do. Six pirates were still on board, sitting angrily on the deck, and nobody knew what they were planning. Then Hunter came down with the news that I had slipped into one of the boats, and the doctor was quite sure that I was lost. The heat was unbearable, and the stink of the marshes came across the water like a fever.

There was a small rowing boat on the ship, called the jolly boat, and the doctor decided to use it. He and Hunter rowed secretly to the shore to look for a safer place, and behind some trees, near the top of a small hill, they discovered the stockade. It was a strong log house, built of thick tree trunks, with narrow holes in the walls for shooting through. A high wooden fence surrounded it on every side, and the trees had been cut down all around, so nobody could approach without being seen. Best of all, there was a spring of clear, cold water inside the fence. It was the perfect place to defend, and the doctor, who had been a soldier in his youth, recognised that at once.

The doctor and Hunter rowed back to the Hispaniola as fast as they could. On board, they quickly filled the jolly boat with muskets, powder, biscuits and pork. Then they threw the rest of the weapons into the sea, so that the pirates would not be able to use them against us. While they were working, Captain Smollett spoke to the coxswain, Israel Hands, who was the leader of the six men on deck.

"Mr Hands," he said, "there are two of us here with a pistol each. If any one of you six makes a signal of any kind, that man is dead."

One of the six, Abraham Gray, was an honest sailor who had been frightened into joining the pirates. As the captain was leaving the ship, he called down to him: "Gray, I'm leaving this ship, and I order you to follow your captain. I know you're a good man at heart, and I'm giving you thirty seconds to join me."

There was a sudden noise of fighting below the deck, and Gray came running out with a fresh knife cut on his cheek. "I'm with you, sir," he said, and he jumped into the boat.

The jolly boat was so heavily loaded that the sea came almost over the sides, and the tide was pushing them away from the landing place. As they rowed slowly towards the shore, the pirates on the ship began to turn the great cannon round to fire at them. The squire, who was the best shot among them, aimed his musket at the man beside the gun and fired. The man fell, but at the same moment the cannon went off. The cannonball passed over the boat, and the wind of it, or perhaps their own movement, made the boat sink. Luckily, the water was not deep there, and they were able to wade to the shore. But half of their food and most of their powder were lost.

They ran through the woods towards the stockade, and the pirates who had gone ashore ran to stop them. There was a short, violent fight at the edge of the trees. One pirate was killed, but Tom Redruth, the squire's old servant, was shot. They carried him into the log house, where he died that afternoon, with the squire crying beside him like a child. The captain took a British flag from his pocket and raised it on a tall pole above the roof. It was this flag that I had seen from the woods with Ben Gunn.

So, when I climbed over the fence that evening, I found my friends tired and sad, but alive and safe. They were overjoyed to see me, and I told them everything about Ben Gunn. The doctor listened with great attention.

"Is he mad?" he asked.

"I don't know, sir," I said. "He talked very strangely about cheese."

"Ah," said the doctor, and he took a little box out of his pocket. "Do you see this, Jim? It's a piece of Parmesan cheese, made in Italy and very nourishing. I always carry it with me, and I think it may be very useful one day."

That night, we divided the work between us. The captain made a careful list of our provisions. "We have enough food for ten weeks if we're careful," he said. "We have powder and muskets, and the pirates are drinking rum and catching fever in the marshes. If we shoot a few more of them, the others will be glad to sail away."

I was so exhausted that I slept like a log. In the morning, I was woken by a voice outside the fence.

"Flag of truce!" someone shouted, and then, in great surprise: "It's Silver himself!"

Chapter 8: Silver's Visit and the Attack

Silver was standing outside the fence with another man, who was waving a white cloth above his head. It was a cold, misty morning, and a damp wind was blowing the fog down from the hills.

"Who goes there?" shouted the captain. "Stand, or we fire!"

"Flag of truce!" cried Silver. "Cap'n Silver, sir, to come aboard and make terms!"

"Captain Silver? I don't know him. Who's he?"

"Me, sir," Silver answered. "These poor lads have chosen me as their captain, after you deserted the ship. Let me come in, and we'll talk like gentlemen."

"My man," said the captain coldly, "I have no wish to talk to you. If you wish to talk to me, you can come, and that's all."

It took Silver a long time to climb the hill to the log house, because the sand was soft and his crutch sank deeply into it at every step. But at last he arrived, out of breath, and sat down on the doorstep in front of the captain, who did not move or offer him any help.

"You'll have to let me in, Cap'n," said Silver. "It's a cold morning to sit outside on the sand."

"If you had chosen to be an honest man, Silver, you might be sitting in your warm galley now," said the captain. "Say what you have to say, and be quick."

"Well," said Silver, "it was a fine trick that one of your people played last night. Someone came into our camp with a knife while we were sleeping, and one of my men is dead. But that isn't why I'm here. Here's the truth: we want that treasure, and we'll have it. You have a map, haven't you?"

"That's as may be," replied the captain.

"Give us the map," said Silver, "and stop shooting poor sailors. Then you can choose. You can come aboard with us after the treasure is loaded, and I give you my word of honour that I'll put you safely on shore somewhere. Or, if you prefer, you can stay here on the island. We'll divide the food with you, and I'll send the first ship I meet to rescue you. Now that's a fair offer, isn't it?"

Captain Smollett calmly knocked the ash out of his pipe. "Is that all?"

"Every last word, by thunder!" said Silver. "Refuse that, and you'll get musket balls."

"Very good," said the captain. "Now you'll hear me. If you'll come up one by one, without your weapons, I'll put you all in chains and take you home to a fair trial in England. If you won't, my name is Alexander Smollett, and I'll see you all to the devil. You can't find the treasure, and you can't sail the ship, because there isn't a man among you who is able to sail her. And you can't fight us, either. Those are the last good words you'll get from me. The next time I see you, I'll put a bullet in your back. Now go, and quickly!"

Silver's face was terrible with anger, and his eyes were burning. "Give me a hand up!" he cried, but nobody moved. Growling the worst words I have ever heard, he crawled along the sand until he reached the door and pulled himself up on it.

"Before an hour is over," he shouted, "I'll break your old log house like a barrel of rum. Laugh, by thunder, laugh! Before an hour is over, you'll laugh on the other side of your faces. The ones who die will be the lucky ones!"

As soon as he had disappeared among the trees, the captain began to prepare for the attack. He gave every man a position beside the narrow shooting holes, with loaded muskets ready beside him. Then he placed the powder and the cutlasses in the middle of the room. "We are outnumbered," he said, "but we're fighting from a shelter, and they aren't. I have no doubt that we can beat them, if we are determined."

An hour passed slowly. The sun climbed above the trees and burned on the roof, and the air inside the log house became hot and heavy. Then, without any warning, the pirates attacked. A crowd of men came running out of the woods on the north side and climbed over the fence as quickly as monkeys. Our muskets fired again and again, and the little house filled with smoke. Three pirates fell, but four were already inside the fence and running towards us, shouting and waving their swords.

"At them, all hands!" cried the captain. "Cutlasses!"

I grabbed a sword and ran outside into the bright sunshine. Someone was close behind me, but I did not know who it was. In front of me, the doctor was chasing a pirate down the hill, and as I watched, he knocked the man's sword aside and struck him down. Another pirate swung his cutlass at me, but I jumped to one side, slipped in the soft sand and rolled down the slope. When I stood up again, the fight was over.

The attack had failed completely. Five pirates lay dead, and the survivors had run back into the woods. But we had paid a high price for our victory. Joyce had been shot through the head, and Hunter was lying unconscious and dying beside the wall. The captain himself was badly wounded.

"Have they run?" asked the captain from the floor, where the doctor was looking after him.

"All that could, sir," said the doctor, "but five of them will never run again."

"Five!" said the captain. "That's better. Five against three leaves us four to nine, which are better odds than we had at the start. We were seven to nineteen then."

Chapter 9: My Sea Adventure

After the attack, the pirates did not return, and an unusual silence settled over the island. The doctor looked after the wounded captain, and then, in the middle of the afternoon, he did something surprising. He put the map in his pocket, took a gun and walked off into the woods, alone.

"He's gone to see Ben Gunn, I think," said Gray.

The log house was hot and dirty, and it smelled of blood. I looked at the dead bodies of Joyce and Hunter, and I suddenly felt an enormous desire to escape. I remembered what Ben Gunn had told me about his boat under the white rock. Perhaps, I thought, nobody had discovered it yet. It was a foolish idea, and I knew that the captain would never give me permission. So I filled my pockets with biscuits, took two pistols and, when nobody was watching, climbed over the fence and ran into the trees.

I walked across the island until I came to the sea, and I lay hidden among the bushes until the evening. As the fog began to roll in, I found the white rock, and underneath it, covered with goatskins, was Ben Gunn's boat. It was a tiny, lopsided thing, made of a wooden frame covered with skins, and it was so light that I could easily carry it. Sailors call this kind of boat a coracle.

Now a new and even wilder idea came into my head. I would row out to the Hispaniola in the darkness, cut her anchor rope and let her drift onto the shore. Then the pirates would never be able to escape with her.

When the night was completely dark, I put the coracle on the water. It was very difficult to steer. It turned round and round in every direction except the one I wanted. But the tide was going out, and it carried me towards the ship, whose lights I could see through the fog.

The anchor rope was as tight as a bowstring. I took out my knife and cut the strands one by one, waiting each time for the wind to make the rope go loose. While I was working, I heard loud, angry voices coming from the cabin window above me. Israel Hands was having a furious argument with another pirate, and both of them were very drunk.

At last the final strand broke, and the ship began to turn slowly in the current. I was so curious that I pulled myself up the rope and looked through the cabin window. Hands and his companion were fighting, each with a hand on the other's throat. Then the ship moved suddenly, the coracle shot away, and I fell back into my little boat.

The current pulled both the ship and the coracle out towards the open sea. I lay down in the bottom of the coracle and said my prayers, because I expected to die at any moment. As the ship and I drifted along the coast, I passed close to the pirates' camp. Their enormous fire was burning among the trees, and its red reflection shone on the black water. I could hear them singing in rough, drunken voices. The words of their melancholy song came clearly across the bay: "But one man of her crew alive, what put to sea with seventy-five." I shivered, because it seemed a terrible prophecy for our own voyage.

In the end, I was so exhausted that I fell asleep, and I dreamed of home and of the old Admiral Benbow.

When I woke up, it was daylight, and I was floating along the south-west coast of the island. Huge waves were breaking on the rocks, and enormous sea lions lay on the stones, barking loudly. I could not land there. So I let the current carry me north, paddling a little whenever the sea was calm, while the hot sun burned my face and my throat became painfully dry.

Then I saw something astonishing. Less than half a mile away, the Hispaniola was sailing in a strange, wild way, turning first in one direction and then in another. Nobody was steering her. I paddled towards her with all my strength, and when a wave threw the coracle against her side, I jumped up, grabbed a rope and climbed aboard. At the same moment, the ship struck the little boat and sank it. Now I could not go back.

Chapter 10: Israel Hands

The deck was empty and silent. Two men were lying there. One was the pirate in the red cap, who was clearly dead. The other was Israel Hands, pale and wounded, who sat leaning against the side of the ship. Empty bottles rolled across the boards as the ship moved.

"Brandy," Hands groaned when he saw me. "Bring me some brandy, lad."

I went down to the cabin, which the pirates had left in a horrible mess, and I brought him a bottle. Then I drank a long drink of water myself and ate some biscuits and cheese.

"Mr Hands," I said, "I've come on board to take possession of this ship, and you will please regard me as your captain until further notice."

He looked at me sourly but said nothing. Then I pulled down the black pirate flag and threw it into the sea. "God save the King!" I cried, waving my cap. "And that's the end of Captain Silver!"

Hands watched me with a sly expression. "I suppose, Cap'n Hawkins, you'll want to get ashore now," he said slowly. "Well, I'll make a bargain with you. You give me food and drink and a scarf to tie up my wound, and I'll tell you how to sail her. That's fair, isn't it?"

I agreed, because I needed his help. I wanted to take the ship to the North Inlet, a quiet harbour where I could hide her from the pirates. Hands told me what to do, and for a while we worked well together. The wind was kind, and the Hispaniola sailed smoothly along the coast, past the green woods and the sandy beaches. But I did not trust him, and I noticed the strange way he looked at me.

After a while, he began talking about death in a gloomy, philosophical way. "For thirty years," he said, "I've sailed the seas, and I've seen good and bad, better and worse, fair weather and foul. Well, I'll tell you something, Jim. I never saw good come of goodness yet. I like the man who strikes first. Dead men don't bite. That's my opinion." I pretended not to understand, but I remembered his words, and I became more careful than ever.

"Cap'n," he said after a while, "would you go down to the cabin and bring me some wine? This brandy is too strong for my head."

It was obviously an excuse to get rid of me. I went below, but I came back quickly and quietly by another way, and I looked out through a gap. Hands had crawled across the deck. He picked up a long knife that was lying among the ropes, hid it inside his coat and crawled back to his place. Then I knew that he intended to kill me as soon as I had brought the ship safely into the harbour.

I returned with the wine, pretending that I had seen nothing. We sailed into the North Inlet, and I steered the ship carefully towards the soft sand. I was so busy with the steering that I almost forgot the danger. Then some instinct made me turn my head. Hands was coming towards me, with the knife in his hand.

I screamed with terror, and he threw himself at me. I jumped aside, and at that moment the ship hit the beach and leaned over heavily, throwing us both onto the deck. I was up first. I ran to the mast and climbed it as fast as I could, until I reached the crosstrees, high above the deck. There I sat and quickly loaded my pistols, because the powder in them had become wet in the sea.

Hands began to climb slowly after me, with the knife between his teeth. When he was halfway up, I pointed a pistol at him.

"One more step, Mr Hands," I said, "and I'll blow your brains out! Dead men don't bite, you know," I added with a laugh.

He stopped, and he took the knife out of his mouth. "Jim," he said, "I think we're fouled, you and me, and we'll have to make an agreement. I would have had you, but I'm unlucky, I am." Then, suddenly, his right hand went back over his shoulder, and something flew through the air like an arrow. I felt a sharp blow and a terrible pain. The knife had pinned my shoulder to the mast.

In that moment of pain and surprise, both of my pistols went off, and they fell from my hands. With a choked cry, the coxswain let go of the ropes and fell head first into the water.

I was trembling so violently that I almost fell myself. Below me, through the clear water of the bay, I could see Israel Hands lying on the sand, under the shadow of the ship. He did not move again. When I had recovered a little, I pulled myself free from the knife, which had held me only by a little skin, and I climbed slowly down to the deck.

Chapter 11: Prisoner of the Pirates

It was evening when I left the ship and waded to the shore, and I felt extremely proud of myself. I had taken the Hispaniola back from the pirates, and now she lay safely in the North Inlet, ready for our journey home. I hurried across the island towards the stockade, eager to tell my friends everything and to receive their praise.

It was completely dark when I arrived. I climbed silently over the fence and crept towards the log house, where I could hear men snoring peacefully inside. I smiled to myself, because nobody was keeping watch, and I imagined the captain's face when he discovered such carelessness. I stepped in through the doorway, and as I did so, a shrill voice cried out of the darkness:

"Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight!"

It was Silver's green parrot, Captain Flint. Strong hands seized me at once, and somebody brought a burning torch. In its red light, I saw that my worst fears were true. The pirates were in possession of the house and all our provisions. There was no sign of my friends, and I was sure that they had all been killed.

Silver was sitting on a barrel, calmly smoking his pipe. "So," he said, "here's Jim Hawkins, shiver my timbers! Dropped in to visit us, eh? Well, that's friendly of you."

He explained that the doctor had come to them that morning under a flag of truce. The ship had disappeared, and the doctor had made a surprising deal. My friends had given the pirates the stockade, the provisions and the brandy, and they had gone away somewhere. "And you," he added, "were not part of the bargain. They don't want you any more, because they're angry with you for running away. So I think you'll have to join us."

My heart was beating fast, but I spoke as bravely as I could. "I am not such a fool," I said. "I know what to expect from you, and I don't care. But here's something you should know. It was I who heard you in the apple barrel. It was I who cut the ship's rope, and I who took her where you'll never find her. You can kill me if you like, but if you spare me, I'll do my best to save you from the hangman when you're on trial."

The pirates jumped up with angry shouts, and one of them pulled out a knife, but Silver stopped him immediately. "Put that down!" he roared. "Who do you think you are? I like that boy, and he's more of a man than any pair of rats in this house. Anyone who touches him will answer to me."

The men went outside to talk among themselves, and while they were away, Silver whispered quickly to me. "Listen, Jim. I've stood by you, and now you must stand by me. I can see that the game is lost. When the trouble comes, I'll save your life from those men, and you'll save Long John from hanging."

After a while, the men came back and gave Silver a piece of paper. On one side there was a black spot, made with ash, and on the other side somebody had written the word "Deposed". They wanted a different captain. Silver only laughed at them, and he listed all their mistakes, one after another, until they were ashamed. Then, with a magnificent gesture, he threw something on the floor in front of them. It was the map, with its three red crosses, and the doctor himself had given it to him.

The pirates jumped on it like cats on a mouse. They forgot their complaints at once, and they chose Silver as their captain again with loud cheers.

Early the next morning, Dr Livesey arrived at the stockade to treat the wounded and sick pirates. He was as calm and professional as if he were visiting patients in an English village. When he saw me, he was silent for a moment, and then he asked Silver for permission to speak to me alone.

We talked across the fence, in low voices. "Jim," he said sadly, "when Captain Smollett was well, you didn't dare to run away. When he was wounded and couldn't stop you, you ran. By George, that was a cowardly thing to do!"

I began to cry. "Doctor," I said, "I have blamed myself enough already. My life is lost anyway, and I would be dead now if Silver hadn't protected me."

"Jim," said the doctor, in a different voice, "jump over the fence, and we'll run for it together."

"No," I replied. "I gave my word to Silver. You wouldn't break your word, and neither will I." Then I told him quickly about the ship and the place where it was hidden.

"There is a kind of fate in this," he said thoughtfully. "At every step, it's you that saves our lives. You found out the plot, you found Ben Gunn, and now you've recovered the ship." Then he turned to Silver. "Silver," he said quietly, "I'll give you a piece of advice. Don't be in any great hurry to go after that treasure. And when you find it, look out for trouble."

Chapter 12: The Treasure

The pirates set out after breakfast with picks and shovels, and I went with them, tied to Silver by a rope around my waist. They were in an excellent mood, and each of them talked loudly about what he would do with his share of Flint's gold.

We followed the directions on the back of the map, looking for a tall tree on the shoulder of the Spyglass. But on the way, one of the men suddenly screamed with horror. At the foot of a big pine tree lay a human skeleton, with a few pieces of old clothing still on its bones. It was lying perfectly straight, with its feet pointing in one direction and its arms stretched above its head, like a diver.

"This is one of Flint's jokes," said Silver. "Those arms are pointing straight at the treasure. He killed this man and laid him here as a compass, by thunder!"

The pirates became very quiet and nervous. They began to talk in low voices about Flint, and about how he had died at Savannah, shouting for rum. Suddenly, from among the trees ahead of us, a thin, high voice began to sing the old song:

"Fifteen men on the dead man's chest, yo-ho-ho, and a bottle of rum!"

The pirates went white with fear. "It's Flint!" one of them cried. "It's Flint's ghost!"

The voice called out again, with a long, terrible cry: "Darby McGraw! Darby McGraw! Fetch aft the rum, Darby!" Those were exactly the last words that Flint had spoken before he died.

The men were too frightened to move, but Silver kept his head. "Shipmates," he said, "I'm here to get that treasure, and I'll not be beaten by man or devil. I was never afraid of Flint while he was alive, and I won't be afraid of him now that he's dead. And listen, that voice had an echo. Nobody ever saw a ghost with an echo. Now that I think about it, that voice sounded like Ben Gunn's!"

The pirates laughed with relief, and they marched on faster than before. At last they found the tall tree, and they started running towards it, shouting with excitement. Then, suddenly, they stopped. In front of them there was a great hole in the ground. It was certainly not new, because grass had grown on its sides. At the bottom lay a broken pickaxe and some boards from a packing case. On one of them, I could read the name of Flint's ship, the Walrus. The treasure had gone.

The pirates jumped into the hole and dug desperately with their fingers, and one man held up a single gold coin. "Two guineas!" he roared. "Is that your seven hundred thousand pounds, Silver? You've ruined us all!"

Silver quietly passed me a pistol. The five pirates climbed out of the pit on the opposite side and faced us. "That's the man who ruined us," said one of them, pointing furiously at Silver. "And there's the boy. Now, mates..."

He raised his arm to lead the attack, but at that moment three shots flashed out of the trees. Two of the pirates fell, and the other three turned and ran for their lives. A moment later, the doctor, Gray and Ben Gunn came out of the bushes with smoking muskets.

"Thank you, Doctor," said Silver, wiping his forehead. "You came just in time for me and Hawkins. And so it's you, Ben Gunn!"

"I'm Ben Gunn, I am," said the marooned man, looking embarrassed. "And how do you do, Mr Silver? Pretty well, I thank you, says you."

Then the doctor explained the whole mystery to me. During his long, lonely years on the island, Ben Gunn had found the treasure. He had dug it up and carried it, piece by piece, to a cave near the north-east corner of the island. That was why the doctor had given the useless map to Silver. It was also Ben Gunn's voice that had frightened the pirates in the woods. The doctor had left the stockade because it was close to the marshes and full of fever. Instead, they had all moved to Ben Gunn's cave, where there was plenty of fresh goat meat and water.

That evening, in the cave, I saw the treasure at last. There were enormous heaps of coins and piles of gold bars, which glittered in the light of the fire. The coins came from England, France, Spain and Portugal, and from many countries that I had never heard of. When I thought of the blood and sorrow that Flint's treasure had cost, I did not feel very happy.

For several days, we carried the gold down to the beach and loaded it onto the Hispaniola. The three remaining pirates did not attack us again. Finally we decided to leave them on the island with food, medicine, tools, powder and muskets, and a note explaining where to find us. It was a hard decision, but we could not take them with us.

Silver came with us as a prisoner, but we did not tie him up. He was so polite and helpful that it was difficult to remember that he was a murderer. We stopped at a port in South America to find new sailors. There, one night, he escaped in a small boat with a bag of coins worth three or four hundred guineas. To be honest, we were all glad to be rid of him so cheaply.

We arrived in Bristol at last, and each of us received a generous share of the treasure. Captain Smollett retired from the sea. Gray saved his money, studied hard and became an officer. Ben Gunn received a thousand pounds, which he spent or lost in three weeks. After that, the squire gave him a job as a lodge-keeper, and he sings in the church choir on Sundays.

Of Silver, we have heard nothing more. Perhaps he found his old wife, and perhaps he lives comfortably with her and with Captain Flint, his parrot. I hope so, because his chances of comfort in the next world are very small.

The bars of silver and the weapons still lie where Flint buried them, but nothing in the world could take me back to that terrible island. Sometimes, in my worst dreams, I hear the waves breaking on its coast. Then I wake up suddenly in my bed, with the sharp voice of Captain Flint still ringing in my ears: "Pieces of eight! Pieces of eight!"

The End
