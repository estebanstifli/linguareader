Chapter 1: There Is No One Left

When Mary Lennox was sent to Misselthwaite Manor to live with her uncle, everybody said she was the most disagreeable-looking child they had ever seen. It was true: she had a thin little face and a thin little body, thin light hair and an unhappy expression. Her hair was yellow, and her face was yellow too, because she had been born in India and had always been ill in one way or another.

Her father had held a position under the English Government and was always busy. Her mother was a great beauty who cared only about parties and pretty dresses. She had not wanted a little girl at all. When Mary was born, she gave her to an Indian nurse, called an Ayah, and told her to keep the child out of sight. So Mary grew up as a sickly, fretful, ugly little baby, and then as a sickly, fretful, ugly little girl who always had her own way.

The servants obeyed her in everything, because if Mary cried, her mother would be angry. By the time she was six years old, she was the most selfish little pig in the world. She never thanked anyone, and she never said please. When she was angry, she slapped her Ayah's face.

One very hot morning, when she was about nine, Mary woke up feeling cross. The woman by her bed was not her Ayah.

"Why did you come?" she said to the strange woman. "I will not let you stay, so send my Ayah to me."

The woman looked frightened, and she only said that the Ayah could not come. Mary flew into a rage and kicked her, but the woman just repeated that the Ayah could not come.

There was something strange in the air that morning. Nothing was done in its usual way. Several of the servants seemed to be missing, and the others were running about with grey, scared faces, but nobody told Mary anything. At last she wandered into the garden and began to play by herself under a tree. She stuck big red flowers into little piles of earth and pretended she was making a garden of her own. All the time she was getting angrier.

"Pig, pig, daughter of pigs!" she said to herself, thinking of her Ayah.

Then she heard her mother's voice, and she saw her standing on the veranda with a young officer. She looked very pale, and her pretty eyes were wide with fear.

"Is it so very bad?" she asked.

"Awfully," the young man answered in a trembling voice. "You ought to have gone to the hills two weeks ago."

At that moment a loud sound of crying came from the servants' huts. Mrs Lennox caught the young man's arm.

"Oh, what is it?" she cried in terror.

"Someone has died," said the officer, "and you did not tell me it had broken out among your servants."

"I did not know!" cried Mrs Lennox. "Come with me, come with me!" and she turned and ran into the house.

After that, terrible things happened: the cholera had broken out in its most deadly form, and people were dying like flies. The Ayah had been taken ill in the night, and it was because she had just died that the servants had cried so loudly. Before the next day, three more servants were dead and the others had run away in terror.

During the confusion of the second day, Mary hid herself in her room, and everyone forgot her. She cried and slept, and slept and cried. Once she crept into the dining room and found it empty. There was a half-finished meal on the table. She ate some fruit and biscuits, and because she was thirsty, she drank a glass of wine. It made her very sleepy, and she went back to her room and lay down. She slept for a very long time.

When she woke, the house was perfectly still, and she had never known it so silent before. There were neither voices nor footsteps anywhere.

"I wonder who will take care of me now," she thought. "I hope they will get a new Ayah who knows some new stories."

She was not sad that her Ayah had died. She was not an affectionate child and had never cared much for anyone. She only felt that it was rude of them all to forget her.

Then she heard footsteps outside, the door opened, and two officers looked in.

"What a strange thing," said the younger one. "There is a child here, alone, in a place like this! Who is she?"

"I am Mary Lennox," the little girl said, stamping her foot. "I fell asleep when everyone had cholera, and I have only just woken up. Why does nobody come?"

"Poor little kid," said the older man, looking at her sadly, "there is nobody left to come."

It was in that strange and sudden way that Mary found out that she had neither father nor mother left. They had both died in the night, and the few servants who had not died had left the house as quickly as they could. That was why the place was so quiet. There was no one in the bungalow but herself and a little rustling snake.

At first Mary was sent to stay with an English clergyman and his family, who were very poor and had five noisy children. Mary hated their untidy house, and she was so disagreeable that after a few days nobody would play with her. The children gave her a nickname, and they called her "Mistress Mary Quite Contrary", after an old rhyme, and they sang it at her until she was furious.

"You are going to be sent home," Basil, the eldest boy, told her one day, "to your uncle. His name is Mr Archibald Craven, and he lives in a great big lonely old house in the country. He is so cross that he will not let people see him. He has a crooked back, and he is horrid."

"I don't believe you," said Mary, and she put her fingers in her ears.

But it was true. A few weeks later, Mary sailed to England with an officer's wife who was taking her own children to boarding school. In London she was met by Mrs Medlock, the housekeeper at Misselthwaite Manor. Mrs Medlock was a stout woman with very red cheeks and sharp black eyes. She wore a purple dress and a black bonnet with purple flowers on it.

Mary did not like her at all, but then Mary very seldom liked anyone, and it was quite clear that Mrs Medlock did not think much of Mary either.

"My word, she is a plain little piece of goods," Mrs Medlock said to someone at the station. "And we had heard that her mother was a beauty."

The next morning they took the train to Yorkshire. Mary sat in her corner of the carriage, looking plain and fretful, with her thin black-gloved hands folded in her lap.

Chapter 2: Across the Moor

For a long time Mrs Medlock read her newspaper, and Mary looked out of the window at the grey rain. At last the housekeeper folded the paper and began to talk, perhaps because she was tired of the silence.

"I suppose I may as well tell you something about where you are going," she said. "Do you know anything about your uncle?"

"No," said Mary.

"Did you never hear your father and mother talk about him?"

"No," said Mary, frowning, because her father and mother had never talked to her about anything in particular.

"Well, you are going to a queer place," said Mrs Medlock. "The house is six hundred years old, and it is on the edge of the moor. There are nearly a hundred rooms in it, though most of them are shut up and locked. There are pictures and fine old furniture, and there is a big park round it, with gardens and trees, but there is nothing else."

"Mr Craven is not going to bother about you, that is certain. He never bothers about anyone, and he has a crooked back, and that set him wrong. He was a sour young man until he married. His wife was a sweet, pretty thing, and he would have walked the world over to get her a blade of grass she wanted. When she died, it made him stranger than ever. He cares about nobody and will not see people, and most of the time he goes away. When he is at Misselthwaite, he shuts himself up in the west wing."

It sounded like a story in a book, and it did not make Mary feel cheerful. A house with a hundred rooms, nearly all shut up, on the edge of a moor, whatever a moor was. A man with a crooked back who shut himself up.

"You must not expect to see him," Mrs Medlock went on. "And you must not expect that there will be people to talk to you. You will have to play about and look after yourself. You will be told which rooms you can go into and which you must keep out of. There are gardens enough, but when you are in the house, do not go wandering and poking about."

"I shall not want to go poking about," said sour little Mary. She turned her face to the window and soon fell asleep.

When she woke, it was dark, and the train had stopped at a small station, and a carriage was waiting for them outside. They drove for a long time, and Mary saw a few cottages with lights in the windows, and then there was nothing but darkness. A strong wind was blowing, and it made a low, wild, rushing sound.

"What is that?" asked Mary. "It is not the sea, is it, Mrs Medlock?"

"No, not the sea," answered Mrs Medlock, "nor fields nor mountains. It is just miles and miles of wild land that nothing grows on but heather and gorse and broom, and nothing lives on it but wild ponies and sheep. That is the moor."

"I don't like it at all," said Mary to herself.

At last the carriage stopped in front of a huge, long, low house. Most of its windows were dark. The front door was made of great oak panels, and it opened into an enormous hall with portraits on the walls and suits of armour in the corners. Mary looked very small and lost in her black dress as she stood on the stone floor.

A thin old man stood near the servant who had opened the door.

"You are to take her to her room," he said in a husky voice. "He does not want to see her. He is going to London in the morning."

"Very well, Mr Pitcher," Mrs Medlock answered.

Mary was led up a broad staircase and down a long corridor, then up some steps and through another corridor, and another. At last a door opened into a room with a fire in it and supper on a table.

"Well, here you are," said Mrs Medlock. "This room and the next are where you will live, and you must keep to them, so don't forget that!"

And so Mistress Mary arrived at Misselthwaite Manor. Perhaps she had never felt quite so contrary in all her life.

Chapter 3: Martha

When Mary opened her eyes in the morning, a young housemaid was kneeling by the fireplace, raking out the ashes. Mary lay and watched her for a while. Then she looked out of the window and saw a great stretch of land that seemed to have no trees on it. It looked like an endless, dull, purplish sea.

"What is that?" she asked, pointing at the window.

The housemaid, whose name was Martha, stood up and looked.

"That's the moor," she said with a friendly grin. "Do you like it?"

"No," answered Mary, "I hate it."

"That's because you're not used to it," said Martha, going back to the fire. "You think it's too big and bare now, but you'll like it. I just love it, because it's lovely in spring and summer, when the gorse and broom and heather are in flower. It smells of honey, and there's such a lot of fresh air. I wouldn't live away from the moor for anything."

Mary listened to her with a puzzled expression. The servants she had known in India were not like this at all. They never talked to their masters as if they were equals. They bowed and called them "protector of the poor". This girl was a round, rosy, good-natured Yorkshire girl, and she did not seem to be afraid of anyone.

"Are you going to be my servant?" asked Mary.

"I'm Mrs Medlock's servant," said Martha stoutly. "But I'm to do the housemaid's work up here and wait on you a bit. But you won't need much waiting on."

"Who is going to dress me?" demanded Mary.

Martha sat up on her heels and stared. "Canna' tha' dress thysen?" she said.

"What do you mean, I don't understand your language," said Mary.

"Eh, I forgot," said Martha. "Mrs Medlock told me I'd have to be careful or you wouldn't know what I was saying. I mean, can't you put on your own clothes?"

"No," answered Mary, quite angrily, "I never did in my life, because my Ayah dressed me, of course."

"Well," said Martha, "it's time you learned. My mother always says she can't see why grand people's children don't turn out fools, with nurses washing them and dressing them as if they were puppies!"

Mary was so angry that she threw herself face down on the pillows and burst into tears. Martha was a little frightened, and very sorry.

"Eh, you mustn't cry like that," she said. "I didn't mean to make you angry, and you're right to be cross. I beg your pardon, Miss, so do stop crying."

There was something comfortable and friendly in her strange Yorkshire talk, and slowly Mary stopped. Martha went on talking while she laid the breakfast. She told Mary that she was one of twelve children. They lived in a little cottage on the moor, and her father earned only sixteen shillings a week. Her mother had a hard time getting porridge for all of them.

"There's our Dickon," she said, "he's twelve years old, and he's always out on the moor. He's got a young pony he calls his own. He found it on the moor when it was little, and it follows him about. Dickon's kind to animals, and they all like him. The birds come and eat out of his hand."

Mary had never had a pet of her own. She began to feel a slight interest in Dickon, and since she had never been interested in anyone but herself before, it was the beginning of a healthy feeling.

After breakfast, Martha told her to put on her coat and go out to play.

"If you go round that way, you'll come to the gardens," she said, pointing. "There's lots of flowers in summer, but there's nothing blooming now." She stopped for a second, then added, "One of the gardens is locked up. No one has been in it for ten years."

"Why?" asked Mary, in spite of herself.

"Mr Craven had it shut when his wife died so sudden. He won't let anyone go inside. It was her garden, so he locked the door and dug a hole and buried the key. There's Mrs Medlock's bell ringing, and I must run."

Chapter 4: The Robin

Mary went out into the grounds. She did not want to play, but she wanted to see the garden that had been shut up for ten years. She wondered what it was like and whether there were any flowers still alive in it.

She walked along a path and through a door in a wall into a big garden full of vegetable beds. There were bare fruit trees trained flat against the walls. An old man with a spade was digging in one of the beds. He looked up at her with a surly face.

"What is this place?" asked Mary.

"One of the kitchen gardens," the old man answered without looking up.

"What is that?" said Mary, pointing through another green door.

"Another of them, and there's another on the other side of the wall, and there's the orchard on the other side of that."

Mary wandered through the gardens and the orchard, but she found no door into a garden that was locked. Then, from the top of a tree on the other side of a high wall, she heard a bird singing. It was a robin redbreast with a bright red breast, and its cheerful little whistle seemed to call to her. She stood and listened until it flew away.

When she went back to the kitchen garden, the old man was still digging.

"There is a tree in there," she said to him, pointing at the wall. "I saw the tops of the trees above it, and a bird with a red breast was sitting on one of them, singing. But there is no door anywhere."

The old man stopped digging. To her surprise, a slow smile spread over his face, and he looked quite different. He turned towards the orchard and began to whistle a low, soft whistle. Almost at once there was a little rushing flight through the air, and the bird with the red breast flew down and landed on the earth near the gardener's foot.

"Here he is," chuckled the old man, and he spoke to the bird as if he were speaking to a child. "Where has tha' been, tha' cheeky little beggar? I've not seen thee all day."

The robin put his head on one side and looked up at him with his soft bright eye, which was like a black dewdrop. He hopped about and pecked the earth, looking for seeds and insects.

"Will he always come when you call him?" asked Mary, almost in a whisper.

"Aye, that he will, for I've known him since he was a little thing. He came out of the nest in the other garden. When he flew over the wall, he was too weak to fly back, so we got friendly. When he went back over the wall, the rest of the family had gone, and he was lonely, and he came back to me."

"I'm lonely," said Mary, who had not realised before that this was one of the things that made her feel sour and cross.

The old gardener looked at her for a minute. "Then tha' and me are a good bit alike," he said. "We're neither of us good-looking, and we're both of us as sour as we look. We've got the same nasty tempers, both of us, I'll warrant."

Nobody had ever spoken to Mary so plainly before. She wondered if she really looked as sour as old Ben Weatherstaff, for that was the gardener's name. She also wondered if her temper was as nasty as his, and she felt quite uncomfortable.

Suddenly the robin flew up into an apple tree near them and burst into song.

"What did he do that for?" asked Ben.

"I don't know," said Mary.

"He has made up his mind to make friends with thee," replied Ben. "He's taken a fancy to thee."

"To me?" said Mary, and she moved softly towards the little tree and looked up. "Would you make friends with me?" she said to the robin, just as if she were speaking to a person. "Would you?" And she did not say it in her hard little voice. She said it in a voice so soft and eager that Ben Weatherstaff was as surprised as she had been when she heard him whistle.

But the robin spread his wings and flew away over the wall.

"He has flown over the wall into the orchard. He lives in the garden where there is no door, doesn't he?" said Mary.

"There's no such garden," said Ben shortly. "Don't you go poking your nose where it's no cause to go. Get on and play, for I've no more time." And he threw his spade over his shoulder and walked off, without even saying goodbye.

Chapter 5: The Cry in the Corridor and the Key

At first each day was exactly like the others. Every morning Mary woke in her tapestried room and found Martha kneeling by the fire. Every morning she ate her breakfast in the nursery, which had nothing amusing in it. And after each breakfast she looked out of the window at the huge moor, and then she went outside.

She did not know that this was the best thing she could have done. When she ran quickly along the paths to keep warm, the wind from the moor stirred her blood and filled her lungs with fresh air. She did not know that it was making her stronger. But after a few days she woke up hungry, and for the first time she ate all her porridge.

In the evenings she sat by the fire and listened to Martha's stories about the cottage, about her mother, and about Dickon and his animals. One night, while the wind was howling round the house, Mary heard another sound. It was a far-off, crying sound, somewhere inside the house.

"Do you hear anyone crying?" she asked.

Martha looked confused. "No," she answered, "it's the wind. Sometimes it sounds like somebody lost on the moor."

"But listen," said Mary, "it's in the house, down one of those long corridors."

At that very moment a door must have opened somewhere downstairs, because a strong draught blew along the passage. The crying was heard more plainly than ever.

"There, I told you so!" said Mary. "It is someone crying, and it isn't a grown-up person."

Martha ran and shut the door. "It was the wind," she said stubbornly. "And if it wasn't, it was little Betty Butterworth, the scullery maid. She's had the toothache all day."

But something troubled and awkward in her manner made Mary stare very hard at her. She did not believe she was speaking the truth.

The next day the rain poured down in torrents, and Mary could not go out. Martha had gone home to the cottage for her day off. Mary sat alone for a while, and then a new idea came into her head. There were a hundred rooms in the house, and most of them were shut up. She decided to go and look at them. Nobody had told her she must not.

She wandered for hours, up and down narrow staircases and along wide corridors hung with pictures. In one room she found a cabinet full of tiny ivory elephants, and she played with them for a long time. In another, on an old velvet cushion, she found a hole, and inside the hole a mother mouse was sleeping with six baby mice.

"If they wouldn't be so frightened, I would take them back with me," she thought.

On her way back she got lost, and while she was standing still, wondering which way to go, she heard the crying again, nearer this time. She put her hand on a tapestry on the wall, and the tapestry moved, because it was covering a door. At that moment Mrs Medlock came out of it with her keys in her hand and a very cross look on her face.

"What are you doing here?" she said, and she took Mary by the arm. "What did I tell you about poking about?"

"I turned round the wrong corner," explained Mary, "and I heard someone crying."

"You heard nothing of the sort!" said the housekeeper. "You come along back to your own nursery, or I'll box your ears." And she half pushed, half pulled her up one passage and down another.

Mary sat by the fire, pale with rage. "There was someone crying, there was, there was!" she said to herself.

When Martha came back the next morning, she brought a present from her mother. It was a skipping rope with red and blue handles. Mary had never seen one before, and she asked what it was for.

"For!" cried Martha, "do you mean they've no skipping ropes in India, for all they've got elephants and tigers and camels? Just watch me," and she ran into the middle of the room and began to skip and skip, while Mary turned in her chair to stare at her.

Mary took the rope outside and skipped round the gardens until her cheeks were red, and she had never felt so alive. When she skipped into the walk by the long wall covered with ivy, the robin was there, hopping on a heap of freshly dug earth, looking for a worm.

Mary stopped and looked at the earth, where something was almost buried. It was a ring of rusty iron or brass. When she picked it up, she saw that it was an old key, which looked as if it had been buried for a long time.

Mistress Mary stood and looked at it with an almost frightened face.

"Perhaps it has been buried for ten years," she said in a whisper. "Perhaps it is the key to the garden!"

She put it in her pocket, because if she could find the door, she would carry the key with her every day.

The next morning she went to the walk again. The robin was there, singing on top of the wall. Just then a strong gust of wind swung aside some loose ivy that was hanging from the wall. Mary jumped forward and caught it in her hand. Underneath it there was a round knob: the knob of a door.

Her heart began to thump, and her hands shook. She pushed back the ivy and found the lock. She took the key out of her pocket and put it in the keyhole. It took both hands to turn it, but it did turn.

Then she took a long breath and looked behind her to see if anyone was coming, but no one was. She held back the swinging curtain of ivy and pushed the door, which opened slowly, slowly.

Then she slipped through it, shut it behind her, and stood with her back against it. She was standing inside the secret garden.

Chapter 6: Dickon

It was the sweetest, most mysterious-looking place anyone could imagine. The high walls were covered with the leafless stems of climbing roses. There were rose bushes too, and other roses had spread from tree to tree, making lovely bridges of their own. There were no leaves or flowers on them now, and Mary did not know if they were dead or alive. A grey mist of branches hung over everything.

"How still it is!" she whispered, "and I am the first person who has spoken in here for ten years."

She walked softly, as if she were afraid of waking someone. Then she saw some little pale green points pushing up through the earth.

"Yes, they are tiny growing things," she said. "It isn't quite a dead garden, because even if the roses are dead, there are other things alive."

She did not know anything about gardening, but the grass seemed so thick round the green points that she thought they did not have room to grow. She found a sharp piece of wood and knelt down and dug and weeded out the grass until she had made nice little clear places round them. She worked for two or three hours and only stopped when it was time for her midday dinner.

"Martha," she said that evening, "if I had a spade, I could make a little garden and plant seeds in it."

Martha's face lit up at once. "Our Dickon can get you a spade and some seeds," she said. "There's a shop in Thwaite that sells little garden sets. I'll write to him, and he'll bring them."

A few days later, when Mary came round a laurel hedge, she heard a strange low whistling sound. A boy was sitting under a tree, playing on a rough wooden pipe. He was a funny-looking boy of about twelve, with a turned-up nose, cheeks as red as poppies, and very round blue eyes. A brown squirrel was clinging to the tree and watching him. Two rabbits were sitting up and sniffing near him, and a pheasant was stretching its neck from behind a bush. It seemed as if they were all coming to listen to the music.

When he saw Mary, he held up his hand and spoke to her in a voice almost as low as his whistling.

"Don't you move," he said, "or it would frighten them."

He stood up very slowly, and the squirrel ran back into the tree, the pheasant drew in its head, and the rabbits dropped on all fours and hopped away.

"I'm Dickon," the boy said, "and I know you're Miss Mary."

Then Mary understood that she had known at once that he was Dickon. Who else could charm rabbits and pheasants as the natives charm snakes in India?

He had brought the spade and a fork and a rake, and some packets of flower seeds. He told her their names and how to plant them. He talked about them as if they were his friends. Mary liked him at once, and she felt that he liked her. After a while she looked at him seriously.

"Dickon," she said, "can you keep a secret, if I tell you one? It's a great secret, and I don't know what I should do if anyone found it out. I believe I should die!"

"I'm keeping secrets all the time," he said. "If I couldn't keep secrets from the other lads, about foxes' cubs and birds' nests, there'd be nothing safe on the moor. Aye, I can keep secrets."

Mary did not mean to put out her hand and clutch his sleeve, but she did.

"I've stolen a garden," she said very fast. "It isn't mine, and it isn't anybody's. Nobody wants it, nobody cares for it, nobody ever goes into it. Perhaps everything is dead in it already, I don't know."

She took him to the door under the ivy, and they went in together. Dickon looked round and round, his eyes getting bigger and bigger.

"Eh," he almost whispered, "it is a queer, pretty place, it's like as if a body was in a dream."

"Will there be roses?" asked Mary. "Can you tell, or are they all dead?"

Dickon went to the nearest tree and took out his knife. He cut a dry, grey branch, and inside it was greenish and juicy.

"This one is as wick as you or me," he said.

"Wick?" said Mary. "What does that mean?"

"It means alive," Dickon explained, "and when a thing is wick, it has a life in it. The dead branches we'll cut away, but there's a lot of wick ones. There'll be a fountain of roses here this summer."

They went from bush to bush and from tree to tree. Dickon showed her how to use the spade, and how to cut away the dead wood, and how to clear the earth round the roots. The robin followed them everywhere, and he seemed very interested in all their work.

When the clock struck the hour of her midday dinner, Mary did not want to leave.

"Whatever happens," she said, "you will never tell?"

"If you was a missel thrush and showed me where your nest was, do you think I'd tell anyone? Not me," he said, smiling his wide smile. "You're as safe as a missel thrush."

In the weeks that followed, Mary spent almost every hour of daylight in her secret garden, and the change in her was extraordinary. She worked with her spade and her rake until her arms ached, digging and weeding and clearing the earth round the delicate green points, which were becoming more numerous every day. Whenever Dickon came, he brought new seeds and young plants from his mother's cottage, and he taught her the names of everything: crocuses, snowdrops, daffodils, poppies, lilies and roses of every colour. He explained that some flowers were annuals, which grew from seed and died in a single summer, while others came back year after year from their roots and bulbs under the ground.

Mary listened to him with more curiosity than she had ever felt about anything in her life. In India she had been bored and irritable, lying about in the heat and refusing to learn her lessons. Now she was too busy and too interested to remember to be disagreeable. Her thin arms and legs were becoming stronger, her yellow face was getting a little colour in it, and her dull hair had begun to look thicker and brighter. She ate enormous meals of porridge and bread and meat. Mrs Medlock noticed with satisfaction that the child was not quite so plain and sour as she had been when she arrived.

"She's getting fatter," Martha told Mrs Medlock proudly one evening. "And she doesn't look so yellow, so she looks like a different child already."

The robin watched all their work with tremendous attention. He followed Mary from bed to bed, hopping beside her spade and waiting for worms. Sometimes he sat on the handle of her rake as if he were the gardener and she were his assistant. Mary decided that he understood everything she said to him, and she told him her secret thoughts in a low, confidential voice when nobody else was near.

Chapter 7: A Bit of Earth

One day, when Mary went back to her room, Martha was waiting for her with news.

"Mr Craven came back this morning," she said, "and I think he wants to see you. Mrs Medlock is going to take you to him after dinner."

Mary turned pale, because she was afraid that he would find out about the garden and take it away from her.

Mr Craven was sitting in an armchair before the fire. He was not really a hunchback, but he had high, rather crooked shoulders, and black hair streaked with white. His face would have been handsome if it had not been so miserable. When he saw her, he looked as if he did not know what to do with her.

"Are you well?" he asked, and Mary answered that she was.

"They take good care of you? You are very thin."

"I am getting fatter," Mary answered stiffly.

"I forgot you," he said in a tired voice. "I meant to send you a governess or a nurse, but I forgot."

"Please," began Mary. "Please, I am too big for a nurse. And please, please don't make me have a governess yet."

"That is what the Sowerby woman said," he muttered. Martha's mother had met him in the village and had told him that the child needed fresh air and freedom more than lessons. "What do you want to do?" he asked.

"I want to play out of doors," Mary answered, and she hoped her voice did not tremble. "I never liked it in India, but it makes me hungry here, and I am getting fatter."

"Play out of doors as much as you like," he said. "It is a big place, and you may go where you like. Is there anything you want, toys or books or dolls?"

"Might I," said Mary, "might I have a bit of earth?"

In her eagerness she did not realise how strange the words would sound. Mr Craven looked quite startled.

"Earth!" he repeated. "What do you mean by earth?"

"To plant seeds in, to make things grow, to see them come alive," Mary faltered.

He looked at her for a moment, and then passed his hand quickly over his eyes.

"Do you care about gardens so much?" he said slowly. "I knew someone once who loved growing things. When you see a bit of earth you want," he said, with something like a smile, "take it, child, and make it come alive."

"May I take it from anywhere, if it's not wanted?"

"Anywhere," he answered, "but you must go now, for I am tired. I shall be away all summer."

Mary flew back to her room. "I can have my garden!" she cried to Martha. "I may have it where I like! And there won't be a governess for a long time!"

That night Mary was woken by the rain beating against her window and the wind howling round the corners of the house. She could not sleep, and then she sat up in bed and turned her head towards the door, listening. It was the crying again.

"It isn't the wind now," she said in a loud whisper. "That isn't the wind, it is different. It is that crying I heard before."

She took her candle and went out of her room. The corridor was dark, but she was too excited to care. She went down the long passage and through the door behind the tapestry. The crying became louder, and at last she saw a glimmer of light coming from beneath a door. She pushed it open, and she was standing in a big room with old, handsome furniture in it.

There was a low fire glowing faintly, and a night light was burning by the side of a carved bed hung with brocade. On the bed was lying a boy, crying fretfully.

The boy had a sharp, delicate face the colour of ivory, and he seemed to have eyes too big for it. He had a lot of hair which fell over his forehead in heavy locks. He looked like a boy who had been ill, but he was crying more as if he were tired and cross than as if he were in pain.

Mary stood near the door with her candle in her hand, holding her breath. Then she crept across the room, and as she came nearer, the light caught the boy's attention. He turned his head on his pillow and stared at her. His grey eyes opened so wide that they seemed immense.

"Who are you?" he said at last, in a half-frightened whisper. "Are you a ghost?"

"No, I am not," Mary answered, and her own whisper sounded half frightened too. "Are you one?"

Chapter 8: Colin

He stared and stared and stared, and Mary could not help noticing what strange eyes he had. They were agate grey, and they looked too big for his face because they had black lashes all round them.

"No," he replied after waiting a moment or so. "I am Colin."

"Who is Colin?" she faltered nervously.

"I am Colin Craven, and who are you?"

"I am Mary Lennox, and Mr Craven is my uncle."

"He is my father," said the boy.

"Your father!" gasped Mary. "No one ever told me he had a boy, so why didn't they?"

"Come here," he said, and he put out his thin hand and touched her to make sure she was real. "Nobody dares to talk about me, and I won't let people see me and talk me over. I am ill like this all the time. If I live, I may be a hunchback, but I shan't live. My father hates to think I may be like him."

"Oh, what a queer house this is!" said Mary. "Everything is a kind of secret. Rooms are locked up and gardens are locked up, and you! Have you been locked up?"

"No, I stay in this room because I don't want to be moved out of it. It tires me too much, and my father won't let people talk about me, either. The servants are not allowed to speak of me. My mother died when I was born, and it makes him wretched to look at me. He thinks I don't know, but I've heard people talking."

He told her that he hardly ever went out of the room. Sometimes his nurse put him in a wheeled chair, but people stared at him, and he hated it. He had a doctor, Dr Craven, who was his father's cousin, and a great doctor from London had come once. All the servants had to do what he said, because if he was angry, he became ill.

"How old are you?" he asked suddenly.

"I am ten," answered Mary, "and so are you."

"How do you know that?" he demanded in a surprised voice.

"Because when you were born, the garden door was locked and the key was buried. And it has been locked for ten years."

Colin half sat up, turning towards her. "What garden door? Who locked it, and where was the key buried?" he exclaimed, as if he were suddenly very much interested.

"It was the garden Mr Craven hates," said Mary nervously. "He locked the door, and no one knew where he buried the key."

Colin wanted to know everything about it, and he asked question after question. Had she looked for the door, or asked the gardeners? Mary was careful, and she decided that she must not tell him her secret, not yet. She did not know what kind of boy he was.

"They would have to do what I want," Colin said. "I will make them take me there, and you can go too."

Mary's hands clutched each other, because everything would be spoiled.

"Oh, don't, don't, don't do that!" she cried. "If you make them open the door like that, it will never be a secret again."

"What do you mean by a secret?" he said, leaning forward. "Tell me."

Mary's words almost tumbled over one another. "You see," she said, "if no one knows but ourselves... If there was a door hidden somewhere under the ivy, and we found it and slipped through it together and shut it behind us, nobody would know we were inside. We could call it our garden and pretend that we were missel thrushes and it was our nest. Don't you see how much nicer it would be if it was a secret?"

He dropped back on his pillow and lay there with an odd expression on his face.

"I never had a secret," he said, "except that one about not living to grow up. They don't know I know that, so it is a sort of secret. But I like this kind of secret better."

Mary sat by his bed and told him about India, and about the moor, and about Dickon and his animals. She talked until his eyes grew heavy. Then she sang to him softly, the way her Ayah used to sing, until he fell asleep.

The next day it rained again, and Mary told Martha what had happened, and Martha was terrified.

"Eh, Miss Mary," she cried, "Mrs Medlock will think I told you, and I'll lose my place! What did he do, did he scream?"

"No," said Mary, "he was glad to see me. He wants me to come every day."

"You must have bewitched him!" she said.

Mary went to see Colin that day, and the next, and the next. They talked and laughed together, and at the end of a week Colin was asking for her as soon as he woke up. Mrs Medlock and Dr Craven were amazed, because the boy had never been so good-tempered. But Mary still did not tell him about the garden. She wanted to be sure first that he could keep a secret.

Then the rain stopped and the sun came out. For several days Mary was so busy in the garden with Dickon that she did not go to Colin at all.

Chapter 9: A Tantrum

On the first sunny afternoon, Mary came back from the garden with her cheeks glowing, and she went straight to Colin's room. He was lying on his sofa, and he did not even turn his head when she came in.

"Why didn't you get up?" said Mary.

"I did get up this morning, when I thought you were coming," he answered, without looking at her. "I made them put me back in bed this afternoon. My back ached and my head ached, and I was tired. Why didn't you come?"

"I was working in the garden with Dickon," said Mary.

Colin frowned and condescended to look at her. "I won't let that boy come here if you go and stay with him instead of coming to talk to me," he said.

Mary flew into a fine passion, for she could fly into a passion without making a noise. She just grew sour and obstinate and did not care what happened.

"If you send Dickon away, I'll never come into this room again!" she retorted.

"You'll have to if I want you," said Colin.

"I won't!" snapped Mary.

"I'll make you," said Colin, "they shall drag you in."

"Shall they, Mr Rajah!" said Mary fiercely. "They may drag me in, but they can't make me talk when they get me here. I'll sit and clench my teeth and never tell you one thing. I won't even look at you, I'll stare at the floor!"

They were a nice agreeable pair as they glared at each other. If they had been two little street boys, they would have sprung at each other and had a rough fight.

"You are a selfish thing!" cried Colin.

"What are you?" said Mary, "selfish people always say that, but you're the most selfish boy I ever saw."

"I'm not as selfish as you, because I'm always ill, and I'm sure there is a lump coming on my back," he said. "And I am going to die besides."

"You're not!" contradicted Mary unsympathetically. "You just say that to make people sorry for you. I believe you're proud of it."

Colin sat up among his pillows in a rage. "Get out of the room!" he shouted, and he threw his pillow at her.

Mary walked to the door, and when she reached it, she turned round and spoke again. "I was going to tell you all sorts of nice things," she said. "Dickon brought his fox and his rook, and I was going to tell you all about them. Now I won't tell you a single thing!"

She marched out of the door and closed it behind her. But by the time she reached her own room, she was beginning to feel sorry for him. She remembered his white face and his frightened eyes.

In the middle of the night she was awakened by such dreadful sounds that she jumped out of bed in an instant. The servants were running along the corridors, and someone was crying and screaming at the same time, screaming and crying in a horrible way.

"It's Colin," she said, "he's having one of those tantrums the nurse calls hysterics, and how awful it sounds."

She put her hands over her ears and felt sick and shivering. Then suddenly she was angry, far angrier than he was.

"Somebody ought to stop him, somebody ought to beat him!" she cried. She jumped up and ran along the corridor, and the nearer she got to the screaming, the higher her temper mounted. She slapped the door open with her hand and ran across the room to the bed.

"You stop!" she almost shouted. "You stop, I hate you, everybody hates you! I wish everybody would run out of the house and let you scream yourself to death! You will scream yourself to death in a minute, and I wish you would!"

A nice sympathetic child could neither have thought nor said such things, but it happened that the shock of hearing them was the best possible thing for this hysterical boy. Nobody had ever dared to speak to him like that before. He had been lying on his face, beating his pillow with his hands. He actually almost jumped round, so quickly did he turn at the sound of the furious little voice.

"I can't stop!" he gasped and sobbed. "I can't, I can't! I felt the lump, I felt it, I knew I should. I shall have a hunch on my back, and then I shall die."

"You didn't feel a lump!" contradicted Mary fiercely. "If you did, it was only a hysterical lump. Turn over and let me look at it."

The nurse and Mrs Medlock were standing near the door, staring at her with their mouths half open. The nurse came forward, half afraid, and pulled up Colin's nightgown. Mary looked up and down his poor thin back, with a solemn, savage little face. She looked as carefully as if she were a great doctor from London.

"There's not a single lump there!" she said at last. "There's not a lump as big as a pin, except backbone lumps, and you can only feel them because you're thin. I've got backbone lumps myself. There's not a lump as big as a pin! If you ever say there is again, I shall laugh!"

No one but Colin himself knew what effect those crossly spoken childish words had on him. For years he had lain alone with his secret terrors, and nobody had ever told him the truth. Now an angry, unsympathetic little girl insisted that he was not as ill as he thought, and he felt as if she might be speaking the truth.

"I didn't know," the nurse said quietly, "that he thought he had a lump on his spine. His back is weak because he won't try to sit up. I could have told him there was no lump there."

Colin's sobs gradually became quieter, and he turned his face to Mary and put out his hand towards her.

"Do you think I could live to grow up?" he asked.

"You probably will if you do what you are told and do not give way to your temper," the nurse said. "And you must stay out a great deal in the fresh air."

When the nurse and Mrs Medlock had gone, Colin pulled Mary's hand again. "I almost told you about the garden," Mary said. "Shall I tell you now? I have found the way in, and I have been inside it every day."

His eyes shone like stars as Mary told him about the door under the ivy, and the roses that were wick, and the green points coming up everywhere. She told him in a low soothing voice until he was asleep, holding her hand.

Chapter 10: Ben Weatherstaff on the Wall

Spring came quickly that year. Day after day the sun shone on the moor, the warm rain fell softly in the night, and every morning something new had happened in the secret garden. Tiny leaves unfolded on the branches, and the grey mist that had hung over the trees became a delicate green veil. Crocuses opened their purple and gold cups in the grass, and the daffodils pushed up their stiff leaves and then their nodding yellow heads. The roses that had seemed so dead in the winter were covered with little red buds. The air was filled with the humming of bees and the singing of birds who were busy building their nests.

Mary described all these wonderful things to Colin in the evenings, and he listened with shining eyes. He began to ask the same questions again and again, as if he could never hear enough. How big were the leaves today, had the robin finished his nest, and were the roses beginning to open? His imagination was so full of the garden that he forgot to think about his back or his illness. His nurse remarked that he had not had a single bad night for a fortnight.

With the spring came Dickon and his creatures. One morning he walked into Colin's room carrying a newborn lamb in his arms. A little red fox cub called Captain trotted at his side, a crow called Soot sat on his shoulder, and two squirrels, Nut and Shell, peeped out of his coat pockets. Colin had never seen anything like it. He held the lamb on his knees and fed it with a bottle, and when it fell asleep in his lap, he laughed with delight.

They began to make plans, and Colin gave orders that he was going out in his chair, and that all the gardeners must keep away from the paths near the walls. Nobody was to watch him, and the head gardener received the order with astonishment, but he had to obey it, because Colin's orders could not be refused.

On the first fine morning, Dickon pushed the wheeled chair, and Mary walked beside it. They went slowly along the paths, round the laurel hedge and along the walk by the ivy wall. Colin was so excited that he lay back with his eyes half closed. Then Mary pulled back the ivy, Dickon gave a strong, steady push, and the chair rolled through the door.

Colin covered his eyes with his hands and held them there until they were inside and the door was shut. Then he took them away and looked round and round, as Dickon and Mary had done before him.

Over the walls and the earth and the trees, a green veil of tender little leaves had crept. In the grass under the trees there were splashes of gold and purple and white flowers. The sun fell warm on his face like a hand with a lovely touch. Mary and Dickon stood and stared at him. He looked so strange and different, because a pink glow of colour had crept all over him.

"I shall get well, I shall get well!" he cried out. "Mary, Dickon, I shall get well! And I shall live for ever and ever and ever!"

They wheeled him under the plum tree, which was snow-white with blossom and musical with bees. Dickon showed him everything: the buds on the roses, a nest that the robin and his mate were building, the green shoots of the flowers that Mary had planted. Colin was so happy that he hardly spoke, but only looked and listened.

Then suddenly Colin half sat up, pointing to the wall.

"Who is that man?" he said in a quick, low voice.

Dickon and Mary turned round, and Ben Weatherstaff's angry face was glaring at them over the wall from the top of a ladder. He was shaking his fist at Mary.

"I never thought much of thee!" he cried. "I couldn't abide you the first time I set eyes on you. Always asking questions and poking your nose where it wasn't wanted. If it hadn't been for the robin, I'd never have..."

Then he saw who was in the wheeled chair, and his jaw dropped.

"Do you know who I am?" demanded Colin.

Old Ben stared at him with his red eyes as if he were looking at a ghost.

"Who are you?" he said in a shaking voice. "Aye, that I do, with your mother's eyes staring out of your face. You're the poor cripple."

Colin forgot that he had ever had a back. His face was scarlet, and he sat bolt upright.

"I'm not a cripple!" he cried out furiously. "I'm not!"

"He's not!" cried Mary, shouting up the wall in her fierce indignation. "He's not got a lump as big as a pin! I looked, and there was none, not one!"

"Haven't you got crooked legs?" asked Ben hoarsely.

That was too much, and Colin's anger and insulted pride made him forget everything but this one moment. He tore the rugs off his legs.

"Come here!" he shouted to Dickon. "Come here, this minute!"

Dickon was by his side in a second. Mary caught her breath in a short gasp and felt herself turn pale.

"He can do it, he can do it, he can, he can!" she whispered over and over again under her breath, as fast as ever she could.

There was a short fierce scramble: the rugs were thrown on the ground, Dickon was holding Colin's arm, the thin legs were out, the thin feet were on the grass. And Colin was standing upright, as straight as an arrow, looking strangely tall. His head was thrown back and his strange eyes were flashing lightning.

"Look at me!" he flung up at Ben Weatherstaff. "Just look at me, you, just look at me!"

Then Ben Weatherstaff did a queer thing. Tears ran down his wrinkled cheeks, and he struck his old hands together.

"Eh, the lies folk tell!" he burst forth. "You'll make a man yet, God bless you!"

"Come down at once," Colin ordered, "because I want to speak to you, and Mary will show you the door. You must not tell anyone about this."

When the old man came into the garden, he looked round in wonder. He told them a secret of his own. He had been very fond of Colin's mother. After the garden was locked, he had climbed over the wall once or twice a year to prune the roses, because she had once asked him to take care of them. That was why the roses had not all died.

That afternoon Colin planted a rose with his own hands, kneeling on the warm earth while Ben held it straight. The sun was setting and the sky was glowing when they went home. Colin had stood on his own feet, and he knew he would stand again.

Chapter 11: The Magic

After that, they went to the garden every day that it did not rain. Every morning Colin walked a little further, at first holding Dickon's arm, then holding a stick, and at last with nothing at all. Dickon's mother had told him that the best exercise was good, deep breathing and simple exercises, and Dickon taught them to Colin and Mary under the trees.

Colin decided that there was a kind of Magic in the garden. It made the leaves grow and the flowers open, and it was making him well.

"When I was going to try to stand that first time," he said, "Mary kept whispering very fast that I could do it, and I did. Of course, I had to try at the same time, but her Magic helped me."

So every morning they sat in a circle under the plum tree. Dickon's animals sat with them, and even old Ben Weatherstaff joined in, though he sometimes fell asleep. Colin sat in the middle, like a young priest, and chanted: "The sun is shining, the sun is shining, and that is the Magic. Being strong is the Magic, and the Magic is in me, the Magic is in me, it is in me, it is in me. It's in every one of us."

They decided to keep his progress a secret until his father came home. Colin wanted to walk into his father's study and say, "Here I am. I am like any other boy." So when Dr Craven or the nurse came, Colin lay on his sofa and complained, and he was carried out in his chair as before.

But there was one difficulty: they were always hungry now. Colin and Mary ate everything that was sent up to them, and they still wanted more. Dr Craven was puzzled to find the boy so well. Mrs Medlock could not understand why the trays came back empty, yet the children said they were not hungry.

"They're eating nothing at all, and yet they look fatter and rosier every day," she told the doctor.

The truth was simple: Dickon's mother had begun to send baskets to the garden every morning. There were new-laid eggs, fresh milk with cream on top, and hot currant buns wrapped in a clean blue cloth. Dickon showed them how to roast potatoes and eggs in a little oven of stones. The children laughed so much over their secret breakfasts that they had to hide their faces in the cushions of the chair.

"They're getting so fat on it," said Martha, "that they're as round as a pair of puddings."

One day, Dickon brought his mother into the garden. Mrs Sowerby was a tall woman with a kind, rosy face and the same wonderful blue eyes as Dickon. She looked round at the roses and the flowers and the happy children.

"Bless thee, lad," she said to Colin, holding his hand. "Your mother would be glad to see you. She'd be very proud of you."

"Do you think my father will like me?" asked Colin suddenly. He had been thinking about this for days.

"Aye, for sure he will," she answered warmly. "He must come home now, he must come home."

"Do you believe in Magic?" asked Colin.

"That I do, lad," she said, "though I never knew it by that name, but what does the name matter? The same thing that makes the seeds swell and the sun shine made you a well lad. It's the Good Thing, so don't stop believing in it."

Then she told them stories and sang with them, and they all laughed so loudly that the robin flew up into the tree to see what was happening.

Chapter 12: In the Garden

While the secret garden was coming alive, Mr Archibald Craven was wandering about the beautiful places of Europe. For ten years he had been travelling, but wherever he went, he carried his dark thoughts with him. He had climbed the highest mountains and walked in the loveliest valleys, but nothing had made him happy. He never thought about his son at all, except to remember that he was weak and ill.

One day he was walking in a beautiful valley in Austria, and he sat down by a stream to rest. It was a clear little stream, running between banks of thick green moss. He looked at a mass of little blue flowers growing beside it, and for the first time in many years he found that he was seeing something lovely.

Something seemed to unwind in his heart, and he felt strangely calm. "What is it?" he whispered, and he passed his hand over his forehead. "I almost feel as if I were alive."

He did not know it, but at that very moment, far away at Misselthwaite, Colin was standing in the secret garden and crying out, "I am going to live for ever and ever and ever!"

A few weeks later, he was staying beside a lake in Italy. One night he fell into a deep sleep and dreamed. In his dream he heard a voice calling him. It was sweet and clear and happy and far away. It seemed very far, but it was as clear as if it had been at his very side.

"Archie, Archie, Archie!" it said, and then again, sweeter and clearer than before, "Archie, Archie!"

He thought he sprang to his feet. "Lilias, Lilias!" he answered. "Lilias, where are you?"

"In the garden," came back the voice, like a sound from a golden flute. "In the garden!"

And then the dream ended, and when he woke in the morning, a letter was waiting for him on his breakfast tray. It was written in a plain, careful hand.

"Dear Sir," it said, "I am Susan Sowerby that made bold to speak to you once on the moor. It was about Miss Mary I spoke. I will make bold to speak again. Please, sir, I would come home if I was you. I think you would be glad to come, and if you will excuse me, sir, I think your lady would ask you to come if she was here. Your obedient servant, Susan Sowerby."

"I will go back to Misselthwaite," he said, "and I will go at once."

During the long journey home he thought about his son for the first time in ten years. He remembered the night the child was born and his wife died. He had refused to see the baby, and he had been like a madman. Perhaps he had been wrong, all these years.

When he reached the manor, Mrs Medlock met him, looking excited and confused.

"How is Master Colin?" he asked immediately.

"Well, sir," she answered, "he's different, in a manner of speaking. He's out in the garden, as he always is now, and he won't let anybody go near him."

"In the garden!" said Mr Craven, and he went out of the room at once.

He took the walk by the long ivy wall, and he walked slowly, with his eyes on the ground. He felt as if he were being drawn back to the place he had so long forsaken. He knew where the door was, even though the ivy hung thickly over it.

He stopped and stood quite still, listening. He heard running feet scuffling round and round under the trees, and strange sounds of lowered, suppressed voices, exclamations and smothered cries of joy. It sounded like the laughter of young things who were trying not to be heard. Then the feet ran faster and faster towards the door, and the door in the wall was flung wide open.

A boy burst through it at full speed and, without seeing the outsider, dashed almost into his arms.

He was a tall boy and a handsome one. He was glowing with life, and his running had sent splendid colour into his face. He threw the thick hair back from his forehead and lifted a pair of strange grey eyes, full of boyish laughter and rimmed with black lashes like a fringe. It was the eyes that made Mr Craven gasp for breath.

"Who... what, who?" he stammered, staring at the boy.

This was not what Colin had planned, but perhaps it was better. He had won a race, and he drew himself up to his full height.

"Father," he said, "I'm Colin. You can't believe it, and I scarcely can myself. I'm Colin."

"In the garden, in the garden!" repeated Mr Craven, like a man in a dream.

"Yes," hurried on Colin, "it was the garden that did it, and Mary and Dickon and the creatures, and the Magic. No one knows, because we kept it to tell you when you came. I'm well, I can beat Mary in a race. I'm going to be an athlete."

Mr Craven's soul shook with unbelieving joy. He put his hands on the boy's shoulders and held him still. He knew he dared not even try to speak for a moment.

"Take me into the garden, my boy," he said at last. "And tell me all about it."

So they led him inside, and the place was a wilderness of autumn gold and purple and violet and flaming scarlet. The late roses climbed and hung and clustered, and the sunshine made the yellowing trees look like a golden temple. Mr Craven stood silent, just as the children had done when they first came into its greyness.

"I thought it would be dead," he said.

"Mary thought so at first," said Colin. "But it came alive."

They sat down under a tree, and Colin told the whole story, while Mary and Dickon listened and laughed. He told about the robin and the key, the crying in the corridor, the tantrum, the Magic and the secret breakfasts. Mr Craven laughed until tears came into his eyes, and sometimes there were tears in his eyes when he was not laughing.

"Now," said Colin at the end, "it need not be a secret any more. I am never going to get into the chair again. I shall walk back with you, Father, to the house."

That afternoon, the servants at Misselthwaite saw something that none of them had ever expected to see. Mrs Medlock and Martha were looking out of a window, and they saw Mr Craven coming across the lawn from the gardens. He looked as many of them had never seen him. And by his side, with his head up in the air and his eyes full of laughter, walked as strongly and steadily as any boy in Yorkshire, Master Colin.

The End
