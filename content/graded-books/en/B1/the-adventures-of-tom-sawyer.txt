Chapter 1: Tom and Aunt Polly

"Tom! Where is that boy? TOM!"

There was no answer. Aunt Polly pulled her glasses down and looked over them around the room, and then she pushed them up and looked under them. She almost never looked through them, because they were her best glasses and she was proud of them, but she could see just as well through a pair of stove lids.

She bent down and pushed a broom under the bed, breathing hard, but she found nothing except the cat. Then she heard a small noise behind her. She turned just in time to catch a boy by the back of his jacket as he tried to escape through the door.

"There! I should have thought of that cupboard," she said. "What have you been doing in there? Look at your hands and look at your mouth. That's jam, isn't it? I've told you forty times to leave that jam alone. Give me that stick."

The stick rose in the air, and the boy stared over her shoulder with wide, frightened eyes. "Oh! Look behind you, Aunt!"

The old lady turned quickly and lifted her skirts out of danger. In that moment the boy ran out of the door, climbed the high wooden fence and disappeared over the top of it.

Aunt Polly stood still for a moment, and then she began to laugh quietly. "That boy! Will I never learn? He has played tricks like that on me a hundred times, and I still believe every one of them. He's my own dead sister's child, poor thing, and I haven't got the heart to hit him. Every time I let him go, my conscience hurts me, and every time I hit him, my old heart almost breaks."

Tom Sawyer lived with his Aunt Polly in St. Petersburg, a sleepy little town on the banks of the great Mississippi River. His half-brother Sid lived in the house too, and so did his cousin Mary, who was kind to everybody. Sid was a quiet, careful boy who never got into trouble, always did exactly what he was told, and was always ready to tell Aunt Polly what Tom had done wrong. Tom did not like Sid very much.

That afternoon Tom did not go to school. He went swimming in the river instead, and he had a wonderful time. At supper Aunt Polly decided to be clever, and she asked him a lot of innocent questions about the weather.

"It was very warm at school today, wasn't it, Tom? Didn't you want to go swimming?"

Tom felt a little uncomfortable, and he searched her face for a clue, but it told him nothing. "No, Aunt. Well, not very much."

The old lady reached out and touched Tom's shirt, and she was secretly pleased to discover that it was dry. But Tom guessed what she was thinking, and he explained that some of the boys had pumped water over their heads to cool down. "Mine's still wet. See?"

Aunt Polly was annoyed, because she had forgotten about that possibility. Then she had another idea. "Tom, I sewed your shirt collar this morning so that you couldn't open it. You didn't have to undo my sewing to pump water on your head, did you? Unbutton your jacket."

Tom opened his jacket, and the collar was still sewn tightly in place. Aunt Polly was half sorry that her clever plan had failed and half glad that Tom had been obedient for once.

"Well, go along with you, then," she said. "I was sure you'd been swimming, but I was wrong, and I forgive you."

At that moment Sid looked up from his plate. "That's strange. I thought you sewed his collar with white thread, Aunt, but now it's black."

"Why, I did sew it with white! Tom!"

But Tom was already at the door, and as he ran out he shouted, "Siddy, I'll get you for that!"

Outside, Tom examined the two needles that he kept hidden inside his jacket, one with white thread and one with black. "She'd never have noticed if it wasn't for Sid," he said to himself. "Sometimes she uses white and sometimes she uses black, and I can't keep up. I wish she'd choose one or the other. But I'll punish Sid for this, I really will."

Tom was not the best-behaved boy in the village, but his troubles never lasted long. Within two minutes he had forgotten them completely, because he had recently learned a new way of whistling, and he walked down the street practising it with great enthusiasm.

It was a summer evening and not yet dark. Suddenly Tom stopped whistling, because a stranger was standing in front of him, a boy a little bigger than himself. In a small village like St. Petersburg, a new boy was an extremely interesting event.

This boy was well dressed, much too well dressed for an ordinary weekday. He had a fine hat and a clean blue jacket, and he even wore shoes and a tie, although it was only Friday. He looked like a boy from the city, and Tom hated him immediately.

Neither of them spoke. Whenever one boy moved, the other moved too, and they circled each other slowly, face to face and eye to eye, for a long time.

"I can beat you!" said Tom at last.

"I'd like to see you try," replied the stranger. The conversation continued like this for several minutes, with each boy promising to destroy the other and neither of them doing anything about it.

Finally Tom drew a line in the dust with his big toe. "I dare you to step over that line. If you do, I'll hit you so hard that you won't be able to stand up."

The new boy stepped over it at once, and within a moment both boys were rolling in the dirt, pulling hair, tearing clothes and scratching each other's noses. After a few minutes of confused fighting, Tom was sitting on top of the stranger and hitting him with his fists.

"Say that you give up!" he shouted, and at last the new boy said, "I give up!" He walked away crying and brushing the dust off his beautiful clothes, while Tom walked home feeling extremely proud of himself.

But when he got home late that night and climbed carefully in through the window, he found his aunt waiting for him in the dark. When she saw the terrible state of his clothes, she made a firm decision. Tomorrow was Saturday, a holiday from school, but Tom was going to spend it working.

Chapter 2: The Fence

Saturday morning came, and the whole summer world was bright and fresh. There was a song in every heart, the trees were in flower, and the air was full of their sweet smell. Cardiff Hill, beyond the village, was green with trees, and it looked like a pleasant land of dreams.

Tom appeared on the pavement with a bucket of whitewash and a long brush. He looked at the fence, and all the joy of the morning left him. It was thirty yards of wooden boards, nine feet high, and he had to paint every inch of it white. Life suddenly seemed empty and hard.

He sighed, dipped his brush in the bucket and painted the top board. Then he did it again, and again. He compared the tiny white line with the enormous grey fence that remained, and he sat down on a box, completely discouraged.

Soon the other boys would come past on their way to all kinds of wonderful adventures, and they would laugh at him for having to work. The thought burned him like fire. He searched his pockets and found a few treasures: some marbles, a piece of string and other rubbish. It was enough to buy perhaps half an hour of freedom, but not a whole day.

Then, at this dark and hopeless moment, a great idea came to him. It was nothing less than a magnificent idea.

He picked up his brush and went calmly back to work. A few minutes later Ben Rogers came down the street, eating a big juicy apple and pretending to be a steamboat on the river. He made loud engine noises, and he gave orders to himself in a deep captain's voice. Tom went on painting and paid no attention to the steamboat.

Ben stopped and stared for a moment. "Hello, old friend. You've got to work, haven't you? I'm going swimming. Don't you wish you could come? But of course you'd prefer to work, wouldn't you?"

Tom looked at him in surprise. "What do you call work?"

"Why, isn't that work?"

Tom went back to his painting and answered carelessly, "Well, maybe it is and maybe it isn't. All I know is that it suits Tom Sawyer."

"Oh, come on, you don't mean that you like it?"

The brush continued to move. "Like it? Well, I don't see why I shouldn't like it. Does a boy get the chance to whitewash a fence every day?"

That put the thing in a new light. Ben stopped eating his apple. Tom moved his brush gracefully back and forth, stepped back to admire the effect, added a touch here and there, and admired it again. Ben watched every movement, and he became more and more interested.

"Tom, let me paint a little."

Tom thought about it for a moment, and he was almost ready to agree, but then he changed his mind. "No, I'm sorry, Ben, but it wouldn't be possible. Aunt Polly is very particular about this fence, because it's right here on the street. If it was the back fence, I wouldn't mind, and she wouldn't either. But this fence has got to be done very carefully. I don't suppose there's one boy in a thousand, maybe two thousand, who can do it the right way."

"Oh, please, Tom, let me try. Just a little. I'd let you, if you were me."

"Ben, I'd like to, honestly, but Aunt Polly... Well, Jim wanted to do it, and she wouldn't let him. Sid wanted to do it, and she wouldn't let Sid either. If you tried and something went wrong..."

"Oh, I'll be just as careful as you. Let me try. I'll give you the rest of my apple."

Tom gave up the brush with an unwilling face but a happy heart. While Ben worked and sweated in the sun, the retired artist sat on a barrel in the shade nearby, swinging his legs, eating the apple and planning how to catch more victims.

There was no shortage of them. Boys came along every few minutes. They came to laugh, but they stayed to paint. By the time Ben was tired, Tom had sold the next chance to Billy Fisher for a kite in good condition. When Billy was finished, Johnny Miller paid him a dead rat with a string to swing it with. And so it went on, hour after hour.

By the middle of the afternoon, Tom was no longer a poor boy. He was rolling in wealth. Besides the things already mentioned, he owned twelve marbles and a piece of blue glass to look through. He also had a key that could not open anything and a tin soldier. There was a kitten with only one eye, a brass doorknob, a dog collar without a dog, and four pieces of orange peel.

He had had a pleasant, lazy day, with plenty of company, and the fence had received three coats of whitewash. If he had not run out of whitewash, he would have taken the money of every boy in the village.

Tom said to himself that the world was not such a bad place after all. Without knowing it, he had discovered a great law of human behaviour. If you want a man or a boy to desire something, you only need to make that thing difficult to get. Work is whatever a person is obliged to do, and play is whatever a person is not obliged to do.

He went in to Aunt Polly, who was sitting by an open window with the cat asleep on her lap. "May I go and play now, Aunt?"

"What, already? How much have you done?"

"It's all done, Aunt."

"Tom, don't lie to me. I can't bear it."

"I'm not lying, Aunt. It really is all done."

Aunt Polly did not trust such evidence, and she went out to see for herself. When she found the entire fence whitewashed, with three coats on every board and even a line of white on the ground, her astonishment was almost too great for words.

"Well, I never! There's no getting round it, you can work when you want to, Tom." Then she spoiled the compliment by adding, "But it's not often that you want to, I must say. Well, go along and play."

She was so pleased with the result that she took him into the kitchen and gave him the best apple she could find. While her back was turned, he stole a doughnut as well. Then, on his way out, he saw Sid starting up the outside stairs, and he threw several balls of hard earth at him before Aunt Polly could arrive to rescue her favourite. Tom escaped over the fence, feeling that his account with Sid was now settled.

Chapter 3: Becky Thatcher

On his way through the village, Tom passed the house of Jeff Thatcher, and he noticed a new girl in the garden. She was a lovely little blue-eyed creature with yellow hair in two long plaits, a white summer dress and embroidered trousers under it. Tom's heart fell at her feet without a single shot being fired.

Only a week earlier, he had been madly in love with a girl called Amy Lawrence. He had spent months winning her, and she had admitted her feelings only seven short days ago. Now, in one instant, Amy disappeared from his heart like a stranger whose visit had ended.

Tom worshipped this new angel with secret eyes until he saw that she had noticed him. Then he pretended that he did not know she was there, and he began to show off in all sorts of ridiculous ways. He walked on his hands, balanced a stick on his nose and did dangerous gymnastics on the fence. Unfortunately, the girl turned and walked towards the house, but just before she went in, she threw a flower over the fence. Tom ran to it, picked it up with his toes, hopped away with it and hid it inside his jacket, next to his heart.

On Sunday morning Tom had to go to Sunday school. In this school, children won small blue tickets for learning verses from the Bible by heart. Ten blue tickets were worth one red one, ten red tickets were worth one yellow one, and with ten yellow tickets a pupil could win a plain, simple Bible as a prize. It meant learning two thousand verses, and only a few patient pupils had ever managed it.

Tom had no intention of learning anything, but he had a plan. Before the lesson began, he stood at the door and traded with the other boys. He gave them the treasures he had earned from the fence, and they gave him their tickets in exchange.

That morning there was a very important visitor at the school. He was Judge Thatcher, a famous man from the county town twelve miles away, and he had brought his family with him. When Tom saw the little girl beside him, his heart jumped. It was the angel from the garden, and her name, he learned, was Becky.

At the end of the lesson, the headmaster wanted to give a Bible to somebody in front of the judge, because it would make the school look good. But none of the hardworking pupils had enough tickets. Then Tom Sawyer stepped forward with nine yellow tickets, nine red tickets and ten blue ones, and he asked for his Bible.

It was the most astonishing surprise of the decade. The headmaster could not understand it, but the tickets were real, so Tom was taken up to the platform and introduced to the judge. The other boys were sick with envy, especially the ones who had sold him their tickets.

The judge was very kind. "Two thousand verses is a great many," he said. "Now, no doubt you know the names of all the twelve disciples. Won't you tell us the names of the first two?"

Tom pulled at a button and looked embarrassed. His face went red, and his eyes fell.

"Answer the gentleman, Thomas," said the headmaster. "Don't be afraid."

Tom was silent for a long moment. Then, under great pressure, he said, "David and Goliath!"

Let us draw a curtain of charity over the rest of the scene.

On Monday morning Tom went to school late, and the teacher asked him why. He was about to invent a lie when he noticed that the only empty seat on the girls' side was next to Becky Thatcher. Instantly he said, "I stopped to talk with Huckleberry Finn!"

The teacher could hardly believe his ears. Huckleberry Finn was the son of the town drunkard, and every mother in town hated and feared him. He never went to school or to church, he slept wherever he liked, and he wore old clothes that were always falling to pieces. All the other boys envied him and were forbidden to play with him.

The teacher hit Tom with a stick until his arm was tired, and then he ordered Tom to go and sit with the girls. That had been Tom's plan from the beginning.

During the lesson Tom drew a picture of a house on his slate and showed it to Becky. She was interested, and soon they were whispering together. Tom wrote "I love you" on the slate, hid it with his hand and then let her see it.

At lunchtime, when the room was empty, he told her about being engaged. "You only have to tell a boy that you'll never have anybody but him, ever, and then you kiss, and that's all. Anybody can do it."

After some resistance, Becky whispered the promise in his ear and allowed him to kiss her. Tom was delighted, and he chattered happily about the future. "It's so nice. Why, when I was engaged to Amy Lawrence..."

Becky's big eyes filled with tears, and Tom realised his terrible mistake too late. She turned her face to the wall and cried. He tried to comfort her, and he offered her his greatest treasure, a brass knob from the top of a fireplace. She knocked it to the floor.

Tom marched out of the school and did not come back that afternoon. A little later, Becky began to search for him, calling his name, but there was no answer. She sat down and cried again, and she told herself that she hated him.

Chapter 4: Midnight in the Graveyard

That afternoon Tom met Huckleberry Finn in the street. Huck was carrying a dead cat.

"Hello, Huckleberry! What's that?"

"A dead cat," said Huck, very pleased with himself.

"What's a dead cat good for, Huck?"

"Good for? You can cure warts with it, of course."

Huck explained the method in detail. "You take your cat and go to the graveyard about midnight, when somebody wicked has just been buried. When it's midnight, a devil will come, or maybe two or three. You can't see them, you can only hear something like the wind. When they're taking that bad man away, you throw your cat after them and say the magic words. Devil follow body, cat follow devil, warts follow cat, I've finished with you! That'll cure any wart."

"It sounds right," said Tom seriously. "When are you going to try it?"

"Tonight. Old Hoss Williams was buried on Saturday, and I reckon the devils will come for him tonight."

"Let me go with you?"

"Of course, if you're not afraid."

"Afraid! Not likely. Will you meow under my window?"

That night Tom lay awake in bed, waiting. At last, close to eleven o'clock, he heard a sad meow from the garden. He climbed out of the window, crawled across the roof of the shed and jumped to the ground, where Huck was waiting with his dead cat.

Half an hour later they were walking through the tall grass of the old graveyard on the hill, about a mile and a half from the village. The wind moaned through the trees, and Tom was afraid that it might be the spirits of the dead complaining about being disturbed. They found the new grave and hid behind three big trees a few feet away.

For a long time they waited in silence. Then Tom grabbed his friend's arm. "What's that?"

Some strange figures were approaching through the darkness, and one of them was carrying an old lantern that freckled the ground with little spots of light.

"It's the devils, sure enough," whispered Huck, shaking. "Three of them! Can you pray?"

"I'll try, but don't be afraid. They won't hurt us." Then Tom stopped. "Huck, those aren't devils. They're humans! One of them is old Muff Potter."

"So it is. Drunk, as usual, I reckon."

"And Huck, I know another of those voices. It's Injun Joe."

Everybody in town called him by that name, and everybody was afraid of him. He was a bitter, violent man who lived alone and spoke to nobody, and the boys wished they were anywhere else.

The third man was young Doctor Robinson. Muff Potter and Injun Joe began to dig up the grave, while the doctor sat watching them with the lantern at his feet. He wanted the body for his medical studies, and he had paid the two men to steal it. After some time their spades struck the coffin. They opened it, lifted out the body and laid it on a cart.

"Now the thing is ready, doctor," said Potter, "and you'll pay us five dollars more, or here it stays."

"That's the way to talk!" said Injun Joe.

"What do you mean?" said the doctor. "You asked for your pay in advance, and I've paid you."

"Yes, and you've done more than that," said Injun Joe, coming closer to the doctor. "Five years ago you drove me away from your father's kitchen one night, when I came to ask for something to eat. And when I swore I'd get even with you, even if it took a hundred years, your father had me put in prison. Did you think I'd forget? Now I've got you."

He shook his fist in the doctor's face. The doctor struck out suddenly and knocked the man to the ground. Potter dropped his knife and shouted, "Don't you hit my partner!" and the next moment he and the doctor were fighting desperately in the grass. Injun Joe jumped up, picked up Potter's knife and moved around the struggling men like a cat, waiting for his chance.

Suddenly the doctor broke free, seized the heavy board from Williams's grave and hit Potter on the head with it. Potter fell to the ground, unconscious. In the same instant, Injun Joe drove the knife into the young man's chest. The doctor fell on top of Potter and lay still.

At that moment the clouds covered the moon, and the two frightened boys ran away into the dark as fast as their legs would carry them.

Injun Joe stood over the two bodies. He took the doctor's money, and then he placed the bloody knife in Potter's open right hand and sat down to wait. A few minutes later Potter began to move and groan. He sat up, looked at the knife in his hand and then at the doctor, and he dropped it with a shiver.

"Lord, how is this, Joe?"

"It's a dirty business," said Injun Joe calmly. "Why did you do it?"

"I! I never did it! I was drunk, Joe, and my head's all confused. Tell me, Joe, honestly, did I do it? I never meant to. Oh, it's awful, and him so young and promising."

"Why, you two were fighting, and he hit you with the board, and you fell. Then you got up, all confused, and you took the knife and drove it into him just as he hit you again. And you've been lying here as dead as a stone ever since."

Potter began to cry and begged Injun Joe never to tell anyone. Injun Joe promised to keep the secret, and the two men hurried off in different directions. The moon came out again and shone on the dead doctor, the body on the cart and the open grave. Then there was silence once more.

Chapter 5: The Secret

The two boys ran and ran towards the village, too frightened to speak. They did not stop until they reached an old empty building at the edge of town, where they fell through the door and lay in the shadows, breathing hard.

"Huckleberry, what do you think will happen?" whispered Tom when he could speak again.

"If Doctor Robinson dies, I think they'll hang somebody."

"Who'll tell? Us?"

"Don't be silly. Suppose something happened and Injun Joe didn't hang. He'd kill us some time or other, as sure as we're lying here. Muff Potter was too drunk to know what happened, and he's never going to tell what he doesn't know. We've got to keep quiet about this, Tom."

"I agree. It's the best thing. Let's swear to keep it secret."

Tom found a clean piece of wood and wrote an oath on it with a piece of red stone. It said that Huck Finn and Tom Sawyer swore they would never speak about this, and that they hoped they might drop down dead if they ever did. Then each boy pricked his thumb with a pin and signed his initials in blood. They buried the piece of wood beside the wall with some dark ceremonies, and they felt that their lips were now locked for ever.

Tom crept back into his bedroom shortly before dawn, but in the morning he discovered that Sid had been awake and knew that he had gone out. Aunt Polly did not punish him. Instead she cried and asked him how he could break her old heart like this, and that was far worse than any punishment.

By midday the whole village was talking about the terrible news. The doctor's body had been found in the graveyard, and a bloody knife had been found beside it. Somebody recognised it as Muff Potter's knife, and somebody else said that Potter had been seen washing himself in the stream at about two o'clock in the morning.

Half the town went up to the graveyard, and Tom went too, although he would have preferred to go anywhere else. While he was there, the sheriff's men brought Muff Potter back, and Injun Joe stood calmly in the crowd. When Potter saw the dead body, he broke down completely.

"I didn't do it, friends," he cried. "Upon my word and honour, I never did it." Then he turned to Injun Joe. "Oh, Injun Joe, you promised me you'd never..."

Injun Joe told the whole story to the sheriff in a quiet, steady voice, and every word was a lie. The boys waited for the sky to open and the lightning to strike him dead, but nothing happened. Potter was taken away to the little prison at the edge of the village.

For a week afterwards Tom slept very badly. He talked in his sleep, and Sid noticed it. "You talk such nonsense, Tom," Sid said one morning. "Last night you said, It's blood, it's blood, that's what it is! And you said, Don't torture me like this, I'll tell! What will you tell?"

Tom went pale, but fortunately Aunt Polly came to his rescue without knowing it. "It's that dreadful murder. I dream about it myself, almost every night." After that, Tom complained of toothache for a week and tied his jaws up every night so that he could not talk in his sleep.

Every day or two during this time, Tom went to the small window of the prison and secretly gave Muff Potter whatever little presents he could find. Potter was grateful, and his gratitude made Tom's conscience hurt even more.

Meanwhile Tom's other troubles had grown worse. Becky Thatcher had stopped coming to school because she was ill, and Tom became so unhappy that he lost interest in everything. Aunt Polly was worried, and she tried every medicine she read about in the newspapers. Finally she discovered a new medicine called Painkiller, which tasted like liquid fire, and she gave Tom a spoonful every day.

Tom soon grew tired of it. One day, when nobody was watching, he gave a spoonful to the cat. The cat flew two yards into the air, gave a terrible scream and raced round and round the room, knocking over flowerpots and crashing into furniture. Then it jumped out of the open window, taking the rest of the flowerpots with it.

Aunt Polly stood frozen in amazement. "Tom, what on earth is the matter with that cat?"

"I don't know, Aunt," said Tom, laughing so hard that he could hardly breathe. Then Aunt Polly saw the spoon under the bed, and Tom's laughter stopped.

"Why did you treat that poor animal like that?" she asked, after she had hit him on the head with her thimble.

"I did it because I was sorry for him. He hasn't got any aunt."

"Hasn't got any aunt? What does that have to do with anything?"

"Well, if he had an aunt, she would have burned him up herself! She would have roasted his insides without any more feeling than if he was a human."

Aunt Polly suddenly felt a pain of guilt. What was cruel to a cat might be cruel to a boy too. Her eyes filled with tears, and she put her hand gently on Tom's head. After that, there was no more Painkiller.

When Becky returned to school, Tom showed off in front of her, shouting and jumping over fences, but she only turned up her nose and walked past him. His heart was broken again.

A few days later, Becky made a mistake of her own. The teacher, Mr Dobbins, kept a mysterious book in his desk, and every child in school wanted to know what was inside it. One afternoon Becky found the desk unlocked and the key in it. She opened the book and saw a beautiful picture of a human body. At that moment Tom stepped into the room, and Becky jumped so violently that she tore the page in half.

"Tom Sawyer, you're horrible to creep up on me like that!" she cried. "Now you'll tell, and I'll be whipped, and I've never been whipped in school before!"

Tom thought it over and decided that she was a silly girl, but when the teacher discovered the torn page later that afternoon, he saw her face go white with fear. Mr Dobbins asked each pupil in turn whether they had done it. When he came to Becky, Tom jumped to his feet.

"I did it!"

The whole school stared at him in astonishment. Tom received the most terrible whipping of his life, and he was ordered to stay at school for two hours after the others had gone. But he did not mind at all, because he had seen the expression of gratitude and adoration in Becky's eyes. Before he fell asleep that night, he heard her words in his memory: "Tom, how could you be so noble?"

Chapter 6: The Pirates

The summer holidays began, but they were not as happy as Tom had expected. Becky had gone away to the country with her parents, and the murder was still a dark weight on his heart. At last Tom made up his mind. Nobody loved him, he decided, and so he would escape from the world and become a pirate.

He found two other boys who also felt badly treated. One was his best friend Joe Harper, whose mother had punished him for something he had not done. The other was Huckleberry Finn, who was always ready for any adventure. They agreed to meet at midnight at a lonely spot on the riverbank.

Three miles below St. Petersburg, the Mississippi was more than a mile wide, and there was a long, narrow island covered with trees in the middle of it. It was called Jackson's Island, and nobody lived there. The boys decided that it was the perfect hiding place for pirates.

At midnight each boy arrived with stolen supplies. Tom brought a large piece of cooked ham, Joe brought bread, and Huck brought a frying pan and some tobacco. They found a small wooden raft tied up near the bank, and they borrowed it, which they did not consider stealing. Tom named himself the Black Avenger of the Spanish Main, Huck became Red-Handed Huck, and Joe became the Terror of the Seas.

It was two o'clock in the morning when they landed on the island. They built a fire against a fallen tree, cooked some bacon in the frying pan for supper and ate it with half of the bread. It seemed glorious to be free in the wild woods of an unknown island, far from the haunts of men. Then they lay down by the fire and talked about the life of pirates.

"It's the life for me," said Tom. "You don't have to get up in the morning, and you don't have to go to school, or wash, or do any of that silly stuff."

Before falling asleep, Tom and Joe both felt a little guilty about the food they had stolen. They decided that from now on they would be pirates who did not steal, and then they went peacefully to sleep.

When Tom woke up in the morning, he could not remember where he was for a moment. Then he saw the cool grey light of dawn, the quiet woods and the great river, and his heart filled with joy. The boys swam in the shallow water, caught fish for breakfast and fried them with bacon, and they thought it was the most delicious meal they had ever eaten.

After breakfast they explored the island. They found that it was about three miles long and a quarter of a mile wide, and that it was separated from the nearest shore by a narrow channel. They swam every hour or so, and it was the middle of the afternoon before they returned to their camp.

Then they heard a strange, deep sound in the distance: Boom!

"What's that?" said Joe quietly.

They ran to the edge of the island and looked out through the bushes. The little steamboat from the village was moving slowly down the river, full of people, and a cloud of white smoke burst from its side, followed by the same deep sound.

"I know now!" said Tom. "Somebody's drowned!"

"That's it!" said Huck. "They did that last summer when Bill Turner was drowned. They fire a cannon over the water, and that makes the body come up to the top."

The boys watched and listened. Suddenly an idea flashed through Tom's mind. "Boys, I know who's drowned. It's us!"

They felt like heroes in an instant. People were missing them, and hearts were breaking because of them, and tears were being shed. It was wonderful to think about.

But when night came and the fire burned low, Joe and Tom grew quiet. They were thinking about the people at home who were not enjoying this as much as they were. Joe suggested carefully that perhaps they might go back to civilisation soon, but Tom laughed at him until he gave up the idea.

Later, when the other two were asleep, Tom got up quietly. He wrote something on a piece of white bark, put it in Joe's hat with some of his own treasures. Then he disappeared into the trees towards the sandbar at the top of the island.

Chapter 7: Tom Goes Home

Tom waded across the shallow water as far as he could and then swam the rest of the way to the Illinois shore, about a hundred yards. He walked through the woods along the bank until he found the ferryboat, which was about to cross to the village. He climbed into the small boat that it was pulling behind it, lay down in the bottom and waited.

Soon he reached the village and ran through the quiet streets to his aunt's house. A light was burning in the sitting room, and he crept up to the window. Inside sat Aunt Polly, Sid, Mary and Joe Harper's mother, all talking together.

Tom went to the door and lifted the latch very gently. He pushed it open a little, slipped through the gap and lay down on the floor under the bed, so close to Aunt Polly that he could almost touch her foot.

"He wasn't bad, as I was saying," Aunt Polly was telling Mrs Harper. "He was only careless and wild, that's all. He never meant any harm, and he was the best-hearted boy that ever was." She began to cry.

"It was just the same with my Joe," said Mrs Harper. "Always full of tricks, but as kind and unselfish as he could be. And only last week I whipped him for taking that cream, and then I remembered that I'd thrown it out myself because it was sour. I'll never see him again in this world, never, never!"

Sid said that he hoped Tom was better off where he was, but that if he had been better in some ways... Aunt Polly stopped him at once. "Not a word against my Tom, Sid! God will take care of him."

Tom was crying too by this time, more out of pity for himself than for anybody else. From their conversation he learned many interesting things. At first the town had believed that the boys had drowned while swimming. Then people had discovered that the little raft was missing, and they had hoped the boys would turn up further down the river. But now it was Wednesday night, and if the bodies were not found by Sunday, there would be a funeral that morning.

Mrs Harper went home, and Aunt Polly knelt down beside her bed and prayed for Tom so lovingly that he was crying again before she had finished. When she was finally asleep, Tom crept out from under the bed. He took the piece of bark from his pocket and thought about leaving it by her candle, but then a happy idea came into his mind. He put the bark back in his pocket, bent over and kissed her faded lips, and then he slipped out of the house.

He found a boat at the ferry, rowed it across the river and walked back through the woods. It was broad daylight when he reached the camp, where Joe and Huck were eating breakfast.

"Tom's honest, Huck, and he'll come back," Joe was saying. "He won't desert us. He knows that would be a disgrace to a pirate."

"Which he is!" cried Tom, stepping dramatically into the camp. Over a splendid breakfast of bacon and fish, he told them about his adventures, adding plenty of decorations of his own.

The next few days passed slowly. The boys swam, played and fished, but Joe and Huck grew more and more homesick, and at last Joe said that he was going home. Huck agreed to go with him. Then Tom told them his secret plan, and their faces lit up immediately. They shouted with delight and said that it was a wonderful idea, and they decided to stay.

On Sunday morning the bell of the little church began to ring, slowly and sadly. The villagers gathered quietly, talking in whispers about the dead boys. Aunt Polly arrived with Sid and Mary, followed by the Harper family, all dressed in black.

The minister spoke so beautifully about the lost boys that everybody in the church cried. He described how good, kind and generous they had been, and many people remembered that they had always thought the boys were rascals who deserved to be punished.

Then there was a sound at the back of the church. The door opened slowly, and the three dead boys came marching down the middle of the church: Tom first, Joe next, and Huck, in his old torn clothes, at the back. They had been hiding in the empty gallery, listening to their own funeral.

Aunt Polly, Mary and the Harpers threw themselves on their returned boys and smothered them with kisses. Poor Huck stood alone, embarrassed and uncomfortable, wondering where to hide. He was turning to go when Tom held him by the arm.

"Aunt Polly, it isn't fair. Somebody's got to be glad to see Huck."

"And so they shall. I'm glad to see him, poor motherless thing!" said Aunt Polly, and she gave him so much loving attention that he was more uncomfortable than before.

Then the minister shouted, "Praise God from whom all blessings flow! Sing it, everybody!" The old church shook with the singing, and Tom Sawyer the Pirate looked around at the envious boys and admitted to himself that this was the proudest moment of his life.

Chapter 8: The Trial

Tom's return made him a hero at school, and the other children followed him around wherever he went. Becky was back from the country, and at first they continued to ignore each other, but after the incident of the torn book they were friends once more.

The summer went on, and finally the murder trial began in the courtroom. It was the only subject of conversation in the village, and Tom could not escape it. Every time somebody mentioned the murder, he felt a cold shiver go down his back. He took Huck to a lonely place to talk.

"Huck, have you ever told anybody about that?"

"About what?"

"You know what."

"Oh, of course I haven't. Why, we'd be dead in two days if that came out. Injun Joe would drown us like a couple of cats."

The boys renewed their oath of silence, but they felt sorry for poor Muff Potter. "He's never done anything to anybody," said Huck. "He's fished a lot and been drunk a lot, but he once gave me half a fish when there wasn't enough for two. And lots of times he's stood by me when I was in trouble."

"Well, he mended my kites, Huck, and tied hooks on my fishing line," said Tom. "I wish we could get him out of there."

They went to the prison window that evening and gave Potter some tobacco and matches. He thanked them with such warmth that Tom felt like the worst coward in the world.

For two days the trial went badly for Potter. Witness after witness described how he had been washing in the stream that night, and how he had tried to slip away when the crowd came. The lawyer who was defending him asked them no questions at all, and the people of the village began to whisper that he had given up. Injun Joe sat in the courtroom every day with a face like stone, watching everything. Tom stayed out late each night and came home in a terrible state of excitement.

On the third morning the courtroom was full. After the last witness had spoken, everybody believed that the trial was over. Then Potter's lawyer stood up.

"Your Honour, I wish to call one more witness. Call Thomas Sawyer!"

A wave of surprise passed over every face in the room. Tom walked to the front of the room and took the oath, looking pale and frightened. For a few moments he could not speak, but then he found his voice.

"Thomas Sawyer, where were you on the seventeenth of June, at about midnight?"

Tom looked at Injun Joe's iron face, and his tongue failed him. Then he recovered a little and said, "In the graveyard."

"Were you anywhere near Hoss Williams's grave?"

"Yes, sir. I was hiding behind the big trees on the edge of the grave."

"Were you alone?"

"No, sir. I went there with..."

"Wait. Never mind your companion's name. We will produce him at the proper time. Did you carry anything there with you?"

Tom hesitated and looked confused. "Only a... a dead cat."

There was a ripple of laughter, but the judge stopped it quickly. Then the lawyer said, "Now, my boy, tell us everything that happened. Tell it in your own way, and don't be afraid."

Tom began slowly, but as he went on, the words flowed more and more easily. Soon the only sound in the room was his voice. Every eye was fixed on him, and the crowd hung on his words, hardly breathing. He described the digging, the argument and the fight, and finally he came to the end.

"And as the doctor brought the board down and Muff Potter fell, Injun Joe jumped with the knife and..."

Crash! Quick as lightning, Injun Joe sprang for the window, pushed through the people who tried to stop him, and was gone.

Muff Potter was set free, and Tom was a glittering hero once again. The old people loved him, the young ones envied him, and his name even appeared in the village newspaper. Some people believed he would become President one day, if he managed to escape hanging first.

Tom's days were days of glory and joy, but his nights were seasons of horror. Injun Joe filled all his dreams, always with death in his eyes. A reward was offered for his capture, and the countryside was searched, but nobody found him. Half of Tom's mind wanted the man to be caught, and the other half was afraid that he would come back. He knew he would never sleep peacefully until that man was dead and he had seen the body with his own eyes.

Chapter 9: The Haunted House

There comes a time in every normal boy's life when he has a strong desire to go somewhere and dig for hidden treasure. This desire suddenly came upon Tom one day. He looked for Joe Harper, but Joe was busy, so he went to find Huck Finn, who was always available and had plenty of time to spare.

"Where shall we dig?" asked Huck.

"Oh, almost anywhere. Treasure is usually hidden under a dead tree, or in a haunted house, or on an island."

"Who hides it?"

"Why, robbers, of course. Who else would? They always bury it and leave it there."

They tried digging under an old dead tree on Cardiff Hill, but they found nothing except sore hands. Then Tom suggested the old haunted house in the valley beyond the hill. Nobody had lived there for years, and people said that ghosts walked through its rooms at night.

"Well, I don't like haunted houses, Tom," said Huck. "Ghosts are worse than dead people. They come sliding around in a white sheet when you're not expecting them, and they look over your shoulder and grind their teeth."

They agreed to go during the day, when ghosts were not supposed to be active. But the next day was Friday, and Huck remembered just in time that Friday was a very unlucky day for any kind of business. So they played Robin Hood all afternoon instead, and they went to the haunted house on Saturday.

The house was a ruin. The floors were covered in weeds, the windows were broken, and the stairs had partly fallen down. The boys entered on tiptoe, with their hearts beating fast, and they talked in whispers. After a while they grew braver, threw their tools into a corner and climbed the stairs to look at the rooms above.

Suddenly Tom stopped. "Sh!" he whispered. "Listen! Somebody's coming!"

They lay down on the dusty floor with their eyes to holes in the boards, and waited in terror. Two men came in. One was an old Spaniard who was deaf and unable to speak, and who had been seen in the village once or twice recently. He had long white hair, a green cloth over his eyes, and a large hat. The other man was a dirty, untidy stranger.

The men sat down on the floor, and the stranger began to talk. Then the Spaniard spoke too, in a low voice, and the boys almost cried out in fear. It was the voice of Injun Joe! The deaf Spaniard was only a disguise.

The two men were robbers, and they had come to hide some stolen money in the house. While they were digging a hole in the corner, Injun Joe's knife struck something hard. It was a box, and when they pulled it out and opened it, the boys saw that it was full of gold coins. There were thousands of dollars there, much more than the two men had stolen themselves.

"Old Murrel's gang used to be around here one summer," said the stranger. "This must be their money."

"Now you won't need to do that other job," he went on.

"You don't know me," said Injun Joe darkly. "It isn't robbery that I'm after. It's revenge!" A wicked light flamed in his eyes. "I'll need your help with it. When it's finished, we'll go to Texas."

The boys were delighted to see the gold, but then Injun Joe noticed some fresh earth on the boys' pick and shovel in the corner. He was suspicious at once. Somebody else had been in the house, and that somebody might still be there. He decided to take the box away and hide it somewhere safer, in "Number Two, under the cross."

Then he began to walk slowly up the broken stairs, with his knife in his hand. The boys thought about the cupboard, but they could not move. Then there was a crash of rotten wood, and Injun Joe fell to the ground in the ruins of the stairs. He stood up, swearing, and the stranger laughed at him.

"If anybody's up there, let them stay there," said the stranger. "It'll soon be dark. If they want to jump down and follow us, they're welcome."

The men left with the box, and the boys lay still for a long time before they dared to come down. They went home through the evening, wondering what Number Two meant.

Tom thought about it all night. In the morning he decided that it must be the number of a room in one of the two small hotels in the village. He discovered that in the second hotel, Room Number Two was always kept locked, and that people went in and out of it only at night. That evening the boys agreed on a plan. Huck would watch the hotel every night, and if anything happened, he would come to Tom's window and meow.

A few nights later, when it was very dark, Tom crept into the hotel with a bunch of keys he had borrowed from his aunt, while Huck kept watch outside. After a few minutes Tom came running out of the alley as fast as he could.

"Run!" he shouted. "Run for your life!"

They did not stop until they reached an old shed at the other end of the village. Tom explained that the keys had not worked, so he had tried the door handle, and the door had opened. Inside, he had almost stepped on Injun Joe's hand. The man was lying asleep on the floor, drunk, with an empty bottle beside him.

"Did you see the box?"

"I didn't wait to look around. I just ran."

They agreed that Huck would continue to watch the hotel every night, and that they would search the room on the night when Injun Joe went out.

Chapter 10: Two Adventures

On Friday morning Tom heard some good news: Judge Thatcher's family had come back to town the evening before. For a while, Injun Joe and the treasure were forgotten, and Becky became the centre of Tom's world again. Becky asked her mother to hold the long-promised school picnic the next day, and her mother agreed.

On Saturday morning a happy crowd of children, with a few young adults to look after them, climbed aboard an old ferryboat that would take them three miles down the river. Becky's mother told her that she could stay the night with a friend, Susy Harper, because they would come back late.

Tom had a different plan. "Instead of going to Joe Harper's house, we'll climb the hill and stop at the Widow Douglas's," he told Becky. "She'll have ice cream, and she'll be awfully glad to have us." The widow was a kind, generous woman who lived in a fine house on the top of Cardiff Hill. Becky liked the idea very much, and they agreed to keep it a secret from her mother.

After the feast in the woods, somebody shouted, "Who's ready for the cave?" Everybody was ready. They all took candles and climbed up to the entrance of McDougal's cave, a huge hole in the side of the hill. Inside was an enormous maze of passages that seemed to go on for ever. People said that you might wander for days and nights in its secret corridors and never find the end. No man knew the whole cave, and most of the young men knew only a small part of it.

The children ran up and down the main passage, laughing and playing hide-and-seek. Hours passed in this way. When the ferryboat finally took them back to town, it was already dark, and the tired, happy group did not count heads.

That same night Huck was on guard outside the hotel. At about eleven o'clock the door opened, and two men slipped out, one of them carrying something under his arm. "It must be the box," thought Huck, and he followed them through the dark streets, keeping well behind them. They went up Cardiff Hill and stopped in the bushes near the Widow Douglas's house. Huck crept closer and listened.

"The light's still on," said the stranger. "Maybe she's got company. Let's give it up."

"Give it up, and I'm just leaving this country for ever?" said Injun Joe in a terrible voice. "Her husband was the judge who had me whipped like a dog in front of the prison, in front of the whole town! He died before I could pay him back, but I'll take it out of her. I won't kill her, but I'll cut her face so badly that nobody will look at her again."

Huck was horrified. He knew that the widow had been kind to him many times. He crept away as quietly as he could, and then he ran down the hill to the house of an old Welshman who lived with his two strong sons. Huck banged on the door until they opened it.

"Please don't ever tell anybody that I told you," he begged. "Injun Joe would kill me for sure." Then he explained what he had heard.

Three minutes later the old man and his sons were climbing the hill with their guns. Huck hid behind a big rock and waited. Then he heard shots and a cry, and he ran down the hill as fast as his legs could carry him. The robbers had escaped in the dark, but the widow was safe.

The next morning was Sunday, and there was a new shock for the village. Mrs Thatcher came to Aunt Polly at church. "Is my Becky going to stay with you all day? I suppose she's tired after the picnic."

"Your Becky?"

"Yes. Didn't she stay with you last night?"

"Why, no."

Then Aunt Polly turned pale. "Has Tom stayed at your house, Mrs Harper? I can't find him anywhere."

Nobody had seen Tom or Becky since the cave. The church bells rang wildly, the whole town woke up, and within half an hour two hundred men were hurrying down the river towards McDougal's cave.

Chapter 11: Lost in the Cave

In the cave, Tom and Becky had wandered away from the others while they were exploring the long passages. They wrote their names on the walls with candle smoke and went deeper and deeper, looking for new wonders. They found a spring of water that came from a ceiling covered with sparkling stone, and a huge room with hundreds of bats hanging from the roof. The bats flew at their candles, and the children ran into another passage to escape them.

When they finally decided to go back, Tom could not find the way. Every passage looked like all the others. "It's all right," he kept saying, but his voice was not confident, and soon Becky began to cry. "Oh, Tom, we're lost! We'll never get out of this awful place!"

They walked on and on until they were too tired to continue. Tom blew out Becky's candle, because they had to save the light. He found a little cake in his pocket that Becky had saved from the picnic, and they shared it. They drank from a spring, slept a little and woke up hungry again. They had no idea whether it was day or night.

After a long time, Tom decided to explore the side passages with a long piece of kite string, so that he could always find his way back to Becky. In one of the passages he turned a corner and saw a human hand, holding a candle, appear from behind a rock. Tom shouted with joy, but the hand was followed by the body it belonged to, and it was Injun Joe! Tom was frozen with terror, but the man turned and ran away. Tom decided that Injun Joe had not recognised his voice in the echoing cave.

Tom said nothing about it to Becky. Hours later, while Becky slept, he tried another passage with the string. When he came back he was very excited.

"Becky, I've found a way out! I went down a passage to the end of the string, and I saw a tiny spot of daylight far away. I crawled towards it, and I pushed my head through a hole, and there was the river rolling by!"

They crawled out into the night air five miles below the cave entrance, and they cried with happiness. Some men came along in a small boat, and at first they did not believe the story, but at last they took the children home. Late on Tuesday night, the bells rang out again, and the whole village ran into the streets, shouting, "They're found! They're found!"

Tom and Becky were weak and exhausted, and they stayed in bed for several days. About two weeks later, Tom went to visit Becky, and Judge Thatcher told him that nobody would ever get lost in the cave again. "I've had its big door covered with iron and locked, and I've got the key."

Tom turned as white as paper. "Oh, Judge, Injun Joe's in the cave!"

Within minutes a group of men were rowing down to the cave. When they unlocked the door, they found Injun Joe lying dead on the ground, with his face close to the crack under the door. He had died of hunger and thirst. Tom was sorry for him, because he knew how much the man had suffered. But he also felt a great sense of relief, and he slept peacefully for the first time in months.

A few days later Tom took Huck to the cave in a small boat, through the secret hole that he had found. "Huck," he said quietly, "the money was never in Room Number Two. It's in the cave!"

With their candles they found a rock with a cross drawn on it in candle smoke. Under the rock was a small hiding place, and in it was the box of gold. The boys carried it home in bags, hidden under some old blankets in a cart.

When they counted the money, they found that it came to over twelve thousand dollars. It was more money than anyone in the village had ever seen. Judge Thatcher and Aunt Polly put it in the bank, and the boys received a dollar each for every day of the year.

The Welshman had told everyone how Huck saved the widow, and now the Widow Douglas decided to take Huck into her home. She dressed him in clean clothes, taught him to eat with a knife and fork, and sent him to school and church. But after three weeks Huck disappeared, and Tom found him sleeping in an old barrel behind a shed, wearing his old rags and looking perfectly happy.

"Don't talk about it, Tom," said Huck. "I've tried, and it doesn't work. The widow's good to me, and friendly, but I can't stand her ways. She makes me get up at the same time every morning, and wash, and comb my hair. And I can't smoke, and I can't swear, and I have to go to church. Being rich isn't what it's said to be. It's all worry and sweat."

Tom thought for a moment. "Huck, I'm going to start a band of robbers, and I can't let you into the gang if you aren't respectable. You'll have to go back to the widow."

Huck's face fell, and he was silent for a long time. Then he said, "Well, I'll go back to the widow for a month, Tom, and I'll try to stand it, if you'll let me belong to the gang."

"All right, Huck, it's a deal!" said Tom. "We'll meet at midnight, at the old haunted house, and swear the oath in blood."

So ends this story. It is the history of a boy, so it must stop here. If it went any further, it would become the history of a man.

The End
