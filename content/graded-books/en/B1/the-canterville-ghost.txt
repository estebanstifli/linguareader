Chapter 1: A House with a Ghost

When Mr Hiram B. Otis, the American Minister in London, bought Canterville Chase, everybody told him that he was making a very foolish mistake. There was no doubt at all, they said, that the old house was haunted.

Even Lord Canterville, who was selling the house, was an honest man and felt that he had to warn him. He mentioned the matter when they met to discuss the price.

"We have not wanted to live in the place ourselves," said Lord Canterville, "since my grand-aunt, the Dowager Duchess of Bolton, had a terrible fright there. She was dressing for dinner one evening when two skeleton hands were placed on her shoulders. She never really recovered from the shock. Several members of my family have seen the ghost too, and so has the rector of the parish. After that unfortunate accident with the Duchess, none of our servants would stay with us. Lady Canterville often got very little sleep at night because of the strange noises that came from the corridor and the library."

"My Lord," answered the Minister, "I will take the furniture and the ghost at the same price. I come from a modern country, where we have everything that money can buy. Our young people go to Europe, and they bring home your best actors and your finest singers. If there were such a thing as a ghost in Europe, we would have it at home very soon. We would put it in one of our public museums."

"I am afraid that the ghost exists," said Lord Canterville with a smile, "although it may not like the offers of your clever businessmen. It has been well known for three centuries, since 1584 in fact, and it always appears before the death of a member of our family."

"Well, so does the family doctor, Lord Canterville. But there is no such thing as a ghost, sir. I am sure that the laws of Nature are not going to change for the British aristocracy."

"You are certainly very natural in America," said Lord Canterville, who did not quite understand the last remark. "If you do not mind a ghost in the house, that is all right. But please remember that I warned you."

A few weeks later the sale was completed, and at the end of the summer the Minister and his family went down to Canterville Chase.

Mrs Otis had been a famous beauty in New York. She was now a handsome woman of middle age, with fine eyes and excellent health. Many American ladies like to pretend that they are always ill when they come to Europe. They think it is very European. Mrs Otis had never made this mistake. In many ways she was quite English, which shows that the English and the Americans have everything in common nowadays, except, of course, language.

Her eldest son, whom his parents had named Washington in a moment of patriotism, was a fair-haired, rather good-looking young man. He was famous for his dancing at parties in Newport. He also knew a great deal about flowers, which was his only weakness. Otherwise he was extremely sensible.

Miss Virginia E. Otis was a little girl of fifteen, as light and lovely as a young deer, with a fine freedom in her large blue eyes. She was a wonderful horse rider. Once she had raced old Lord Bilton on her pony twice round the park. She won by a length and a half, just in front of the statue of Achilles. The young Duke of Cheshire, who was watching, fell in love with her immediately. His guardians sent him back to school at Eton that same night, and he went away in tears.

After Virginia came the twins, who were usually called "The Stars and Stripes" because they were always being punished. They were delightful boys and, apart from the Minister himself, the only true republicans in the family.

Canterville Chase was seven miles from Ascot, the nearest railway station, so Mr Otis had sent a message asking for a carriage to meet them. They started their drive in high spirits. It was a lovely July evening, and the air was full of the warm smell of the pine woods. Now and then they heard a wood pigeon singing sweetly to itself. In the long grass they could see the white breast of a pheasant. Little squirrels watched them from the beech trees as they went past. The rabbits ran away through the bushes with their white tails in the air.

However, as they turned into the long drive that led up to Canterville Chase, the sky suddenly became dark with clouds. A strange silence seemed to fill the air. A great flock of black birds passed silently over their heads, and before they reached the house, large drops of rain had begun to fall.

An old woman was waiting for them on the steps. She was neatly dressed in black silk, with a white cap and apron. This was Mrs Umney, the housekeeper. Lady Canterville had asked Mrs Otis to keep her in her old job, and Mrs Otis had kindly agreed. She made a low curtsy to each of them as they got out of the carriage.

"I welcome you to Canterville Chase," she said in a strange, old-fashioned way.

They followed her through the fine Tudor hall into the library. It was a long, low room with dark oak walls. At the end there was a large window of coloured glass. Tea had been prepared for them there. They took off their coats and sat down, and while Mrs Umney served them, they looked around the room.

Suddenly Mrs Otis noticed a dull red stain on the floor, just by the fireplace. She had no idea what it really was.

"I am afraid something has been spilt there," she said to Mrs Umney.

"Yes, madam," replied the old housekeeper in a low voice. "Blood has been spilt on that spot."

"How horrible!" cried Mrs Otis. "I don't like blood stains in a sitting room at all. It must be removed at once."

The old woman smiled and answered in the same low, mysterious voice.

"It is the blood of Lady Eleanore de Canterville. She was murdered on that very spot by her own husband, Sir Simon de Canterville, in 1575. Sir Simon lived for nine years after that, and then he disappeared suddenly in very mysterious circumstances. His body has never been found, but his guilty spirit still haunts the Chase. The blood stain has always been admired by tourists and other visitors, and it cannot be removed."

"That is all nonsense," cried Washington Otis. "Pinkerton's Champion Stain Remover and Paragon Detergent will clean it up in no time."

Before the frightened housekeeper could stop him, he had fallen on his knees. He took a small black stick out of his pocket and began to rub the floor quickly. In a few moments there was no sign of the blood stain at all.

"I knew Pinkerton would do it," he said proudly, and he looked round at his admiring family.

But as soon as he had said these words, a terrible flash of lightning lit up the dark room. A great crash of thunder made them all jump to their feet, and Mrs Umney fainted.

"What a terrible climate!" said the American Minister calmly, as he lit a long cigar. "I suppose this old country has so many people that there is not enough good weather for everybody. I have always believed that emigration is the only answer for England."

"My dear Hiram," cried Mrs Otis, "what can we do with a woman who faints?"

"Take it out of her wages, as if she had broken something," answered the Minister. "She won't faint after that."

In a few moments Mrs Umney opened her eyes. There was no doubt, however, that she was very upset, and she warned Mr Otis seriously that trouble was coming to the house.

"I have seen things with my own eyes, sir," she said, "that would make any Christian's hair stand on end. Many nights I have not closed my eyes because of the awful things that happen here."

Mr Otis and his wife told the honest woman kindly that they were not afraid of ghosts. They promised to raise her wages. She asked for God's blessing on her new master and mistress, and then the old housekeeper went slowly upstairs to her own room.

Chapter 2: Chains in the Night

The storm continued wildly all through the night, but nothing special happened. The next morning, however, when the family came down to breakfast, they found the terrible blood stain on the library floor once again.

"I don't think it can be the fault of the Paragon Detergent," said Washington. "I have tried it with everything. It must be the ghost."

So he rubbed out the stain a second time. But on the following morning it was there again. On the third morning it was there too, although Mr Otis himself had locked the library door the night before and had carried the key upstairs.

The whole family was now very interested. Mr Otis began to think that perhaps he had been too dogmatic when he said that ghosts did not exist. Mrs Otis talked about joining a society for the study of the supernatural. Washington wrote a long letter to two famous scientists. He asked them how blood stains could stay in the same place for centuries when a crime had been committed there. That night, all their doubts about the ghost were removed for ever.

The day had been warm and sunny, and in the cool of the evening the whole family went out for a drive. They did not return home until nine o'clock, when they had a light supper. The conversation did not turn to ghosts at all. They talked, as cultured Americans always do, about the theatre and the great difficulty of travelling by train in England. Then they talked about the importance of New York as a world city. Nobody mentioned anything supernatural, and nobody spoke of Sir Simon de Canterville. At eleven o'clock the family went to bed, and by half past eleven all the lights were out.

Some time later, Mr Otis was woken by a strange noise in the corridor outside his room. It sounded like the clank of metal, and it seemed to be coming nearer every moment. He got up at once, struck a match and looked at the time. It was exactly one o'clock. He was quite calm, and he felt his pulse, which was not at all fast. The strange noise continued, and now he could also hear the sound of footsteps.

He put on his slippers, took a small bottle out of his dressing case and opened the door. Right in front of him, in the pale moonlight, stood an old man of terrible appearance. His eyes were as red as burning coals. Long grey hair fell over his shoulders in knots, and his old-fashioned clothes were dirty and torn. Heavy, rusty chains hung from his wrists and his ankles.

"My dear sir," said Mr Otis, "I really must insist that you oil those chains. I have brought you a small bottle of the Tammany Rising Sun Lubricator for that purpose. It is said to work perfectly after only one use, and several of our most famous priests have written letters to the newspapers praising it. I shall leave it here for you by the bedroom candles, and I will be happy to give you more if you need it."

With these words the Minister of the United States put the bottle down on a marble table, closed his door and went back to bed.

For a moment the Canterville ghost stood quite still in natural anger. Then he threw the bottle violently onto the floor. He ran down the corridor, giving out a deep groan and shining with a strange green light. However, just as he reached the top of the great oak staircase, a door was flung open. Two little figures in white appeared, and a large pillow flew past his head!

There was clearly no time to lose. The ghost quickly escaped through the wall, using the fourth dimension of space as a way out, and the house became quite quiet again.

He reached a small secret room in the left wing of the house. There he leaned against a moonbeam to get his breath back and began to think about his position. Never, in a brilliant and uninterrupted career of three hundred years, had he been so badly insulted.

He thought of the Dowager Duchess, whom he had frightened into a fit as she stood before the mirror in her lace and diamonds. He thought of the four housemaids, who had screamed and screamed when he simply grinned at them through the curtains of a spare bedroom. He remembered the rector of the parish, whose candle he had blown out one night as he was coming late out of the library. Ever since that night the poor man had needed a doctor for his nerves.

He remembered old Madame de Tremouillac too. She had woken up early one morning and seen a skeleton sitting in an armchair by the fire, reading her diary. She had stayed in bed for six weeks with brain fever. When she recovered, she became a very religious woman and broke off all connection with the famous unbeliever Monsieur de Voltaire.

He remembered the terrible night when the wicked Lord Canterville was found choking in his dressing room. The knave of diamonds was halfway down his throat. Before he died, the old lord confessed that he had cheated at cards. He had won fifty thousand pounds from a friend. He swore that the ghost had made him swallow the card.

All his great performances came back to him. There was the butler, who had shot himself in the pantry because he had seen a green hand tapping at the window. There was the beautiful Lady Stutfield. She always had to wear a black velvet band round her throat to hide the marks of five fingers burnt on her white skin. In the end she drowned herself in the fish pond at the end of the King's Walk.

With the pride of a true artist, he went through his most famous roles. He smiled bitterly as he remembered his last appearance as "Red Reuben, or the Strangled Baby". He remembered his first night as "Gaunt Gibeon, the Blood-drinker of Bexley Moor". And he remembered the excitement of one lovely June evening, when he had simply played a game of skittles with his own bones on the tennis court.

And after all this, some miserable modern Americans were going to offer him the Rising Sun Lubricator and throw pillows at his head! It was quite unbearable. Besides, no ghost in history had ever been treated in this way.

He decided, therefore, to take his revenge, and he stayed until daylight in deep thought.

Chapter 3: The Stain Changes Colour

The next morning, when the Otis family met at breakfast, they discussed the ghost for some time. The Minister was naturally a little annoyed to find that his present had not been accepted.

"I have no wish," he said, "to do the ghost any personal injury. Considering how long he has been in the house, I don't think it is polite to throw pillows at him."

This was a very just remark, but I am sorry to say that the twins burst out laughing when they heard it.

"On the other hand," Mr Otis went on, "if he really refuses to use the Rising Sun Lubricator, we shall have to take his chains from him. It would be quite impossible to sleep with such a noise going on outside the bedrooms."

For the rest of the week, however, they were not disturbed at all. The only thing that attracted any attention was the blood stain on the library floor, which reappeared every morning. This was certainly very strange, as the door was always locked at night by Mr Otis and the windows were carefully closed.

The stain also changed its colour, which was even stranger. Some mornings it was a dull, almost brownish red. On other mornings it was a bright red, then a rich purple. Once, when the family came down for morning prayers, they found it a bright emerald green.

These changes amused the family very much, and every evening they made bets on the colour of the stain the next morning. The only person who did not join in the joke was little Virginia. For some reason, she was always rather unhappy at the sight of the blood stain. On the morning when it was emerald green, she very nearly cried.

The ghost made his second appearance on Sunday night. Shortly after the family had gone to bed, they were suddenly woken by a terrible crash in the hall. They rushed downstairs and found that a large suit of old armour had fallen from its stand onto the stone floor. In a high-backed chair, rubbing his knees with an expression of great pain, sat the Canterville ghost.

The twins had brought their pea-shooters with them. They immediately fired two little balls at him with great accuracy, because they had practised for years on their teachers. The United States Minister pointed his revolver at him and called upon him, in the Californian manner, to hold up his hands!

The ghost jumped up with a wild scream of anger and passed through them like a mist. On the way he blew out Washington Otis's candle and left them all in complete darkness.

When he reached the top of the staircase, he recovered himself and decided to give his famous demonic laugh. He had found this extremely useful on more than one occasion. People said that it had turned Lord Raker's hair white in a single night. It had certainly made three of Lady Canterville's French governesses leave before their month was up.

So he laughed his most horrible laugh, until the old ceiling rang and rang again. But as soon as the fearful echo died away, a door opened, and Mrs Otis came out in a light blue dressing gown.

"I am afraid you are far from well," she said, "and I have brought you a bottle of Dr Dobell's medicine. If it is indigestion, you will find it a most excellent remedy."

The ghost looked at her in fury, and immediately began to prepare himself to turn into a large black dog. He was rightly famous for this skill. The family doctor always said that it had caused the permanent madness of Lord Canterville's uncle, the Honourable Thomas Horton. However, the sound of approaching footsteps made him hesitate. In the end he simply became faintly luminous and disappeared with a deep, graveyard groan, just as the twins came up to him.

When he reached his room, he broke down completely and became the victim of the most violent emotion. The bad manners of the twins and the terrible materialism of Mrs Otis were naturally extremely annoying. But what really upset him most was that he had been unable to wear the suit of armour.

He had hoped that even modern Americans would be excited by a ghost in armour. After all, they loved their national poet Longfellow, who wrote about knights. After all, he had spent many weary hours reading his graceful and attractive poetry himself, when the Cantervilles were up in town. Besides, it was his own suit. He had worn it with great success at a famous tournament. Queen Elizabeth herself had praised him for it.

Yet when he had put it on, the huge breastplate and the steel helmet were far too heavy for him. He had fallen heavily on the stone floor. He had cut both his knees badly and hurt the fingers of his right hand.

For some days after this he was extremely ill. He hardly left his room at all, except to keep the blood stain in proper repair. However, by taking great care of himself, he recovered. He then decided to make a third attempt to frighten the United States Minister and his family.

He chose Friday the seventeenth of August for his appearance, and he spent most of that day looking through his wardrobe. In the end he decided on a large hat with a red feather, a burial sheet torn at the wrists and neck, and a rusty dagger. Towards evening a violent storm of rain came on. The wind was so strong that all the windows and doors in the old house shook and rattled. In fact, it was just the kind of weather that he loved.

Chapter 4: A Ghost Meets a Ghost

The ghost had made his plan very carefully. First he would go quietly to the room of Washington Otis. That young man was the enemy who had cleaned away the famous blood stain with his Pinkerton's Paragon Detergent. The ghost would stand at the foot of his bed and talk nonsense to him from behind the curtains. Then he would stab himself three times in the throat, to the sound of soft, slow music.

He was very angry with Washington, and he had no wish to be gentle with him. Next, he would go to the room of the Minister and his wife. There he would place a cold, wet hand on Mrs Otis's forehead. At the same time he would whisper the terrible secrets of the dead into her trembling husband's ear.

He had not quite decided about little Virginia. She had never insulted him in any way, and she was pretty and gentle. A few deep groans from inside the wardrobe would be enough for her, he thought. If she did not wake up, he might pull at her blanket with shaking fingers.

But the twins were different. He was going to teach them a lesson they would never forget. First, he would sit on their chests, so that they would have terrible dreams. Their beds stood very close together, so he would then stand between them in the form of a green, ice-cold corpse. They would be frozen with fear. Finally, he would throw off his burial sheet and walk round the room as "Dead Daniel, or the Suicide's Skeleton", with white bones and one rolling eye. It was a role that had always been very successful. He considered it as good as his famous "Mad Martin, or the Masked Mystery".

At half past ten he heard the family going to bed. For some time the wild laughter of the twins disturbed him. They were clearly enjoying themselves before they went to sleep, as schoolboys do. But at quarter past eleven everything was still. When midnight struck, he started out.

The owl beat against the windows, and the raven called from the old tree. The wind went round the house, moaning like a lost soul. But the Otis family slept on, without any idea of what was coming. Above the noise of the rain and the storm, the ghost could hear the regular snoring of the Minister of the United States.

He stepped silently out of the wall with an evil smile on his cruel, wrinkled mouth. The moon hid her face behind a cloud as he passed the great window. His own coat of arms was painted there in blue and gold, next to the arms of his murdered wife. On and on he went, like an evil shadow. The darkness itself seemed to hate him as he passed. Once he thought he heard something calling, and he stopped. But it was only the barking of a dog from the farm, and he went on. Now and then he whispered strange old curses and waved his rusty dagger in the midnight air.

At last he reached the corner of the corridor that led to poor Washington's room. For a moment he waited there. The wind blew his long grey hair about his head and twisted his burial sheet into strange, horrible shapes.

Then the clock struck the quarter. He felt that the time had come. He laughed quietly to himself and turned the corner.

But as soon as he did so, he fell back with a cry of terror. He hid his white face in his long, bony hands.

Right in front of him stood a horrible ghost! It was as still as a statue and as ugly as a madman's dream. Its head was bald and shining, and its face was round, fat and white. A terrible laugh seemed to have frozen its features into an endless grin. Rays of red light came from its eyes, and its mouth was a wide well of fire. It wore a great white robe, like his own, which covered its huge body. On its chest there was a sign with strange writing in old letters. In its right hand it held up a long sword of shining steel.

The Canterville ghost had never seen a ghost before, and naturally he was terribly frightened. He took a second quick look at the awful creature. Then he ran back to his room as fast as he could. On the way he tripped over his long burial sheet, and he dropped the rusty dagger into the Minister's riding boots. The butler found it there in the morning.

Once he was in the safety of his own room, he threw himself down on a small bed and hid his face under the blankets. After some time, however, the brave old Canterville spirit returned to him. He decided to go and speak to the other ghost as soon as it was light.

"Two ghosts are better than one," he said to himself. "With the help of my new friend, I can easily deal with those terrible twins."

So, just as the dawn was turning the hills to silver, he went back to the place where he had first seen the strange ghost. But when he got there, a horrible sight met his eyes.

Something had clearly happened to the ghost. The light had gone from its empty eyes, and the shining sword had fallen from its hand. It was leaning against the wall in a strange and uncomfortable position. He rushed forward and took it in his arms. To his horror, the head slipped off and rolled across the floor! The body fell onto its back. He found that he was holding a white bed curtain, with a kitchen knife and an empty turnip at his feet.

He could not understand this strange change at all. With feverish hands he picked up the sign, and in the grey morning light he read these terrible words:

"YE OTIS GHOSTE. YE ONLIE TRUE AND ORIGINALE SPOOK. BEWARE OF YE IMITATIONES. ALL OTHERS ARE FALSE."

Suddenly he understood everything. He had been tricked and laughed at! The old Canterville look came into his eyes. He bit his teeth together, and he raised his thin hands high above his head.

He cried out in the strange old language of his youth. "When the cock has crowed twice, "there will be blood, and murder will walk in this house without a sound!"

Hardly had he finished this awful promise when a cock crowed from the red roof of a distant farmhouse. The ghost laughed a long, low, bitter laugh, and he waited. Hour after hour he waited. But for some strange reason, the cock did not crow again.

Finally, at half past seven, the housemaids arrived to clean the corridor. He had to give up his terrible watch, and he walked sadly back to his room. He thought of his hopeless promise and his failed plans. Then he looked through several of his favourite old books about knights and ghosts. In each of them, the cock had always crowed twice when somebody made a promise like his.

"I wish that wicked bird were dead," he whispered. "In the old days I would have run through its neck with my good sword. It would have crowed again for me, even if it were dying!"

Then he went to lie down in a comfortable coffin of lead, and he stayed there until evening.

Chapter 5: Tricks and Tricks

The next day the ghost felt very weak and very tired. The terrible excitement of the last four weeks was beginning to have its effect. His nerves were completely broken, and he jumped at the smallest noise. For five days he did not leave his room at all. In the end he decided to give up the blood stain on the library floor.

"If the Otis family do not want it," he said, "they clearly do not deserve it. They are obviously people who live on a low, material level of existence. They cannot understand the value of real feelings."

The question of supernatural appearances, however, was quite a different matter. He could not control that. It was his solemn duty to appear in the corridor once a week. He also had to talk nonsense from the great window on the first and third Wednesday of every month. He could not see how he could honourably escape from these duties.

It is true that his life had been very evil. But in everything connected with the supernatural, he was extremely serious. So for the next three Saturdays he walked along the corridor as usual between midnight and three o'clock. He took every possible care not to be heard or seen.

He took off his boots and walked as lightly as possible on the old wooden floor. He wore a large black velvet cloak, and he was careful to oil his chains with the Rising Sun Lubricator. I must admit that it was not easy for him to accept this last idea. However, one night, while the family were having dinner, he went secretly into Mr Otis's bedroom and took the bottle. He felt a little ashamed at first, but he was sensible enough to see that the oil was very useful. To a certain degree, it helped him in his plans.

Still, in spite of everything, he was not left in peace. Strings were tied across the corridor in the dark, and he fell over them. On one occasion, when he was dressed for the role of "Black Isaac, or the Huntsman of Hogley Woods", he had a bad fall. The twins had covered the floor with butter, from the door of the Tapestry Chamber to the top of the oak staircase.

This last insult made him so angry that he decided to make one final effort. He would visit the twins on the following night in his famous character of "Reckless Rupert, or the Headless Earl".

He had not appeared in this costume for more than seventy years. The last time, he had given such a fright to the pretty Lady Barbara Modish that she broke off her engagement to the present Lord Canterville's grandfather. She ran away to Gretna Green with handsome Jack Castleton. Nothing in the world, she said, would make her marry into a family with such a horrible ghost. Poor Jack was later shot in a duel by Lord Canterville, and Lady Barbara died of a broken heart before the end of the year. So in every way the appearance had been a great success.

However, it was a very difficult costume to put on. The ghost needed three full hours to get ready. At last everything was perfect, and he was very pleased with his appearance. The big leather riding boots were a little too large for him. He could only find one of the two horse pistols. But on the whole he was quite satisfied.

At quarter past one he slipped out of the wall and crept down the corridor. When he reached the room of the twins, he found that the door was slightly open. He wanted to make an effective entrance, so he flung it wide open.

At that moment a heavy jug of water fell right down on him. It soaked him to the skin, and it missed his left shoulder by only a few inches. At the same time he heard loud shouts of laughter from the big bed.

The shock to his nervous system was so great that he ran back to his room as fast as he could. The next day he was in bed with a bad cold. The only thing that comforted him in the whole affair was that he had not brought his head with him. If he had, the results might have been very serious.

After this he gave up all hope of ever frightening this rude American family. As a rule, he was happy to creep around the corridors in his slippers, with a thick red scarf round his throat against the cold draughts. He also carried a small gun, in case the twins attacked him.

The final blow came on the nineteenth of September. He had gone downstairs to the great entrance hall, where he felt sure that he would be safe. He wanted to make fun of the large photographs of the Minister and his wife. They now hung where the Canterville family pictures had once been.

He was simply but neatly dressed in a long burial sheet, with spots of dirt from the churchyard. He had tied up his jaw with a strip of yellow cloth, and he carried a small lamp and a gravedigger's spade. In fact, he was dressed for the character of "Jonas the Graveless, or the Body-Snatcher of Chertsey Barn". It was one of his most remarkable roles, and the Cantervilles had every reason to remember it. It had been the real cause of their quarrel with their neighbour, Lord Rufford.

It was about quarter past two in the morning, and as far as he could tell, nobody was awake. However, as he was walking slowly towards the library, two figures suddenly jumped out at him from a dark corner. They waved their arms wildly above their heads and screamed "BOO!" in his ear.

The poor ghost was filled with panic, which was only natural in the circumstances. He ran towards the staircase, but Washington Otis was waiting for him there with the big garden hose. He was surrounded by his enemies on every side and almost trapped. In the end he disappeared into the great iron stove, which, luckily for him, was not lit. He had to make his way home through the chimneys, and he arrived at his own room terribly dirty, untidy and miserable.

After this night he was never seen again on any night-time adventure. The twins waited for him on several occasions. Every night they put nut shells on the floor of the corridor, to the great annoyance of their parents and the servants, but it was no use. It was quite clear that the ghost's feelings were so deeply hurt that he would not appear.

Mr Otis therefore went back to work on his great book about the history of the Democratic Party, which he had been writing for some years. Mrs Otis organised a wonderful clambake, a seafood party in the American style, which amazed the whole county. The boys took up lacrosse, and Virginia rode her pony up and down the country lanes. It was generally believed that the ghost had gone away. In fact, Mr Otis wrote a letter to Lord Canterville to tell him the news. In reply, Lord Canterville expressed his great pleasure and sent his best wishes to the Minister's wife.

Chapter 6: Virginia and the Ghost

The Otis family, however, were wrong. The ghost was still in the house, and although he was now almost an invalid, he was not ready to give up. This was especially true when he heard that among the guests was the young Duke of Cheshire.

The Duke's uncle, Lord Francis Stilton, had once made a bet of a hundred guineas with Colonel Carbury that he would play dice with the Canterville ghost. The next morning he was found on the floor of the card room. He was so badly paralysed that, although he lived to a great age, he could never say anything again except "Double sixes". The story was well known at the time, although, out of respect for the two noble families, everybody had tried to keep it quiet.

The ghost was naturally anxious to show that he had not lost his old power over the Stiltons, who were distant relatives of his. So he made plans to appear before Virginia's young lover in his celebrated role of "The Vampire Monk, or the Bloodless Benedictine". It was such a terrible performance that when old Lady Startup saw it on a fatal New Year's Eve in 1764, she screamed loudly and had a serious fit. She died three days later. Before she died, she took everything she owned away from the Cantervilles, who were her nearest relatives, and left it all to her London chemist.

At the last moment, however, the ghost's terror of the twins stopped him from leaving his room. So the little Duke slept in peace under the great feathered canopy of the royal bedroom. He dreamed of Virginia.

A few days later, Virginia and her curly-haired young gentleman went out riding in the meadows. She tore her dress badly on a hedge. When she got home, she decided to go up by the back staircase so that nobody would see her.

As she was running past the Tapestry Chamber, she noticed that the door was open. She thought she saw somebody inside, and she went in. She expected to find her mother's maid, who sometimes brought her sewing to this room.

To her great surprise, however, it was the Canterville ghost himself! He was sitting by the window, watching the golden autumn leaves fly through the air and the red leaves dance madly down the long avenue. His head was resting on his hand, and he looked very sad and lonely. In fact, he looked so tired and miserable that little Virginia forgot her first idea, which was to run away and lock herself in her room. Instead, she felt sorry for him, and she decided to try and comfort him.

Her step was so light, and his sadness was so deep, that he did not notice her until she spoke to him.

"I am so sorry for you," she said, "but my brothers are going back to Eton tomorrow. Then, if you behave yourself, nobody will annoy you."

"It is ridiculous to ask me to behave myself," he answered. He looked round in surprise at the pretty little girl who had dared to speak to him. "Quite ridiculous. I must rattle my chains, and groan through keyholes, and walk about at night. That is what you mean, I suppose? It is my only reason for existing."

"It is no reason at all for existing," said Virginia. "You know that you have been very wicked. Mrs Umney told us on the first day we arrived here that you had killed your wife."

"Well, I admit it," said the ghost crossly. "But it was a purely family matter. It concerned nobody else."

"It is very wrong to kill anybody," said Virginia. She could be very serious sometimes, like someone from the old Puritan families of New England.

"Oh, I hate the cheap seriousness of these moral ideas!" said the ghost. "My wife was very plain. She never starched my collars properly, and she knew nothing about cooking. Once I shot a fine deer in Hogley Woods, and do you know how she sent it to the table? But that does not matter now, for it is all over. Still, I don't think it was very nice of her brothers to starve me to death, even though I did kill her."

"Starve you to death? Oh, Mr Ghost, I mean Sir Simon, are you hungry? I have a sandwich in my bag. Would you like it?"

"No, thank you. I never eat anything now. But it is very kind of you all the same. You are much nicer than the rest of your horrible, rude, dishonest family."

"Stop!" cried Virginia, and she stamped her foot. "It is you who are rude and horrible. And as for dishonesty, you know that you stole the paints out of my box to make that ridiculous blood stain in the library. First you took all my reds, including the bright red, so I could not paint any more sunsets. Then you took the emerald green and the yellow. In the end I had nothing left except dark blue and white, and I could only paint moonlight scenes. They are always sad to look at, and not at all easy to paint. I never told anybody about it, although I was very annoyed. And the whole thing was so silly! Who ever heard of emerald green blood?"

"Well, really," said the ghost, rather quietly, "what was I to do? It is very difficult to get real blood nowadays. And as your brother began it all with his Paragon Detergent, I really saw no reason why I should not have your paints. As for colour, that is always a matter of taste. The Cantervilles have blue blood, for example, the bluest in England. But I know that you Americans don't care for things of this kind."

"No, we don't," said Virginia. "But you could easily come to America. My father will be very glad to give you a free ticket. There is a heavy tax on spirits of every kind, but there will be no difficulty with the customs. All the officers are Democrats. And once you are in New York, you are sure to be a great success. I know lots of people there who would give a hundred thousand dollars to have a grandfather. They would give much more than that to have a family ghost."

"I don't think I would like America," said the ghost.

"I suppose that is because we have no ruins and no old things," said Virginia, a little sharply.

"No ruins! No old things!" answered the ghost. "You have your navy and your manners."

"Good evening," said Virginia. "I am going to ask Papa to get the twins an extra week's holiday."

Chapter 7: The Garden of Death

"Please don't go, Miss Virginia," cried the ghost. "I am so lonely and so unhappy, and I really don't know what to do. I want to go to sleep, and I cannot."

"That's quite silly!" said Virginia. "You only have to go to bed and blow out the candle. Sometimes it is very difficult to stay awake, especially at church. But there is no difficulty at all about sleeping. Even babies know how to do that, and they are not very clever."

"I have not slept for three hundred years," he said sadly. Virginia's beautiful blue eyes opened wide in surprise. "For three hundred years I have not slept, and I am so tired."

Virginia became very serious, and her little lips trembled like rose leaves. She came towards him, knelt down at his side and looked up into his old, tired face.

"Poor, poor ghost," she said softly. "Have you no place where you can sleep?"

"Far away, beyond the pine woods," he answered in a low, dreamy voice, "there is a little garden. There the grass grows long and deep. There are the great white stars of the hemlock flower, and the nightingale sings all night long. All night long he sings, and the cold moon looks down, and the old yew tree spreads out its giant arms over the sleepers."

Virginia's eyes filled with tears, and she hid her face in her hands.

"You mean the Garden of Death," she whispered.

"Yes, Death. Death must be so beautiful. To lie in the soft brown earth, with the grasses waving above your head, and to listen to silence. To have no yesterday and no tomorrow. To forget time, to forgive life, to be at peace. You can help me. You can open for me the doors of Death's house, for Love is always with you, and Love is stronger than Death is."

Virginia trembled, and a cold shiver ran through her. For a few moments there was silence. She felt as if she were in a terrible dream.

Then the ghost spoke again, and his voice sounded like the sighing of the wind.

"Have you ever read the old prophecy on the library window?"

"Oh, often," cried the little girl, looking up. "I know it quite well. It is painted in strange black letters, and it is difficult to read. There are only six lines."

When a golden girl can win
Prayer from out the lips of sin,
When the empty almond tree bears fruit,
And a little child gives away its tears,
Then shall all the house be still
And peace come to Canterville.

"But I don't know what they mean," she added.

"They mean," he said sadly, "that you must weep with me for my sins, because I have no tears. You must pray with me for my soul, because I have no faith. And then, if you have always been sweet and good and gentle, the Angel of Death will have mercy on me. You will see terrible shapes in the darkness, and wicked voices will whisper in your ear. But they will not hurt you, because the powers of Hell cannot win against the purity of a little child."

Virginia did not answer. The ghost looked down at her bowed golden head and wrung his hands in wild despair.

Suddenly she stood up. She was very pale, and there was a strange light in her eyes.

"I am not afraid," she said firmly. "I will ask the Angel to have mercy on you."

He rose from his seat with a faint cry of joy. He took her hand, bent over it in the old-fashioned way and kissed it. His fingers were as cold as ice, and his lips burned like fire. But Virginia did not hesitate as he led her across the dark room.

On the faded green tapestry, little hunters were embroidered. They blew their horns and waved their tiny hands to her, warning her to go back.

"Go back, little Virginia!" they cried. "Go back!"

But the ghost held her hand more tightly, and she shut her eyes against them. Horrible animals with lizard tails and wide eyes watched her from the carved fireplace.

"Beware, little Virginia, beware!" they whispered. "We may never see you again!"

But the ghost moved on more quickly, and Virginia did not listen.

When they reached the end of the room, he stopped and said some words that she could not understand. She opened her eyes and saw the wall slowly disappearing like a mist. A great black cave opened in front of her. A bitter cold wind came round them, and she felt something pulling at her dress.

"Quick, quick," cried the ghost, "or it will be too late."

In a moment the wall had closed behind them, and the Tapestry Chamber was empty.

Chapter 8: Where is Virginia?

About ten minutes later, the bell rang for tea. Virginia did not come down, so Mrs Otis sent one of the servants up to fetch her. After a little time he returned and said that he could not find Miss Virginia anywhere.

Virginia often went out to the garden in the evening to get flowers for the dinner table, so Mrs Otis was not worried at first. But six o'clock struck, and Virginia did not appear. Now her mother became really anxious. She sent the boys to look for her in the garden, while she and Mr Otis searched every room in the house.

At half past six the boys came back. They said that they could not find any sign of their sister anywhere. Everybody was now in a state of terrible excitement, and nobody knew what to do.

Then Mr Otis suddenly remembered something. A few days before, he had given a group of gipsies permission to camp in the park.

"Perhaps they have taken her," he said.

He set off at once for Blackfell Hollow, where he knew the gipsies were staying. His eldest son and two of the farm workers went with him. The little Duke of Cheshire was terribly worried. He begged to go with them, but Mr Otis would not allow it, because he was afraid there might be a fight.

When they arrived at the spot, however, they found that the gipsies had gone. They had clearly left in a great hurry, because the fire was still burning, and some plates were lying on the grass.

Mr Otis sent Washington and the two men to search the district. Then he ran home and sent telegrams to all the police inspectors in the county. He asked them to look out for a little girl who had been kidnapped by tramps or gipsies. After that he ordered his horse to be brought round. He told his wife and the three boys to sit down to dinner, and he rode off down the Ascot road with a servant.

He had hardly gone a couple of miles, however, when he heard somebody riding fast behind him. He looked round and saw the little Duke coming up on his pony. The boy's face was very red, and he had no hat.

"I'm awfully sorry, Mr Otis," said the boy, out of breath, "but I can't eat any dinner while Virginia is lost. Please don't be angry with me. If you had let us be engaged last year, there would never have been all this trouble. You won't send me back, will you? I can't go! I won't go!"

The Minister could not help smiling at the handsome young boy. He was quite touched by his love for Virginia.

"Well, Cecil," he said, leaning down from his horse and patting him on the shoulder, "if you won't go back, I suppose you must come with me. But I must get you a hat at Ascot."

"Oh, I don't care about hats! I want Virginia!" cried the little Duke, laughing.

So they rode on together to the railway station. There Mr Otis asked the station master if anybody like Virginia had been seen on the platform. But nobody had seen her. The station master sent telegrams up and down the line, and he promised to watch out for her.

Mr Otis bought a hat for the little Duke from a shop that was just closing. Then they rode on to Bexley, a village about four miles away. He had heard that the gipsies often went there, because there was a large common next to it. They woke up the village policeman, but they could get no information from him. They rode all over the common, but found nothing.

At last, at about eleven o'clock, they turned their horses' heads for home. They reached the Chase tired and almost heartbroken. Washington and the twins were waiting for them at the gate with lanterns, because the avenue was very dark. They had found no sign of Virginia.

The gipsies had been caught in Brockley meadows, but she was not with them. They explained that they had left the camp so suddenly because they had made a mistake about the date of Chorton Fair. They had been afraid that they would arrive too late. In fact, they were very sorry to hear that Virginia had disappeared. They had been very grateful to Mr Otis for allowing them to camp in his park, and four of them stayed behind to help in the search.

The fish pond had been searched, and every corner of the Chase had been examined, but without any result. It was clear that, for that night at least, Virginia was lost to them. Mr Otis and the boys walked up to the house in deep sadness. The servant followed behind with the two horses and the pony.

In the hall they found a group of frightened servants. Lying on a sofa in the library was poor Mrs Otis. She was almost out of her mind with fear and worry, and the old housekeeper was bathing her forehead with eau de Cologne.

Mr Otis immediately insisted that she should have something to eat, and he ordered supper for the whole family. It was a sad meal. Hardly anybody spoke, and even the twins were silent and serious, because they loved their sister very much.

When they had finished, Mr Otis sent everybody to bed, although the little Duke begged to stay up.

"Nothing more can be done tonight," said the Minister. "In the morning I will telegraph to Scotland Yard and ask them to send some detectives at once."

Chapter 9: The Secret Room

Just as they were leaving the dining room, the clock began to strike midnight. When the last stroke sounded, they heard a crash and a sudden cry. A terrible crash of thunder shook the house, and strange, unearthly music floated through the air.

A panel at the top of the staircase flew back with a loud noise. Out onto the landing stepped Virginia! She was very pale and white, and she had a little box in her hand.

In a moment they had all rushed up to her. Mrs Otis held her passionately in her arms. The Duke covered her face with violent kisses, and the twins began a wild war dance round the group.

"Good heavens, child! Where have you been?" said Mr Otis, rather angrily. He thought that she had been playing some foolish trick on them. "Cecil and I have been riding all over the country looking for you, and your mother has been frightened to death. You must never play these practical jokes again."

"Except on the ghost! Except on the ghost!" shouted the twins, as they jumped about.

"My darling, thank God you are found. You must never leave my side again," whispered Mrs Otis. She kissed the trembling child and smoothed her golden hair.

"Papa," said Virginia quietly, "I have been with the ghost. He is dead, and you must come and see him. He was very wicked, but he was really sorry for everything he had done. He gave me this box of beautiful jewels before he died."

The whole family looked at her in silent surprise. But she was quite serious. She turned and led them through the opening in the wall and down a narrow secret corridor. Washington followed with a lighted candle, which he had taken from the table.

At last they came to a great oak door, covered with rusty nails. When Virginia touched it, it opened slowly on its heavy hinges. They found themselves in a little low room with a curved ceiling and one tiny window with bars.

A huge iron ring was fixed into the wall, and a long chain was attached to it. Chained to the ring was a thin skeleton. It was stretched out at full length on the stone floor. It seemed to be trying to reach, with its long, fleshless fingers, an old-fashioned plate and a jug. They had been placed just out of its reach. The jug had clearly once been filled with water, because its inside was covered with green mould. There was nothing on the plate except a pile of dust.

Virginia knelt down beside the skeleton. She folded her little hands together and began to pray silently. Meanwhile the rest of the family looked in wonder at the terrible scene, whose secret was now revealed to them.

"Look!" cried one of the twins suddenly. He had been looking out of the window to try to discover which wing of the house the room was in. "Look! The old dry almond tree is in flower. I can see the flowers quite clearly in the moonlight."

"God has forgiven him," said Virginia seriously, as she stood up. A beautiful light seemed to shine on her face.

"What an angel you are!" cried the young Duke. He put his arm round her neck and kissed her.

Chapter 10: Peace at Canterville

Four days after these strange events, a funeral left Canterville Chase at about eleven o'clock at night. The coffin was carried on a carriage pulled by eight black horses. Each horse had a great tuft of black ostrich feathers on its head. The coffin was covered with a rich purple cloth, with the Canterville coat of arms embroidered on it in gold.

By the side of the carriage and the coaches walked the servants with lighted torches. The whole procession was wonderfully impressive. Lord Canterville was the chief mourner. He had come up from Wales especially to attend the funeral, and he sat in the first carriage with little Virginia. Then came the United States Minister and his wife, then Washington and the three boys. In the last carriage was Mrs Umney. Everybody felt that, as the ghost had frightened her for more than fifty years of her life, she had a right to see the end of him.

A deep grave had been dug in the corner of the churchyard, just under the old yew tree. The service was read in the most impressive way by the Reverend Augustus Dampier. When the ceremony was over, the servants put out their torches, as was the old custom in the Canterville family.

As the coffin was lowered into the grave, Virginia stepped forward. She laid on it a large cross made of white and pink almond flowers. At that moment the moon came out from behind a cloud and filled the little churchyard with its silent silver light. From a distant wood a nightingale began to sing. Virginia thought of the ghost's description of the Garden of Death. Her eyes filled with tears, and she hardly said a word during the drive home.

The next morning, before Lord Canterville went back to town, Mr Otis spoke to him about the jewels that the ghost had given to Virginia. They were magnificent, especially a certain ruby necklace in an old Venetian setting. It was a really superb example of sixteenth-century work, and the jewels were so valuable that Mr Otis felt he could not allow his daughter to accept them.

"My Lord," he said, "I know that in this country, land and family treasures are passed down from father to son. I feel sure that these jewels belong to your family. I must ask you, therefore, to take them to London with you. You can think of them simply as part of your property, which has been returned to you in rather strange circumstances.

"As for my daughter, she is only a child. Up to now, I am glad to say, she has shown little interest in such luxuries. Besides, Mrs Otis tells me that these jewels are of great value, and that they could be sold for a large sum. For these reasons, Lord Canterville, I think it would be wrong for any member of my family to keep them.

"Perhaps I should add that Virginia is very anxious that you should allow her to keep the box. It is very old, and rather damaged, and she would like to have it as a memory of your unhappy but misguided ancestor."

Lord Canterville listened very seriously to the speech of the worthy Minister. Now and then he pulled his grey moustache to hide a smile. When Mr Otis had finished, he shook him warmly by the hand.

"My dear sir," he said, "your charming little daughter has done my unlucky ancestor, Sir Simon, a very important service. My family and I owe her a great deal for her wonderful courage. The jewels are clearly hers. Upon my word, if I were heartless enough to take them from her, the wicked old man would come out of his grave in two weeks. He would give me a terrible life.

"Besides, they cannot be family treasures. They are not mentioned in any will or legal document, and nobody has ever known about them. I assure you that I have no more claim to them than your butler. And when Miss Virginia grows up, I dare say she will be pleased to have pretty things to wear.

"Besides, Mr Otis, you have forgotten that you bought the furniture and the ghost at the same price. Anything that belonged to the ghost passed at once into your possession. Sir Simon may have been very active in the corridor at night, but in the eyes of the law he was really dead. So you became the owner of his property when you bought the house."

Mr Otis was very unhappy about Lord Canterville's refusal, and he begged him to think again. But the good-natured lord was quite firm. In the end he persuaded the Minister to allow his daughter to keep the present that the ghost had given her.

In the spring of 1890, the young Duchess of Cheshire was presented to the Queen on the occasion of her marriage. Everybody admired her jewels. For Virginia married her young lover as soon as he was old enough. They were both so charming, and they loved each other so much, that everyone was delighted with the match.

Everyone, that is, except the old Marchioness of Dumbleton. She had tried to catch the Duke for one of her seven unmarried daughters, and she had given no fewer than three expensive dinner parties for that purpose. Strangely enough, Mr Otis himself was not very pleased at first. He liked the young Duke very much as a person, but he did not approve of titles. Still, when he walked up the church with his daughter on his arm, there was not a prouder man in the whole of England.

After their honeymoon, the Duke and Duchess went down to Canterville Chase. On the day after their arrival, they walked over in the afternoon to the lonely churchyard by the pine woods. There had been great difficulty at first about the words to be carved on Sir Simon's gravestone. In the end it was decided to carve simply his initials and the lines from the library window.

The Duchess had brought with her some lovely roses, which she placed on the grave. They stood beside it for some time. Then they walked into the ruins of the old church. The Duchess sat down on a fallen pillar, while her husband lay at her feet, smoking a cigarette and looking up into her beautiful eyes.

Suddenly he threw his cigarette away, took her hand and said, "Virginia, a wife should have no secrets from her husband."

"Dear Cecil! I have no secrets from you."

"Yes, you have," he answered with a smile. "You have never told me what happened to you when you were locked up with the ghost."

"I have never told anyone, Cecil," said Virginia seriously.

"I know that, but you could tell me."

"Please don't ask me, Cecil. I cannot tell you. Poor Sir Simon! I owe him a great deal. Yes, don't laugh, Cecil, I really do. He made me see what Life is, and what Death means, and why Love is stronger than both."

The Duke stood up and kissed his wife lovingly.

"You can have your secret as long as I have your heart," he whispered.

"You have always had that, Cecil."

"And you will tell our children some day, won't you?"

Virginia blushed.

The End
