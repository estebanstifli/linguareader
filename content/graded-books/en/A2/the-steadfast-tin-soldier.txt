Chapter 1: Twenty-Five Brothers

There was once a little boy called Thomas. He lived with his parents in a tall old house in the middle of a big city. His bedroom was at the top of the house, and from his window he could see red roofs, church towers and a canal with boats.

On the morning of his seventh birthday, Thomas woke up very early. He ran down the stairs in his pyjamas. On the kitchen table, there was a wooden box with a red ribbon around it.

"Happy birthday, Thomas!" said his mother and father.

Thomas pulled off the ribbon and opened the lid. Inside the box, there were twenty-five tin soldiers. They lay next to each other in a long, straight line. They all had red jackets with white belts, blue trousers and tall black hats. Each one held a gun on his shoulder. They all looked straight ahead with serious faces.

"Tin soldiers!" cried Thomas, and he clapped his hands. "They are wonderful!"

"Your grandfather had tin soldiers too, when he was a boy," said his father. "He played with them every day. He always said that tin soldiers are the bravest soldiers in the world. They never run away."

"Can I play with them now?" asked Thomas.

"First, breakfast," said his mother. She put a plate of warm pancakes with honey on the table. It was his favourite breakfast. But Thomas ate very quickly. He could not take his eyes off the box.

The soldiers were all brothers. A toymaker made them from the same old tin spoon. He melted the spoon in a little pot and poured the hot metal into twenty-five small shapes. But the spoon was not very big. When the toymaker came to the last soldier, there was not enough tin. So the last soldier had only one leg.

Thomas took the soldiers out of the box one by one and stood them on the table. Twenty-four soldiers stood on two legs. The last one stood on one leg.

All day, Thomas played with his new soldiers. He put them in long lines on the floor. He made them march across the carpet and climb over the books. He made them fight against the cushions of the sofa. The soldiers always won. The one-legged soldier was always at the front of the line.

"He is the captain," said Thomas. "He is the bravest of them all."

"Look," said Thomas. "This one is different."

"Oh dear," said his father. "That is a mistake. We can take him back to the shop."

"No!" said Thomas. "I like him. He is special."

And the strange thing was this: the soldier with one leg stood as firmly as his brothers with two. He did not fall over. He did not even move. He stood very straight and very still, and he looked straight ahead.

That night, the soldiers lay in their box in the dark. They talked quietly together. Tin soldiers can talk, but only when people cannot hear them.

"Brother," said the soldier next to him, "does it hurt? Having only one leg, I mean."

"No," said the one-legged soldier. "It does not hurt. I can stand, and I can hold my gun. That is enough for a soldier."

"But you cannot march," said another brother. "And you cannot run."

"A good soldier does not run," he answered calmly. "He stays in his place."

The other soldiers were quiet for a moment. They thought about his words. Then the oldest brother spoke.

"You are right," he said. "We have two legs, but you have the strongest heart of all of us."

The one-legged soldier did not answer. But inside his little tin body, he felt warm and happy.

He was a steadfast tin soldier. Steadfast means that you stay strong and do not change, even when things are difficult. And this little soldier was going to need all his strength. This is his story.

Chapter 2: The Paper Castle

The table in the living room was full of toys. Thomas had a wooden horse, a spinning top, a box of coloured pencils, a little train and a small black box with a lid. But the most beautiful toy on the table was a castle made of paper.

The castle was white, with four towers and little windows. You could look through the windows and see the rooms inside. In front of the castle, there was a small lake made from a piece of mirror. Little trees stood around the lake, and white paper swans swam on the water. Everything was pretty, but the prettiest thing of all was a young lady.

Thomas's aunt made the castle for him last Christmas. She spent many evenings cutting the paper with small scissors. Thomas was not allowed to play with it. "It is too delicate," said his mother. "You can look at it, but please do not touch it." So the castle always stayed in the same place, at the end of the table, like a small white palace in a dream.

She stood in the open door of the castle. She was also made of paper, and she was a dancer. She wore a dress of fine white cotton, and a thin blue ribbon went over her shoulder. In the middle of the ribbon, there was a shiny silver star. It was as big as her whole face.

The little dancer held her arms high above her head. She stood on the point of one foot. Her other leg was lifted high behind her, so high that the tin soldier could not see it.

"She has only one leg, like me," thought the soldier. "She is the perfect wife for me."

Then he had a sad thought. "But she is a lady, and she lives in a castle. I am only a soldier, and I live in a box with twenty-four brothers. That is no place for her. But I would like to meet her."

That evening, Thomas put his soldiers back in their box. But he forgot the one-legged soldier. He stayed on the table behind the black box, and from there he could see the dancer perfectly.

He looked at her all evening. She did not move. She stood on her one foot, with her arms high in the air. She never lost her balance. The soldier thought she was the most wonderful creature in the world.

Next to the black box, there was an old wooden horse. Its paint was old and its tail was short, because Thomas's father played with it when he was a boy. The horse saw how the soldier looked at the dancer.

"Ah," said the horse in a slow, deep voice. "I know that look. You like the little dancer, don't you?"

The soldier did not move. But he said, "Yes. She is very beautiful. Do you know her?"

"Everybody knows her," said the horse. "She came here last Christmas with the castle. She never speaks to anybody. She only dances. Day and night, she stands on her toe."

"She is steadfast," said the soldier. "Like me."

"Maybe," said the horse. "But listen to me, young soldier. Be careful. In that black box lives someone very jealous. He does not like anybody to look at the dancer. He thinks she belongs to him."

Chapter 3: Midnight

Late at night, the family went to bed. The house became dark and quiet. The clock in the corridor began to strike: one, two, three... all the way to twelve.

At midnight, something magical happened. The toys woke up.

First, the nutcracker jumped off the shelf and did a somersault. Then the train started to run around the table, "choo, choo!" The spinning top spun and spun. The pencils came out of their box and drew pictures on the wall. Inside the box, the tin soldiers banged on the lid. They wanted to come out and play, but they could not open it.

The little bird in the cage woke up and began to sing. It was a happy, noisy party.

Only two toys did not move. One was the tin soldier, and the other was the little dancer. She stood on the point of her toe, with her arms high. He stood on his one leg. He did not take his eyes off her for one second.

Then the clock struck twelve again, very loudly. Suddenly, the lid of the little black box flew open. It was not a box of sweets. It was a jack-in-the-box! A little goblin jumped out on a spring. He had a black face, a green hat, long ears and an ugly smile.

The goblin turned and looked at the soldier.

"Tin soldier!" he said in a high, nasty voice. "Keep your eyes to yourself! Do not look at the dancer. She is not for you."

But the soldier pretended not to hear. He looked straight ahead at the dancer, as always.

"Very well," said the goblin angrily. "You do not listen to me. Just wait until tomorrow! Then you will see!"

And with a loud noise, he went back into his box. The lid closed on top of him.

Soon it was morning. The sun came up, and all the toys went back to their places. When Thomas came downstairs, everything was quiet and in order. Nobody knew about the party.

Chapter 4: The Fall

That morning, Thomas took the one-legged soldier and put him on the windowsill. The window was open, because it was a warm day. Thomas wanted to show him the street below.

"Look," said Thomas. "That is the city. Those are the roofs and the canal."

A little sparrow landed on the windowsill next to the soldier. It looked at him with its head on one side. Then it looked at the black box on the table. The sparrow did not like what it saw. "Be careful, soldier," it said quietly. "Somebody in this room does not like you." Then it flew away over the roofs.

Then his mother called him for breakfast, and he ran to the kitchen.

Nobody knows exactly what happened next. Maybe it was the goblin. Maybe it was the wind. But suddenly, the window opened wide, and the tin soldier fell. He fell head first, from the third floor all the way down to the street.

It was a terrible fall. He turned over and over in the air. At last, he landed between two stones in the road. His hat was stuck between the stones, and his one leg was up in the air. His gun was stuck in the ground.

Thomas and the maid ran down the stairs and into the street. They looked for the soldier everywhere. Once, Thomas almost stepped on him, but he did not see him.

The soldier could shout, "Here I am!" But he did not. "A soldier does not shout," he thought. "That is not proper when I am wearing my uniform."

So Thomas and the maid looked and looked. But they could not find him, and in the end they went back inside.

Then it began to rain. At first, there were only a few drops. Then the rain came faster and faster. Soon it was pouring. The water ran along the street like a river. The soldier lay between the stones, cold and wet. But he did not complain.

Many feet walked past him. Some were in big black boots, and some were in small shoes. A horse and cart passed very close. The wheels almost crushed him. Then a big dog came along the street. It put its wet nose down and smelled him.

"Hmm," said the dog. "What is this? It is not a bone. It is not a sausage. It smells of tin and rain."

The dog pushed him with its nose. Then it lost interest and walked away. The soldier stayed between the stones, as straight as ever.

"I am not afraid," he thought. "Thomas will find me. And if he does not find me, I will find my way home."

After the rain stopped, two boys came down the street. They were poor boys with old shoes and dirty faces. They saw something red between the stones.

"Look!" said the first boy. "A tin soldier! He can go for a sail."

Chapter 5: The Paper Boat

The boys took an old newspaper and folded it into a little boat. They put the tin soldier in the middle of it. Then they put the boat into the water by the side of the street. The water was running very fast after the rain.

The boat sailed away. The two boys ran next to it and clapped their hands.

"Go, soldier, go!" they shouted.

"Where is he going?" asked the second boy.

"To the sea!" said the first boy. "He is going to find a ship and sail around the world. He is going to see whales and islands and pirates!"

The boys ran after the boat for a long time. They jumped over the puddles and laughed. A grey cat sat on a wall and watched the little boat with yellow eyes. Then the boys reached the corner of the street, and their mother called them home.

"Goodbye, soldier!" they shouted, and they waved.

What a journey it was! The water ran fast, and there were big waves. The paper boat went up and down, up and down. Sometimes it turned around so quickly that the soldier felt sick. But he stood straight. He did not move a muscle. He looked straight ahead and held his gun on his shoulder.

"I must be brave," he thought. "A soldier is always brave."

Suddenly, the water carried the boat into a dark hole under the street. It was a long tunnel for the rainwater. It was as dark inside as it was in his box at home.

"Where am I going now?" thought the soldier. "I am sure this is the goblin's fault. I want the little dancer here in the boat with me. With her next to me, the dark is not important."

At that moment, a big water rat came out of a hole in the wall. It lived in the tunnel. It had grey fur, a long tail and very sharp teeth.

"Stop!" said the rat. "Do you have a passport? Show me your passport!"

But the soldier did not answer. He only held his gun more tightly.

The boat sailed on, and the rat swam after it. The rat was very angry. It shouted to the bits of wood and straw in the water.

"Stop him! Stop him! He did not pay the toll! He did not show his passport!"

But the water was running faster and faster. The rat could not catch the boat.

Chapter 6: Into the Canal

Soon the soldier could see a light at the end of the tunnel. But he also heard a loud noise. It was a roaring sound, like thunder. It was frightening enough to scare the bravest man.

At the end of the tunnel, the water fell down into a big canal. For the little soldier, it was as dangerous as a huge waterfall is for us.

Before that, the tunnel became wider and the water became calmer for a moment. The soldier could see light on the walls. He could see old bottles, leaves and pieces of wood around his boat. A small spider sat on a piece of wood next to him.

"Excuse me," said the spider politely. "Do you know where we are going?"

"No," said the soldier. "But I think it is somewhere far from home."

"Then good luck to both of us," said the spider. It jumped onto the wall and ran up into the dark. The soldier stayed in his boat. A soldier does not leave his place.

He was very close to the end now. He could not stop. The boat went out of the tunnel. The poor soldier held himself as straight as possible. Nobody could say that he even blinked.

The boat turned around three or four times. It filled with water up to the top. It began to sink. The soldier stood in the water up to his neck. The paper became softer and softer. Then the water came over his head.

He thought of the pretty little dancer. He thought he was never going to see her again. In his head, he heard the words of an old song:

"Go forward, brave soldier, go! Your end is near, and the waters flow."

Then the paper broke into pieces. The soldier fell through and sank down into the deep, green water.

But at that exact moment, a big fish swam by. It opened its mouth and swallowed the tin soldier in one bite.

Oh, how dark it was inside the fish! It was even darker than the tunnel, and there was very little room. But the soldier stayed steadfast. He lay as straight as he could, with his gun on his shoulder. He thought about his brothers in the box. He thought about the wooden horse and the paper castle. Most of all, he thought about the little dancer on her one foot.

"She is still standing at the door of her castle," he thought. "She is waiting. I must stay strong for her."

The fish swam around and around. It made strange movements. Then, after a long time, it was quite still. Something like a flash of lightning went through the fish. Then there was daylight, and a voice said:

"Look! The tin soldier!"

Chapter 7: Home Again

What happened? The fisherman caught the fish in his net that morning. He took it to the market and sold it. The market was full of noise and colours. There were baskets of apples, cheeses as big as wheels and boxes of fresh fish on ice. People shouted prices and pushed each other. The fisherman put the big fish on his table and waited. A woman bought the fish and took it home to her kitchen. Then the cook cut it open with a big knife. She was a big, cheerful woman with red cheeks, and she always sang while she worked. When the knife went into the fish, it hit something hard.

"What is this?" she said. "A stone? A coin? Is it my lucky day?"

She looked inside the fish. Something red and blue was shining there. She could not believe her eyes. It was a small man with a tall black hat and one leg.

And here was the strange thing. The kitchen was the kitchen of Thomas's house! The cook was the family's cook. She took the soldier out of the fish with two fingers. She washed him in the sink and dried him with a towel. Then she carried him to the living room.

"Look what I found inside a fish!" she said.

Everybody wanted to see this wonderful little man. He travelled through the city inside a fish! Thomas was delighted to have his soldier back. He put him on the table again.

But the tin soldier did not feel proud. He looked around the room. It was the same room. There were the same toys, the same windows and the same castle. And there, in the door of the castle, was the little dancer. She still stood on one foot, with her other leg high in the air. She was steadfast too.

The soldier was so happy that he almost cried tin tears. But a soldier does not cry. So he only looked at her, and she looked at him. They did not say anything. They did not need to say anything.

That night, at midnight, the toys had a party for him. The train ran around the table with its whistle. The nutcracker opened and closed his mouth. The little bird sang a special song. Inside the box, the twenty-four brothers banged on the lid with their guns.

"Welcome home, brother!" they shouted. "Tell us about your journey!"

So the tin soldier told them everything. He told them about the fall from the window and the dog in the street. He told them about the paper boat and the angry rat in the tunnel. He told them about the waterfall and the dark inside the fish. The toys listened with open mouths.

"You are a real hero," said the wooden horse.

And the little dancer? She did not speak. But the soldier thought that her paper smile was a little bigger than before.

Only the goblin in the black box was not happy. He did not come out of his box that night. But the soldier could hear him inside it. He was laughing a strange, nasty laugh.

That afternoon, the family sat in the living room. It was a cold day, so there was a big fire in the stove. Thomas was playing on the floor with his toys.

Suddenly, he picked up the tin soldier. He looked at him for a moment. Then, without any reason, he threw the soldier into the fire.

"Thomas!" cried his mother. "Why did you do that?"

But Thomas did not know why. He did not understand it himself. Perhaps the goblin in the black box made him do it.

Chapter 8: The Tin Heart

The tin soldier stood in the middle of the fire. All around him were bright flames. He felt a terrible heat, but he did not know if it came from the fire or from his love. The bright colours of his uniform were gone. Maybe the water took them away on his long journey. Maybe his sadness took them.

He looked at the little dancer, and she looked at him. He felt that he was slowly melting. But he still stood steadfast, with his gun on his shoulder.

Then somebody opened the door of the room. A cold wind came in. It caught the little paper dancer. She flew like a butterfly across the room, straight into the stove next to the tin soldier.

For one short moment, they stood together in the bright fire. For the first time, they were really close. The soldier could see her face clearly. He could see her little painted eyes and her silver star. And he was sure, completely sure, that she was looking at him.

"You came," he thought.

She did not say anything. She was made of paper, and paper cannot speak. But she did not need to say anything. She came to him, and that was enough.

There was a bright flash, and in a moment she was gone.

The soldier melted away into a small lump of tin.

The next morning, the maid cleaned the stove and took out the ashes. In the ashes, she found the tin soldier. But he was not a soldier anymore. He was a little tin heart.

Next to the heart lay the dancer's silver star. It was black from the fire, but it was still there.

The maid held the little heart and the black star in her hand. She did not throw them away. She did not know why, but she felt they were special.

"Somebody loved somebody here," she said quietly.

She gave them to Thomas. He looked at them for a long time. Then he put the tin heart and the silver star together in a small box with a red ribbon. He kept the box by his bed for the rest of his life.

When Thomas was an old man, he told this story to his grandchildren. They sat around him by the fire on winter evenings. He took the small box from the shelf and opened it slowly. The children looked at the little tin heart and the black star. Then they asked the same questions every time.

"Grandfather, is the story true?"

"Every word," said Thomas.

"Why did you throw him into the fire?"

"I do not know," said Thomas sadly. "I was only a boy. I think about it every day."

 He always finished it in the same way.

"The soldier had only one leg," he said. "He never ran, he never shouted and he never complained. He was just steadfast. And in the end, nobody could take his heart away."

The End
