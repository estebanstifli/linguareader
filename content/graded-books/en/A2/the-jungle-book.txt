Chapter 1: The Man-Cub

It was a hot evening in the hills of India. Father Wolf woke up in his cave. He stretched his legs and yawned, and Mother Wolf lay next to him with their four little cubs. 

"It is time to hunt," said Father Wolf.

Just then, a small shadow came to the mouth of the cave. It was Tabaqui the jackal. Nobody in the jungle liked Tabaqui, because he ate old bones and told stories and made trouble.

"Good luck, Chief of the Wolves," said Tabaqui. "And good luck to your beautiful children. May they have strong white teeth."

"There is no food here," said Father Wolf coldly.

"Shere Khan, the Big One, is coming to hunt in these hills," he said.

Shere Khan was a tiger. He lived near the Waingunga River, twenty miles away, and he had one bad leg, so his mother called him Lungri, the Lame One.

"He cannot hunt here!" said Father Wolf angrily. "The Law of the Jungle says he must tell us first. He will frighten all the animals away. Then what will we eat?"

"I am only telling you," said Tabaqui. "I can hear him now, down in the trees." And he ran away.

Father Wolf listened. From the valley below came a long, angry cry. It was the voice of a tiger who caught nothing.

"He is not hunting deer tonight," said Father Wolf. "He is hunting Man."

Mother Wolf did not like this. The Law of the Jungle says that animals must not kill Man. If they do, men come with guns and fire, and everybody in the jungle suffers.

Suddenly they heard a terrible noise. The tiger roared, and then he cried out in pain.

"He missed," said Father Wolf. "He jumped into a woodcutter's fire and burned his feet. Silly tiger."

Then something moved in the bushes near the cave. Father Wolf got ready to jump. But he stopped in the air.

"Man!" he said. "Look! A man's cub!"

In front of him stood a little brown baby. He had no clothes and no hair on his body, and he was just old enough to walk. He looked up at Father Wolf and laughed.

"Is that a man's cub?" said Mother Wolf. "I never saw one. Bring it here."

Father Wolf picked up the baby very gently in his mouth. His teeth did not even scratch the skin. He put the baby down among the cubs. The baby pushed the cubs out of the way and got close to the warm fur of Mother Wolf.

"How little he is! How brave he is!" said Mother Wolf softly. "He is not afraid at all."

Then the moonlight at the mouth of the cave went dark. Shere Khan pushed his great square head and shoulders into the cave.

"A man's cub came this way," he said. "Its parents ran away. Give it to me."

The cave was too small for the tiger, so he could not come in.

"The wolves are a free people," said Father Wolf. "We take orders from the Head of the Pack, not from a striped cattle-killer."

Shere Khan roared, and the cave filled with the noise. "I am Shere Khan! The cub is mine! Give it to me!"

Mother Wolf jumped up. Her eyes were like two green moons in the dark.

"And I am Raksha, the Demon!" she said. "The man's cub is mine, Lungri! He will run with the Pack and hunt with the Pack. One day he will hunt you! Now go away, or I will send you home lamer than before!"

Shere Khan knew that he could not win against an angry mother wolf in her own cave. He moved back, slowly.

"We will see what the Pack says," he growled. "The cub is mine, and in the end it will come to my teeth, you thieves!" Then he went away into the night.

Mother Wolf lay down again with the baby and her cubs.

"I will keep him," she said. "I will call him Mowgli, the Frog, because he has no fur and he jumps and kicks like a little frog."

Chapter 2: The Council Rock

The Law of the Jungle says that the Pack must see every new cub. So, when the cubs could walk well, Father Wolf took them to the Council Rock. 

The Council Rock was a high hill covered with big stones. Forty wolves sat there in the moonlight. On the highest rock lay Akela, the great Lone Wolf. He was the leader of the Pack. He was wise and strong.

"Look well, O Wolves!" called Akela. "You know the Law. Look well at the new cubs!"

The wolves came and looked at the cubs, and Mother Wolf watched them with angry eyes. Father Wolf pushed Mowgli into the middle of the circle. Mowgli sat down and played with some little stones in the moonlight.

Then a deep voice came from behind the rocks. It was Shere Khan.

"The cub is mine! Give him to me! What do the Free People want with a man's cub?"

Some young wolves said, "Yes! What do we want with a man's cub?"

Akela did not move his head. "Look well, O Wolves," he said again. "What does the Free People want with the orders of a tiger?"

But the Law says that two members of the Pack must speak for a new cub. If nobody speaks, the cub cannot stay.

"Who speaks for this cub?" asked Akela.

Nobody answered, and Mother Wolf got ready to fight.

Then a big brown bear stood up. His name was Baloo. He was sleepy and old, and he taught the Law of the Jungle to the young wolves.

"The man's cub?" said Baloo slowly. "I speak for the man's cub. There is no harm in him. Let him run with the Pack. I will teach him the Law."

"We need one more," said Akela. "Who speaks besides Baloo?"

A black shadow jumped down into the circle. It was Bagheera, the Black Panther. His fur was black as ink, and his voice was soft as honey. He was clever like a jackal, brave like a buffalo, and dangerous like an elephant in a bad mood. Nobody wanted to make him angry.

"O Akela, and you, the Free People," said Bagheera. "I have no right to speak here. But the Law says that you can buy the life of a cub. Is that true?"

"Yes, yes!" said the young wolves, who were always hungry. "Let him speak!"

"I will add something to Baloo's words. I know a fat bull. He is lying dead half a mile from here. He was killed only this evening. He is yours if you accept the man's cub. Is that fair?"

The wolves were very hungry. "He will die in the winter rains," they said. "What harm can a naked frog do to us? Let him stay with the Pack. Where is the bull, Bagheera?"

Then they all ran down the hill to eat the bull. Only Akela, Bagheera, Baloo and Mowgli's family stayed at the Council Rock.

Shere Khan roared all night, because he was very angry.

"Roar, roar," said Bagheera into his whiskers. "One day this naked thing will make you roar a different song, or I know nothing about Man."

"Men and their cubs are very wise," said Akela. "He can be a help to us one day. Take him away, Father Wolf, and teach him well."

And so Mowgli became a member of the Seeonee Wolf Pack. He paid with a bull and with Baloo's good word.

Chapter 3: Baloo's Lessons

Ten years went by. Mowgli grew up with the wolves. Father Wolf taught him the meaning of every sound in the grass and the song of every bird in the trees.

Bagheera showed him how to climb trees and how to hunt.

But the most important teacher was Baloo. Every day the old bear taught Mowgli the Law of the Jungle.

"The Law is like a big tree," said Baloo. "It is old, and it is strong, and it is over all of us. Learn it well, Little Brother."

Mowgli learnt how to say "Good hunting" to the other animals. And he learnt the Master Words of the Jungle.

"With the Master Words, the jungle will protect you," said Baloo. "Nobody will hurt you. Not the snakes, not the birds, and not the hunting animals."

Mowgli was a clever boy, but sometimes he did not want to listen. Then Baloo hit him gently with his big paw. The bear was strong, so a gentle hit felt quite hard.

One day Bagheera came to watch the lesson.

"Why do you hit him?" said Bagheera. "He is so small. How can his little head remember all your long talk?"

"Better a hit from me than death from his own mistakes," said Baloo. "Now I am teaching him the Master Words. Little Brother, say the words for the Hunting People."

Mowgli sat up. "We be of one blood, ye and I," he said. He spoke like a bear.

"Good. Now the words for the birds."

Mowgli whistled like the kite, Chil.

"Now the words for the Snake People."

Mowgli made a long, perfect hiss. Then he jumped on Bagheera's back with joy.

"See?" said Baloo proudly. "Now he is safe in the whole jungle."

"Except from his own tribe," said Mowgli, and he laughed. "And soon I will have my own tribe. I will lead them through the branches all day long."

Baloo stopped. "What is this new nonsense, little dreamer?"

"Yes," said Mowgli. "And we will throw branches and dirt at old Baloo! They promised me."

"Who promised you?" asked Bagheera quietly.

"The grey apes," said Mowgli. "Baloo hit me, and I was sad. So I walked away, and the grey apes came down from the trees. They said I was their brother. They gave me nuts and said I was going to be their leader one day."

"Mowgli," said Baloo. His voice was like thunder on a hot night. "The Monkey People have no law. They have no leader and no memory. They talk and talk, but they do nothing. We of the jungle do not speak to them. We do not drink where the monkeys drink. We do not even think of them."

Mowgli felt very ashamed. Then the three friends lay down for an afternoon sleep. But high in the trees, the grey apes were watching them.

Chapter 4: The Monkey People

The Monkey People were called the Bandar-log. They always wanted the other animals to notice them. They thought a man's cub was a very interesting thing. He could weave sticks together, and the monkeys wanted to learn this. "Then we will be the wisest people in the jungle," they said.

So they waited. When Baloo and Bagheera were asleep, they came down quietly from the trees.

Mowgli woke up because strong little hands held his arms and legs. Branches hit his face. The monkeys were carrying him up into the trees! He looked down and saw Baloo. The bear woke the whole jungle with his deep cry. Bagheera jumped up a tree, showing all his teeth. But the branches were too thin for a heavy panther, and he fell down again.

"They noticed us! Bagheera noticed us!" shouted the monkeys happily.

Then began the flight of the Bandar-log through the treetops. It was a terrible journey. Two strong monkeys held Mowgli under the arms and swung him from tree to tree, twenty feet at a time. Sometimes Mowgli saw the ground far below him, and he felt sick and afraid.

"I must send a message to Baloo and Bagheera," he thought. "At this speed, they cannot follow me."

He looked up at the sky. High above him flew Chil the kite. He saw the monkeys and came down a little to look.

Mowgli gave the Master Word for the birds. "We be of one blood, ye and I!"

Chil was surprised. "In whose name, brother?" he called.

"Mowgli, the Frog. Man-cub, they call me! Mark my trail! Tell Baloo of the Seeonee Pack and Bagheera of the Council Rock!"

Then the monkeys swung Mowgli away into the trees again. But Chil flew higher and followed the moving branches with his sharp eyes.

On the ground, Baloo and Bagheera were angry and afraid.

"We can never catch them," said Bagheera. "They are too fast. But who do the monkeys fear?"

Baloo thought hard. Then he said, "I remember. The monkeys fear Kaa, the Rock Snake. He can climb as well as they can, and he steals young monkeys at night. The whisper of his name makes their tails go cold."

"He is always hungry," said Baloo. "Promise him many goats."

They went together to find Kaa. The great python was lying on a warm rock in the afternoon sun. He was thirty feet long, and he was very proud of his beautiful new skin.

"Good hunting!" said Baloo politely.

"Good hunting for us all," said Kaa. "Is there any food near here? I am as empty as a dry well."

"We are hunting," said Bagheera. "The Bandar-log took our man-cub."

"The Bandar-log!" said Kaa. Something moved in his long, cold body. "They are foolish. They call me names. They call me a footless yellow earthworm."

"Yes, they said that," said Bagheera quickly. "We heard them."

Just then a voice came from the sky. It was Chil.

"Baloo! I saw the man-cub! The Bandar-log took him to the monkey city, to the Cold Lairs!"

"Thank you, Chil!" shouted Baloo. "I will keep the best bits of my next kill for you!"

The Cold Lairs was an old, empty city of stone, deep in the jungle. Kings lived there long ago, but now only the monkeys lived there.

"It is half a night's journey," said Baloo.

"Then let us go," said Kaa. And the three hunters went quickly through the jungle.

Chapter 5: The Cold Lairs

The monkeys arrived at the Cold Lairs in the afternoon. They danced and sang foolish songs, but Mowgli was tired and hungry.

"Bring me food," he said, "or let me hunt here."

Twenty or thirty monkeys jumped away to get him nuts and fruit. But on the way they started to fight, and they forgot all about the food. Mowgli walked through the empty city. He saw the old palace with no roof, the dry fountains and a summer-house of white stone with snakes under it.

"Baloo was right," he thought. "They have no law and no hunting call. They have no leaders. They only have foolish words and little thieving hands."

The monkeys came back and made him sit down. "Teach us to weave sticks," they said, and then they forgot about it at once.

When the moon came up, a black shadow moved over the city wall. It was Bagheera. He started to hit the monkeys to the left and right, but a hundred monkeys jumped on him and bit him and pulled him. He fell down under the grey bodies.

Five or six monkeys pushed Mowgli over the wall of the summer-house. He fell into it. Under the floor were many cobras. Mowgli gave the Snake People's Word at once. "We be of one blood, ye and I!"

"Stay still, Little Brother," hissed the cobras. "Do not put your feet on us."

From the summer-house, Mowgli called to Bagheera. "Roll to the water tanks, Bagheera! Roll and jump! Get to the water!"

Bagheera fought his way slowly to the old water tanks. The monkeys could not follow him there, because they hate water.

Then old Baloo arrived. He sat up on his back legs, opened his big arms, and the monkeys came at him. Baloo held them and hit them, "bat-bat-bat," like the sound of a paddle in the water.

But the monkeys were too many, and Baloo was getting weak. "Kaa!" shouted Bagheera from the water. "Kaa, help us!"

Then Kaa came over the wall. He hit the monkeys with his head, like a hammer. The monkeys screamed and ran in every direction. "It is Kaa! Run! Run!"

They climbed the walls and the old towers and sat there, silent and afraid. Kaa broke the wall of the summer-house with his great head. Mowgli jumped out and ran to Baloo and Bagheera.

"Are you hurt?" said Baloo. He held the boy in his big arms.

"I am sore, and hungry, and bruised a little," said Mowgli. "But, oh, they hurt you badly, my brothers! You are bleeding."

"It is nothing," said Bagheera. He licked his lips. "But here is Kaa. We owe him the fight, and you owe him your life. Thank him, Mowgli."

Mowgli turned and saw the great python's head above his own.

"We be of one blood, thou and I," said Mowgli. "I take my life from you tonight. My kill will be your kill, if you are ever hungry, O Kaa."

"Thank you, Little Brother," said Kaa. "I will remember."

Then Kaa went into the middle of the old city. In the moonlight he began the Hunger Dance of Kaa. He moved his long body in circles and loops, slowly, slowly, while he sang a soft song. The monkeys watched him and could not move. Then they started to walk towards him, one by one.

Baloo and Bagheera felt the power of the dance too. "Keep your hand on my shoulder," said Mowgli. "Or you must go back, back to Kaa!" He pulled them away.

"Let us go," said Bagheera. "Quick."

In the morning, when they were home, Bagheera spoke.

"By the Law of the Jungle, you are to blame, and you must be punished."

"It is just," said Mowgli. "I am sorry."

Bagheera gave him six soft pats with his paw. They were not soft for a boy of seven, but Mowgli did not cry. That is the Law of the Jungle: when a punishment is finished, it is forgotten. Mowgli climbed on Bagheera's back and slept all the way home.

Chapter 6: The Red Flower

The years went by. Akela grew old and weak. And Shere Khan began to make friends with the young wolves of the Pack. He gave them the bones of his kills, and he talked to them.

"Why do you good hunters follow a dying wolf and a man's cub?" he said. "They tell me that you cannot look him in the eyes at the Council."

Bagheera heard about this. "Little Brother," he said one day, "Akela is very old. Soon he will miss his kill, and then he will not be the leader anymore. The young wolves listen to Shere Khan. Soon you will be a man."

"I was born in the jungle," said Mowgli. "I obey the Law. I took thorns from the feet of many wolves. They are my brothers!"

"Feel under my jaw," said Bagheera. Mowgli put his hand under the panther's chin. He felt a little bald spot.

"I was born among men, in the King's cages at Oodeypore. I wore a collar there. One night I broke the lock and ran away. Because I knew the ways of men, I became more terrible than Shere Khan. Little Brother, you must go back to men one day, as I came back to the jungle."

"Look at me," said Bagheera. Mowgli looked into his eyes. After half a minute, the big panther turned his head away.

"That is why," said Bagheera. "Not even I can look in your eyes. They hate you because their eyes cannot meet yours. Because you are a man."

"What must I do?" asked Mowgli.

"Go down quickly to the village," said Bagheera. "Take some of the Red Flower that they grow there. It is fire. Every animal in the jungle fears it. Keep it ready for the night of the Council."

So that night, Mowgli ran down to the village. In the morning, a boy carried a pot of hot coals out of a house. Mowgli took the pot from the boy and ran away with it. All day he fed the fire in the pot with sticks and dry grass.

That evening Akela missed his kill. The young wolves called a Council at the Rock. Shere Khan came too and lay there, proud, while the young wolves listened to him.

"The man-cub stayed too long among us," said Shere Khan. "Give him to me."

"He is our brother," said Akela. "He ate our food and slept with us."

"He is a man!" howled the young wolves.

Mowgli stood up. In his hands he held the fire pot. "Listen, you dogs!" he shouted. "You told me many times that I am a man, and now I feel that you are right. So I will not call you my brothers anymore. I will call you dogs, as a man does."

He threw the fire on the dry grass. A red flame jumped up. The wolves moved back in terror.

Mowgli put a dead branch in the fire. Then he walked to Shere Khan and took the tiger by the fur under his chin. "Up, dog!" he said. He hit Shere Khan on the head with the burning branch. The tiger cried with fear, and his fur began to smoke.

"When I come to the Council Rock again," said Mowgli, "I will come with Shere Khan's skin on my head. Akela will live as he wants. Now go!"

He waved the fire, and the wolves ran away. Only Akela, Bagheera and about ten wolves stayed with him.

Then something began to hurt inside Mowgli. He started to cry. "What is it, Bagheera? Am I dying?"

"No, Little Brother," said Bagheera. "Those are only tears, such as men use. Now I know you are a man, and not a man's cub. Let them fall, Mowgli."

In the morning he said goodbye to Mother Wolf and his four brothers. "Do not forget me," he said.

"Come back soon," said Mother Wolf. "I loved you more than my own cubs, little frog."

Then Mowgli went down the hill alone, to meet those mysterious things that are called men.

Chapter 7: The Village

Mowgli walked for twenty miles. At last he came to a village at the edge of the jungle. Little boys looked after the buffaloes in the fields, and when they saw Mowgli, they shouted and ran away.

The people of the village came out and looked at the marks on his arms and legs from the wolves' teeth.

"He is a wolf-child from the jungle," they said.

A tall woman came forward. Her name was Messua. She was the wife of the richest man in the village, and she looked at Mowgli for a long time.

"Many years ago, a tiger took my little son into the jungle," she said quietly. "This boy looks like him. He has the same eyes."

The priest said, "The jungle took your son, and the jungle gave him back. Take the boy into your house, sister."

So Messua took Mowgli home. She gave him milk and bread and called him Nathoo, the name of her lost son. Mowgli did not understand her language, but he liked her, and soon he could say many words.

But the house felt like a trap, and he could not sleep under a roof. That night he went out and lay in the long grass at the edge of the field.

A grey nose touched him. It was Grey Brother, the oldest of Mother Wolf's cubs.

"Brother," said Grey Brother. "Shere Khan went far away to grow his fur again. It is badly burnt. But he says that when he comes back, he will kill you."

"Bring me news always, Grey Brother," said Mowgli. "I will never forget our cave."

For three months Mowgli lived in the village. He learnt to wear a cloth around his body, and he learnt about money and ploughing. The village boys laughed at him, but he remembered the Law of the Jungle and did not hurt them.

In the evenings, the men of the village sat under a big tree and told stories. The best storyteller was Buldeo, the hunter. He had an old gun and told wonderful stories about the jungle, but all his stories were wrong.

"The tiger that took Messua's son was a ghost tiger," said Buldeo one evening. "He has a lame leg, because a moneylender lived inside him. That moneylender limped too."

"That tiger is lame because he was born lame," said Mowgli. "Your stories are all lies."

Buldeo was angry. "If the boy is so clever," said Buldeo, "he can go and watch the buffaloes."

So the head of the village gave Mowgli a job. Every morning he took the village buffaloes out to the plains to eat grass. He sat on the back of Rama, the great herd bull. The buffaloes lay in the mud pools all day, and Mowgli sat in the shade and slept.

Every day Grey Brother came to a big tree near the river and said, "No news yet."

Then one morning Grey Brother was waiting for him with his fur standing up.

"Shere Khan came back," he said. "He is going to wait for you tonight at the village gate. Now he is sleeping in the big dry ravine of the Waingunga. He ate a big meal this morning and drank a lot of water."

Mowgli smiled a slow smile. "Then he is heavy and sleepy," he said. "And he is a fool. Grey Brother, I have a plan."

Chapter 8: Tiger! Tiger!

"Is Akela with you?" asked Mowgli.

"I am here," said a deep voice from the long grass. It was Akela, the old Lone Wolf.

"Good," said Mowgli. "Akela, cut the herd in two. Put the cows and the calves on one side. Put the bulls and the strong buffaloes on the other side. Can you do it?"

"I did it many times when I was young," said Akela.

The two wolves ran in and out among the herd. Soon there were two groups. The cows stood with their calves in the middle. The bulls stood together and stamped their feet.

"Grey Brother," said Mowgli. "Take the cows to the bottom of the ravine. Keep them there. Akela and I will take the bulls round to the top."

The ravine was long and narrow, and its sides were very steep. The tiger lay asleep somewhere in the middle of it.

Mowgli rode on Rama's back. He and Akela took the bulls in a big circle, far away from the ravine. At last they came to the top end of the ravine. Below them, far away, Mowgli could see the cows and Grey Brother.

Then Mowgli put his hands to his mouth and shouted down into the ravine. His voice went from rock to rock.

After a long time, a sleepy, angry roar came back. Shere Khan was awake.

"Who calls?" said the tiger.

"I, Mowgli! Cattle-thief, it is time to come to the Council Rock! Down! Hurry them down, Akela! Down, Rama, down!"

The bulls started to run down the ravine. Rama went first, and the others followed. Akela howled behind them. The noise was like thunder.

Shere Khan heard the thunder of their feet. He jumped up and ran down the ravine. But the sides were too steep, and he was too heavy with food and water.

At the bottom of the ravine he saw the cows with their calves. They lowered their horns. The tiger could not go forward. He could not go back. The bulls were coming behind him.

Rama did not stop. He felt something soft under his feet, and he ran on. The other bulls followed him. When everything was quiet again, Shere Khan was dead.

"It was a dog's death," said Mowgli. He took out the knife that he always carried now that he lived with men. "But his skin will look fine on the Council Rock. We must get to work quickly."

Mowgli worked for an hour to take off the great striped skin. The wolves helped him. Suddenly a hand touched his shoulder. It was Buldeo with his old gun. The children of the village told him about the running buffaloes.

"What is this?" said Buldeo. "You killed the Lame Tiger? There is a reward of one hundred rupees for his skin. I will take it to the government. Perhaps I will give you one rupee."

"I need the skin for myself," said Mowgli. "Go away, old man."

"Akela, this man is making me tired," said Mowgli.

Suddenly Buldeo was lying on his back in the grass. A big grey wolf stood over him. Mowgli went on working as if he was alone.

"Great King," said Buldeo in a small voice. "I did not know that you were a sorcerer. I am an old man. Please let me go."

"Go, and peace be with you," said Mowgli. "But next time, do not touch my kill. Let him go, Akela."

Buldeo ran back to the village and told everybody a terrible story about magic and sorcery.

When Mowgli came back to the village in the evening with the buffaloes, the people were waiting at the gate. They threw stones at him.

"Sorcerer! Jungle demon!" they shouted. "Go away, or the priest will turn you into a wolf again! Shoot him, Buldeo!"

"Again?" said Mowgli. "Last time they said I was a man. Now they say I am a wolf. Let us go, Akela."

Only Messua ran to him. "Oh, my son," she cried. "They say you are a sorcerer. I do not believe it. But go away, or they will kill you."

"Goodbye, Messua," said Mowgli. "Run back now. And remember: the tiger that took your son is dead."

He sent the buffaloes into the village. Then he walked away into the jungle with Akela and Grey Brother.

That night they climbed up to the Council Rock. Mother Wolf came out of the cave to meet them. Mowgli spread the great striped skin over the flat stone where Akela once sat.

"Look well, O Wolves!" he called. "I kept my word!"

The old Pack came to the Council Rock. Many of the wolves were lame and thin now, because they had no good leader.

"Lead us again, O Akela," they said. "Lead us again, O man-cub. We are tired of having no law."

"No," said Bagheera softly. "You wanted to be free. Now you are free. Eat it, O Wolves."

"Men threw me out, and the Pack threw me out," said Mowgli. "Now I will hunt alone in the jungle."

"And we will hunt with you," said his four wolf brothers.

So Mowgli went away and hunted with the four cubs in the jungle from that day on. Years later, when he was a man, he married. But that is a story for older people.

The End
