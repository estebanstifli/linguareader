Chapter 1: An Emperor Who Loved Clothes

Many years ago, there was an emperor in a big, rich city. He was not a bad man. But he was not a very good man either. He loved one thing more than anything in the world. That thing was new clothes.

Every morning, the Emperor got up and put on a new coat. Every afternoon he put on another new coat. In the evening, he changed his clothes again. He had a different coat for every hour of the day.

"Which coat is the best?" he asked his servants every morning.

"The blue one, Your Majesty," said one servant.

"The red one, Your Majesty," said another servant.

"No, no," said the Emperor. "The gold one. The gold one is the best today."

He walked up and down in front of his big mirror. He turned to the left and he turned to the right. He smiled at himself for a long time.

The Emperor did not care about his soldiers. He did not care about the theatre or about long rides in the forest. He only went to these places because he wanted to show his new clothes to the people.

In other countries, people asked, "Where is the king?" And the answer was, "The king is in his meeting room." But in this city, people asked, "Where is the Emperor?" And the answer was always the same. "The Emperor is in his dressing room."

The dressing room was very large, bigger than the kitchen and bigger than the dining hall. There were hundreds of coats in it. There were shirts of silk and trousers of velvet. There were hats with long feathers and shoes with silver buttons. There were cloaks of every colour: green, purple, orange, and white.

Two old servants worked in the dressing room all day. Their names were Otto and Bruno. Otto was tall and thin. Bruno was short and round. They brushed the coats, they folded the shirts. They cleaned the shoes.

"I am so tired," said Bruno one evening. He sat down on a little chair. "Today the Emperor changed his clothes eleven times."

"Eleven times?" said Otto. "Yesterday it was only nine."

"Tomorrow it will be twelve," said Bruno. "You will see."

Otto laughed quietly. "Don't let the Emperor hear you," he said.

The city was a happy and busy place, with markets in the squares and boats on the river. Every day, many visitors came to the city. Merchants came with spices and carpets. Musicians came with drums and flutes. Travellers came from far away to see the famous city and its famous Emperor.

The people of the city talked about the Emperor all the time.

"Did you see his new coat yesterday?" asked a woman at the market.

"Yes, I did," said her friend, "and it was yellow, like the sun."

"I saw it too," said a baker, "but I liked his green coat better. The green one was very elegant."

The Emperor knew about these conversations. He loved them, because he wanted everyone to talk about his clothes. He wanted every man, woman. Child in the city to say, "Our Emperor is the best-dressed man in the world."

But the Emperor had a problem: he was bored with his clothes. He had silk, velvet, gold, and silver. He had every colour and every style.

"I want something new," he said to Otto and Bruno one night. "I want something different and special, something no other emperor has."

"Yes, Your Majesty," said Otto.

"Of course, Your Majesty," said Bruno.

But the two servants did not know what to do, because there was nothing new in the whole city. The Emperor had everything already.

That night, the Emperor went to bed, but he did not sleep well. He dreamed about clothes, about a coat of moonlight and a shirt of stars.

"Something new and special," he said in his sleep.

He did not know it. But his wish was going to come true very soon.

Chapter 2: Two Strangers at the Inn

One windy afternoon, two strangers came into the city. They walked through the big gate with old bags on their backs. Their boots were dirty and their coats were full of holes.

The first stranger was a tall man with a long black beard. His name was Max. The second stranger was small and quick, with bright little eyes. His name was Felix.

They were not good men. They were thieves and liars. They traveled from city to city and played clever tricks on people. Then they took the money and ran away.

"Look at this place," said Felix. He looked at the fine houses and the rich shops. "Look at all this gold!"

"Yes," said Max, with a slow smile. "There is a lot of money here. We only need a good plan."

They went into an inn near the market square. The inn was warm and noisy. People sat at long tables and ate soup and bread and drank beer. Max and Felix sat down in a corner and listened.

At the next table, two merchants were talking.

"Did you see the Emperor this morning?" asked the first merchant.

"Yes," said the second. "He wore a coat with a hundred pearls on it. This afternoon he wore a different coat with a hundred diamonds."

"He loves his clothes more than his own people," said the first merchant with a laugh. "Everyone knows that."

Max looked at Felix. Felix looked at Max, and their eyes were shining.

"Did you hear that?" whispered Felix.

"I heard it," whispered Max. "And I have an idea, a very good idea."

The innkeeper came to their table, a big woman with a white apron. "What can I bring you?" she asked.

"Your best dinner, please, and your best wine," said Max in a loud voice. "We are very great men."

The innkeeper looked at their dirty boots and their old coats. "Important people?" she said. "What do you do?"

Max stood up and spoke so loudly that everyone in the inn turned to listen.

"We are weavers," he said. "We are the best weavers in the world. We make the most beautiful cloth in the world."

"Many people say that," said the innkeeper.

"But our cloth is different," said Felix, "because our cloth is magic."

The room was suddenly very quiet, and everyone stopped eating.

"Magic?" asked a young man at the next table. "How is it magic?"

Max smiled and spoke slowly, so everyone could understand.

"Our cloth has fine colours and fine patterns," he said. "But it has a secret. Stupid people cannot see it. People who are bad at their jobs cannot see it either. For these people, the cloth is invisible. They see nothing at all."

"Nothing at all?" said the innkeeper.

"Nothing," said Felix. "Only wise and clever people can see our cloth."

The people in the inn began to talk all at once.

"What a great thing!" said one man.

"Is it true?" asked a woman.

"Of course it is true," said Max. "We made a cloak for the King of the North last year. With his cloak, he found three stupid ministers in his palace. He sent them all away."

This was a lie. Max and Felix never met the King of the North. But the people did not know that.

The innkeeper brought them the best dinner and the best wine. She did not ask for money.

"Perhaps you can make a dress for me one day," she said with a smile.

"Perhaps," said Max, and he ate his chicken happily.

That evening, the story went from the inn to the market. From the market it went to the shops and the houses. By midnight, it was in the palace kitchen. And in the morning, a cook told it to Bruno. Bruno told it to the Emperor.

Chapter 3: The Weavers at the Palace

The Emperor listened to Bruno's story. His eyes opened wide.

"A magic cloth?" he said. "Stupid people cannot see it?"

"That is what they say, Your Majesty," said Bruno.

The Emperor walked up and down the dressing room and thought hard.

"This is great news," he said. "With clothes of this cloth, I can find the stupid people in my palace. I can see which ministers are bad at their jobs. Then I can send them away and find better ones. Bring these weavers to me at once!"

An hour later, Max and Felix stood in the great hall of the palace. They wore the same old coats, but they walked like princes. They bowed very low in front of the throne.

"Are you the famous weavers?" asked the Emperor.

"We are, Your Majesty," said Max.

"Tell me about your cloth," said the Emperor.

Max told him everything, about the colours and the patterns and about the magic. Felix stood next to him and nodded.

"It is as light as a feather," said Felix, "and as soft as a cloud."

"I want it," said the Emperor. "I want a whole new suit of this cloth, with a coat, a shirt, trousers, and a long cloak. How much do you want?"

Max looked at Felix. Then he named a price, a very, very high price.

The ministers in the hall looked at each other in surprise. But the Emperor did not even blink.

"Give them the money and everything they need," he said.

So the treasurer gave the two weavers a big bag of gold. Max took it with both hands, because it was heavy.

"We also need silk, Your Majesty," said Felix, "the finest silk in your kingdom. And a lot of gold thread."

"Of course," said the Emperor. "Treasurer, give them silk and gold thread."

The weavers received a large, bright room in the palace with tall windows. Servants carried in two big looms. A loom is a wooden machine for weaving cloth.

Then the servants carried in boxes and boxes of silk. There was red silk, blue silk, and silk as white as snow. And there were long rolls of gold thread that shone in the sun.

"Thank you," said Max to the servants. "Now please leave us, because we must work alone. Our work is a secret."

The servants left and closed the door.

Felix ran to the boxes and opened them one by one. "Look at all this!" he whispered. "We are rich, Max! Rich!"

"Shh, be quiet," said Max. "Put everything in our bags, quickly."

They put all the silk and all the gold thread into their old bags. They hid the bags under the beds in the corner.

Then they sat down at the empty looms.

"Now we work," said Max, and he laughed.

He moved his hands in the air and pushed the parts of the loom up and down. Clack, clack, clack. Felix did the same at the other loom. Clack, clack, clack. But there was nothing on the looms, no silk and no thread, nothing at all.

They worked like this all day. In the evening they lit candles and worked late into the night. People in the palace heard the noise from the room.

"Clack, clack, clack," said a kitchen maid in the hall. "Listen, they are working very hard."

"They are working for the Emperor," said a guard. "Of course they work hard."

Every few days, the weavers asked for more.

"We need more silk," said Felix to the treasurer. "The cloth is very big."

"And more gold thread," said Max. "The patterns are very difficult."

The treasurer always gave them more. Every time, the silk and gold went into the bags under the beds.

The clacking never stopped, but the looms were always empty.

Chapter 4: The Old Minister

After one week, the Emperor was curious. He wanted to know about his new cloth.

"I want to see it and how it looks," he said.

But then he stopped. He had a strange feeling in his stomach.

"What if I cannot see it?" he thought. "What if I am stupid, or bad at my job?"

He did not really think he was stupid. He was the Emperor, after all. But he was a little bit afraid.

"I will send someone else first," he decided. "Someone honest and wise."

He thought about his ministers, and then he smiled.

"My old Prime Minister is the right man," he said. "He is very clever. He is very good at his job. Nobody is better."

The Prime Minister was an old man with white hair and small round glasses. He worked for the Emperor for forty years. Everyone in the palace liked him.

"Go to the weavers," said the Emperor. "Look at the cloth and tell me about it."

"Yes, Your Majesty," said the old minister, and he bowed.

He walked down the long hall to the weavers' room. He heard the clacking from far away. Clack, clack, clack.

He knocked on the door.

"Come in, come in!" said Max.

The old minister went inside and looked at the first loom and the second loom. He took off his glasses, cleaned them, and put them on again. He opened his eyes very wide.

There was nothing on the looms, nothing at all.

"Oh no, I cannot see anything!" he thought.

But he said nothing.

"Come closer, sir, and look at this fine pattern," said Felix. "Isn't it lovely?"

He pointed at the empty loom with his finger.

The poor old minister looked and looked. But he still saw nothing, because there was nothing.

"Am I stupid?" he thought. "I never thought so, but maybe I am. Or maybe I am bad at my job. Nobody must know this, nobody!"

"Well?" said Max. "You are not saying anything, sir, so do you like it?"

"Oh, yes!" said the old minister. "It is very, very beautiful."

He moved his glasses up and down on his nose.

"What a pattern, and what colours!" he said. "Yes, I will tell the Emperor that I like it very much."

"We are very happy," said Max. "Now let us show you all the little details."

The two weavers began to describe the cloth. They talked about every colour and every line.

"Here, you see, is a pattern of pink and red roses," said Felix. "And here, along the edge, is a line of little gold birds."

"In the middle, there is a blue and silver river," said Max. "The light moves on the water when you walk."

The old minister listened very carefully. He wanted to remember every word.

"Roses, pink and red," he repeated quietly. "Little gold birds. A blue and silver river."

Then he thanked the weavers and went back to the Emperor.

"Well?" asked the Emperor. "How is the cloth?"

"It is magnificent, Your Majesty," said the old minister. "I never saw anything like it in my life."

He told the Emperor about the roses, the little gold birds. The river of blue and silver. He used the weavers' words exactly.

The Emperor was delighted. "Good, good!" he said.

That night, the old minister did not sleep. He sat by his window and looked at the moon.

"Forty years of work. I am a fool," he said to himself. "I cannot see the cloth."

He felt very sad and very alone. But he did not tell anybody, not even his wife.

Chapter 5: Everyone Talks About the Cloth

The next day, the weavers asked for more money, more silk, and more gold thread. They received everything, and the bags under the beds were very full now. Felix had to find a third bag.

A few days later, the Emperor sent another man to the weavers. This was Sir Hugo, the Master of Ceremonies, a young and proud man with a long moustache. He organised all the parties and parades in the city.

"Go and see how the work is going," said the Emperor. "Will the cloth be ready soon?"

Sir Hugo went to the weavers' room and walked in with his chin high in the air.

He looked at the looms, and he saw nothing. He looked again, and he still saw nothing.

"Impossible," he thought. "I am not stupid. I am the cleverest man in the palace. So I must be bad at my job! But I am very good at my job, so what is happening?"

His face turned red, then white, then red again.

"Isn't it a lovely piece of cloth?" asked Max, and he moved his hands over the empty loom very gently.

"Look at these colours," said Felix. "Did you ever see such a green in your life?"

"N-no, never," said Sir Hugo. "Such a green, really, very green."

"And the little gold birds," said Max. "Do you like the birds?"

"I love the birds," said Sir Hugo quickly, "and they are the best part."

He touched his moustache nervously. Then he said goodbye and left the room very fast.

"It is very fine, Your Majesty," he told the Emperor. "The colours are perfect, the birds are perfect, and everything is perfect."

Now two great men from the palace said the same thing: the cloth was fine and grand. So the news went everywhere.

In the palace kitchen, the cooks talked about it.

"Sir Hugo says it is the most beautiful cloth in the world," said the head cook while she was cutting onions.

"The old minister says the same," said a young kitchen boy. "He says there is a blue and silver river on it."

"I hope I can see it one day," said a maid, and then she stopped. "But what if I can't?" she whispered. "What if I'm stupid?"

Nobody answered her, because everybody was thinking the same thing.

In the market square, people talked about it too.

"They say the cloth is magic and stupid people can't see it," said the baker.

"Then my neighbour won't see it," said a fishwife, and everyone laughed.

"Be careful," said an old man. "Maybe your neighbour will see it, and you won't."

The fishwife stopped laughing.

At the inn, the innkeeper told the story to all her guests. Every day the story got bigger.

"The cloth is softer than a baby's hair. It shines like the moon," she said. "And the weavers are the nicest men in the world. They ate here, you know, at that table in the corner."

In the evenings, families sat at home and talked quietly.

"Who in our street is stupid?" asked a little boy at dinner.

"Shh," said his mother. "Eat your soup."

Everyone in the city wanted to see the cloth. Everyone in the city was a little bit afraid of it.

In the dressing room, Otto and Bruno talked about it too.

"Will we see it, Otto?" asked Bruno. "When the Emperor puts it on?"

"Of course we will," said Otto. "We are not stupid. We are very good at our jobs."

"Yes," said Bruno slowly. "Yes, we are." But he did not look very sure.

Meanwhile, in the weavers' room, the clacking went on and on. Clack, clack, clack. And the looms stayed as empty as before.

Chapter 6: The Emperor Sees Nothing

At last, the Emperor could not wait any longer.

"I want to see my cloth now, while it is still on the looms," he said.

He chose a group of great lords to go with him. The old minister and Sir Hugo were in the group too. They walked together down the long hall. The Emperor walked first, in a coat of purple velvet.

The weavers heard them coming. They worked faster than ever. Clack, clack, clack!

"Welcome, Your Majesty!" said Max, and he bowed very low.

"Isn't it grand?" said the old minister. He pointed at the first loom.

"Look at the colours and the pattern, Your Majesty!" said Sir Hugo.

They pointed at the empty looms. They were sure that everyone else could see the cloth.

The Emperor looked at the first loom. Then he looked at the second loom.

He saw nothing.

His heart began to beat very fast. His hands felt cold.

"What is this?" he thought. "I can't see anything, and this is terrible! Am I stupid, or am I a bad emperor? This is the worst thing in the world!"

All the people in the room were looking at him and waiting for him to speak.

The Emperor took a deep breath. Then he smiled a big smile.

"Oh, it is very beautiful," he said. "It is the most beautiful thing in my palace."

He nodded slowly and looked at the empty loom with a happy face.

"Yes," he said, "I like it very, very much."

Behind him, the lords looked at the looms too. Not one of them saw anything, but they all said the same thing.

"Beautiful!"

"Magnificent!"

"Excellent!"

"Wonderful!"

Nobody wanted to be stupid. Nobody wanted to lose their job.

"Your Majesty," said Sir Hugo, "you must wear clothes of this cloth in the great parade next week."

The great parade was the best day of the year. Every year, the Emperor walked through the streets of the city. All the people came to see him and his new clothes.

"Yes," said the Emperor. "That is an excellent idea."

He turned to the weavers. "You are true artists," he said. "I am making you Knights of the Loom. You can wear a gold medal on your coats. And you will have the title of Imperial Weavers."

"Oh, thank you, Your Majesty!" said Felix. "This is a great honour for us."

The Emperor left the room with all his people. The door closed behind them.

Max and Felix looked at each other. Then they sat on the floor and laughed and laughed. They laughed so much that tears ran down their faces.

"Knights!" said Felix. "We are real knights now!"

"Shh, not so loud," said Max, but he was laughing too.

On the night before the parade, the weavers did not sleep at all. They lit sixteen candles. All the palace could see the light under their door.

"Look how hard they are working," said the guards.

Inside the room, the two men pretended to take the cloth off the looms. They lifted nothing into the air and folded it carefully. Then they cut the air with big scissors. Snip, snip, snip. They sewed with needles, but the needles had no thread.

"Ouch," said Felix once, and he laughed. "I almost cut my finger on nothing."

"The trousers are almost finished," said Max loudly, for the guards outside. "Now the coat, and now the cloak."

Just before sunrise, Max opened the door.

"The clothes are ready!" he said. "The Emperor's new clothes are ready at last!"

Chapter 7: Light as a Spider's Web

On the morning of the parade, the Emperor came to the weavers' room. Otto and Bruno came with him, and many ministers and knights came too.

The two weavers held up their arms, as if they were holding something.

"Here are the trousers, Your Majesty," said Max.

"Here is the coat," said Felix.

"And here is the cloak," said Max. "It is very long, so look at it!"

"These clothes are as light as a spider's web," said Felix. "When you wear them, you will feel nothing. You will think you have nothing on. That is the special beauty of this cloth."

"Yes, yes, of course," said all the ministers. But they could not see anything, because there was nothing to see.

"Would Your Majesty please take off your clothes?" asked Max. "Then we can help you put on the new ones here, in front of the big mirror."

The Emperor took off his coat, his shirt, his trousers. His shoes, and he stood in front of the mirror in his underwear.

The weavers pretended to dress him. First they put on the trousers, then the shirt, and then the coat. They pulled and buttoned and smoothed. They were very careful.

"Now the cloak," said Felix. He walked around the Emperor and pretended to put a cloak on his shoulders.

The Emperor turned to the left and to the right. He looked at himself in the mirror for a long time.

"How well it fits!" said the ministers. "What a fine cut! What colours!"

Otto and Bruno stood near the door. Bruno looked at the Emperor. Then he looked at Otto with his mouth open.

"Otto," he whispered. "Otto, I can't see..."

"Shh!" said Otto quickly, and his face was pale. "Don't say it, don't say anything. Do you want to lose your job?"

Bruno closed his mouth.

Then the Master of Ceremonies, Sir Hugo, came into the room.

"Your Majesty," he said, "the people are waiting outside. The canopy is ready."

A canopy is a fine cloth roof on four long poles. Four men carry it over the Emperor in a parade.

"I am ready," said the Emperor. He turned once more in front of the mirror. "Do my clothes look good?"

"Perfect, Your Majesty!" said everyone in the room.

The two chamberlains came next. Their job was to carry the end of the Emperor's long cloak. They walked behind the Emperor. They bent down and put their hands near the floor. They pretended to pick up the cloak.

They held their hands in the air very carefully. They did not want anyone to know that they could see nothing.

"Thank you, weavers," said the Emperor. "You are true artists."

"Thank you, Your Majesty," said Max, and he bowed. "We will watch the parade from the window."

But they did not watch the parade.

As soon as the Emperor left the room, Max and Felix pulled the bags out from under the beds. The bags were full of gold, silver, silk, and gold thread. They were very heavy.

"Quick," said Max. "Everyone is going to the parade. Nobody will look at us."

They went down the back stairs of the palace. They walked through the empty kitchen and out through a little door. In the street, everyone was running towards the main square. Nobody looked at two men with heavy bags.

They walked to the city gate, but the guards were not there. They were watching the parade too.

"Goodbye, fine city!" said Felix.

"Goodbye, stupid Emperor!" said Max.

They walked out of the gate and along the road. They did not look back. By lunchtime, they were far, far away.

Chapter 8: A Child's Voice

The bells rang across the city, and the parade began.

First came the soldiers with drums and trumpets. Then came the knights on white horses. Then came the four men with the fine canopy.

Under the canopy walked the Emperor, slowly and proudly, with his head high. Behind him walked the two chamberlains, holding nothing in the air.

The streets were full of people. They stood at the windows and on the roofs. Children sat on their fathers' shoulders. Everyone wanted to see the famous new clothes.

When the Emperor came near, the people saw him. Then there was a strange silence. For a moment, nobody said a word.

Then a man shouted, "Oh, look at the Emperor's new clothes! How beautiful!"

"What a fine cloak, and look how long it is!" said a woman.

"Look at the gold birds and the river!" said another. "I can see the river!"

"They fit him perfectly!" said the baker.

Everybody said something nice. Nobody wanted to be stupid. Nobody wanted their neighbours to think they were bad at their jobs.

The innkeeper was there too. She was clapping her hands. "I knew those weavers!" she said loudly. "They ate at my inn!"

The Emperor walked on and smiled. He heard the people, and he felt very happy.

"They can all see it," he thought. "Everyone can see it, everyone except me."

In the crowd, there was a little girl of about six years old. She was standing on a box next to her father. She was looking at the Emperor with big, round eyes.

She looked for a long time at his legs and his arms and his round belly.

Then she pulled her father's sleeve very hard.

"But Papa," she said in a loud, clear voice, "the Emperor isn't wearing anything!"

Her father went red. "Shh!" he said, but then he looked at the Emperor again.

He looked at the Emperor's bare legs and his bare arms. He looked at his underwear.

"Did you hear that?" he said slowly to the man next to him. "Listen to the innocent child. She says the Emperor isn't wearing anything."

The man next to him whispered it to his wife. His wife whispered it to her sister. The whisper went from person to person, faster and faster.

"The little girl says he has no clothes on."

"He has nothing on!"

"Look! He really has no clothes on!"

Soon the whole street was talking. Then someone started laughing. And then the whole crowd shouted together:

"The Emperor isn't wearing anything at all!"

The Emperor heard them. He felt the cold wind on his skin, and he shivered.

"They are right," he thought. "Oh, they are right, and there are no clothes. There never were any clothes."

His face turned bright red. He wanted to run back to the palace and hide in his dressing room.

But he was the Emperor. The parade must go on.

"I must keep walking and finish the parade," he thought. "An emperor cannot run away."

So he lifted his head even higher. He walked even more proudly than before. And behind him, the two chamberlains walked on too. They held their hands up very carefully. They carried the long cloak that was not there.

That evening, in the palace, the Emperor sat in his dressing room with Otto and Bruno. He wore an old brown coat.

"Bruno," he said quietly. "Could you see the clothes this morning?"

Bruno looked at Otto. Otto looked at the floor.

"No, Your Majesty, I couldn't," said Bruno.

"Neither could I," said the Emperor. He was quiet for a long time. Then, for the first time in many years, he laughed at himself.

"Next time," he said, "I will listen to the children first."

Far away, on a dusty road, two men with heavy bags were walking towards another city. But that is another story.

The End
