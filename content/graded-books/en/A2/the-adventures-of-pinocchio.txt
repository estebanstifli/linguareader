Chapter 1: The Talking Log

Once upon a time there was a piece of wood. It was not special at all, just a simple log, the kind people put on the fire in winter.

One day this log arrived in the workshop of an old carpenter. His name was Master Antonio, but everybody called him Master Cherry. That was because the end of his nose was always red and shiny, like a ripe cherry.

Master Cherry looked at the log and smiled. "Good," he said. "I am going to make a leg for my little table."

He took his sharp axe and lifted it high in the air. But just then he heard a very small voice.

"Please don't hit me too hard!"

Master Cherry stopped. He looked around the room, under the bench and inside the cupboard. Then he opened the door and looked into the street. There was nobody.

"I understand," he said, and he laughed nervously. "I imagined that little voice. Back to work!"

He lifted the axe again and hit the log hard.

"Ow! You hurt me!" cried the same little voice.

This time Master Cherry's eyes nearly jumped out of his head. His mouth fell open and his tongue hung down to his chin.

"Where did that voice come from?" he whispered. "There is nobody here, so can a piece of wood cry like a child? No, it is impossible."

He picked up the log and threw it against the wall. Then he listened and waited, but there was nothing.

"Good," he said. "I was wrong, and now I am going to make it smooth."

He took his plane and began to move it up and down the log. And the little voice started to laugh.

"Stop it, you are tickling me!"

This was too much for poor Master Cherry. He fell to the floor like a sack of potatoes. When he opened his eyes, his red nose was blue with fear.

Just then somebody knocked at the door.

"Come in," said the carpenter in a weak voice.

A small, lively old man walked into the workshop. His name was Geppetto, and he wore a yellow wig. The boys of the street liked to call him "Polendina", because the wig looked like yellow corn porridge. Geppetto hated that name, and when he heard it, he became as angry as a wild animal.

"Good morning, Master Antonio," said Geppetto. "Why are you sitting on the floor?"

"I am teaching the ants their letters," said Master Cherry. "What do you want, neighbour, so early in the morning?"

"I had an idea this morning," said Geppetto. "I want to make a wonderful wooden puppet. It is going to dance, and fight, and do jumps in the air. I am going to travel around the world with it and earn some bread and a glass of wine. Can you give me a piece of wood?"

Master Cherry was very happy, because he wanted to say goodbye to that strange log.

But at that moment the little voice called out, "Polendina!"

Geppetto turned red. "Why are you calling me that?" he shouted at Master Cherry.

"I did not call you anything!"

The two old men started to fight, and they pulled hair and scratched faces. At the end, Geppetto had the carpenter's grey wig in his hand, and Master Cherry had Geppetto's yellow wig in his teeth.

Then they stopped and gave back the wigs. They shook hands and promised to be good friends for the rest of their lives.

Master Cherry gave the log to Geppetto. The log jumped out of his hands and hit Geppetto hard on his thin legs.

"Ow! Is this how you give presents, Master Antonio?"

"It was not me! It was the log!"

They nearly started to fight again. But Geppetto only took the log under his arm and walked home, a little lame.

Chapter 2: A Puppet Called Pinocchio

Geppetto lived in a very small room under the stairs. There was one old chair, a bad bed and a little table. On the wall there was a fireplace with a fire and a pot of soup. But the fire and the pot were not real, because they were only a painting.

Geppetto sat down with his tools. "What name am I going to give my puppet?" he said. "I know, I am going to call him Pinocchio, because it is a lucky name."

First he made the hair, and then he made the forehead and the eyes. The moment the eyes were ready, they moved and looked at him.

"Wooden eyes, why are you looking at me?" asked Geppetto. Nobody answered.

Then he made the nose. And the nose began to grow, and it grew and grew and grew. Geppetto cut it, but it only grew longer.

Then he made the mouth, and the mouth immediately started to laugh at him.

"Stop laughing!" said Geppetto. The mouth stopped laughing, but then it put out its tongue.

Geppetto made the chin, the neck, the shoulders, the arms and the hands. The hands took his yellow wig off his head.

"Pinocchio, give me back my wig!" But Pinocchio put the wig on his own head. Geppetto felt very sad. "Bad boy," he said quietly. "You are not finished, and already you have no respect for your father."

At last he made the legs and the feet, and the feet kicked him on the nose.

Geppetto took the puppet by the hand and taught him to walk. Pinocchio's legs were stiff at first, but then he walked a little better. Then he started to run. He ran around the room, out of the door and into the street.

"Stop him!" shouted Geppetto. But the people in the street only laughed. A wooden puppet that ran like a horse was a very funny thing!

Finally a policeman stepped into the road and caught Pinocchio by the nose. Geppetto arrived, out of breath, and said, "Wait until we get home!"

When he heard this, Pinocchio threw himself on the ground and refused to walk. The people began to talk.

"Poor puppet! That old man is cruel, and he is going to beat him at home."

So the policeman let Pinocchio go, and he took poor Geppetto to prison.

Pinocchio ran home across the fields. He went into the little room and sat down, very happy. Then he heard a sound: "Cri-cri-cri."

"Who is there?" asked Pinocchio.

On the wall there was a big cricket. "I am the Talking Cricket," it said. "I am a hundred years old, and I live in this room."

"This is my room now, so go away!"

"I am going to go," said the Cricket, "but first I want to tell you something. Children who are rude to their parents and run away from home are never happy."

"Sing, Cricket, sing! Tomorrow I am going to leave this house, because I don't want to go to school. I want to run after butterflies and climb trees."

"Then you are going to grow up a donkey," said the Cricket, "and everybody is going to laugh at you."

Pinocchio became very angry. He took a wooden hammer from the bench and threw it at the Cricket. The poor Cricket fell from the wall and did not speak again.

Night came, and Pinocchio was hungry. He ran to the pot on the fire, but it was only a painting. Then he found an egg in a corner, and he was very happy. But when he broke it, a little chicken jumped out.

"Thank you, Mister Pinocchio," said the chicken. "Have a nice day!" And it flew out of the window.

Pinocchio cried, because it was cold and it was raining. He went home, put his wet feet on the warm stove and fell asleep. During the night his wooden feet burned away.

In the morning somebody knocked and shouted, "Open the door!" It was Geppetto, home from prison.

"I can't, Father, because I have no feet!"

Geppetto climbed in through the window. He was angry, but when he saw his little puppet without feet, he felt sorry for him. He took three pears out of his pocket. "These were my breakfast," he said, "but I want you to have them."

Then he made two new feet for Pinocchio.

"Thank you, Father," said Pinocchio. "I promise to go to school and study hard."

Chapter 3: The Puppet Theatre

"To go to school, I need clothes and a book," said Pinocchio.

Geppetto was very poor, and he did not have a single coin in his pocket. But he made a little suit from flowered paper. He also made shoes from tree bark and a hat from bread.

Pinocchio ran to the bowl of water and looked at himself. "I look like a real gentleman!" he said.

"Now I need an ABC book," said Pinocchio.

Geppetto stood up and put on his old coat, which was full of holes. He went out into the snow. When he came back, he had a new ABC book in his hand. But he did not have his coat any more.

"Where is your coat, Father?"

"I sold it, because I was too hot."

Pinocchio understood, and he jumped up and kissed his father on the face and on the hands.

When the snow stopped, Pinocchio walked to school with his new book under his arm. As he walked, he made big plans in his little wooden head.

"Today I am going to learn to read, and tomorrow I am going to learn to write. The next day I am going to learn numbers. Then I am going to earn lots of money and buy my father a beautiful new coat, made of gold and silver."

Just then he heard music in the distance. Pee-pee-pee, boom-boom-boom!

"What is that music? I can go to school tomorrow, because school is always there."

He ran down the road to a small square near the sea. There was a big tent with bright colours. A lot of people were standing in front of it.

"What is it?" Pinocchio asked a boy.

"The Great Puppet Theatre! Can't you read the poster? A ticket costs four pennies."

Pinocchio had no money. "Do you want to buy my ABC book for four pennies?" he asked. The boy said no, but a man who sold old clothes bought the book. Poor Geppetto was at home in his shirt, shivering with cold.

Inside the theatre, the puppets Harlequin and Punchinello were on the stage. They were arguing and hitting each other with sticks, and the people laughed and laughed. Suddenly Harlequin stopped and pointed.

"Look, it is our brother Pinocchio!"

"Pinocchio! Pinocchio!" shouted all the puppets. They pulled him onto the stage and hugged him and danced with him. Nobody watched the play, and the people were angry. "We want the play! We want the play!"

Then the owner of the theatre came out. He was a huge man with a black beard down to the floor. His mouth was as big as an oven, and his eyes were like two red lanterns. In his hand he held a long whip, and his name was Fire-Eater.

The puppets stopped dancing and shook like leaves.

After the show, Fire-Eater went to the kitchen. A whole sheep was cooking over the fire, but there was not enough wood.

"Bring me that new puppet," he said. "He is made of good dry wood. He is going to make a fine fire for my dinner."

Pinocchio screamed, "Father, save me! I don't want to die!"

Fire-Eater looked terrible, but he had a soft heart. When he felt sorry for somebody, he always sneezed, and now he sneezed loudly: "Atchoo!"

"Good news," whispered Harlequin. "He is sneezing, and that means he feels sorry for you, so you are saved."

"Do you have a father and a mother?" asked Fire-Eater.

"I have a father, and he is very poor. He sold his coat to buy my school book."

"Atchoo! Atchoo! Atchoo!" Fire-Eater sneezed three times. "Here are five gold coins for your father, and tell him I said hello."

Pinocchio thanked him a thousand times. He kissed all the puppets goodbye and ran home, happy and rich.

Chapter 4: The Fox and the Cat

Pinocchio did not get far. On the road he met a Fox who walked with a limp, and a Cat who was blind. They walked together and helped each other like good friends.

"Good morning, Pinocchio," said the Fox politely.

"How do you know my name?"

"I know your father well, and I saw him yesterday at his door, shivering in his shirt."

"Poor Father! But now he can stop worrying, look!" Pinocchio showed them the five gold coins. They shone in the sun.

"Do you want to have more money?" asked the Fox. "Do you want to turn five coins into a thousand?"

"Of course, but how?"

"It is very easy. Not far from here is a town called Catch-Fools. Near the town there is a magic field, and people call it the Field of Miracles. You dig a little hole, put your gold coins inside and cover them with earth. You water them, put a little salt on top and go to bed. During the night the coins grow into a tree. In the morning it is full of gold coins."

"Wonderful, let's go!" said Pinocchio.

That evening they arrived at the Red Lobster Inn. They were tired and hungry. The Cat ate thirty-five fish and four plates of tripe. The Fox ate a hare and a dozen chickens. Pinocchio ate only one nut and a piece of bread, because he was thinking about the golden tree.

At midnight the innkeeper woke him. "Your friends left early," he said, "and they are waiting for you at the Field of Miracles. And you must pay for the dinner." So Pinocchio paid with one of his gold coins and left.

Suddenly two black figures jumped out from the trees. They were thieves, and they wore black sacks over their heads, with holes for the eyes.

"Your money or your life!"

Pinocchio put the four coins under his tongue. The thieves tried to open his mouth with a knife. But Pinocchio bit hard and bit off a hand! It was not a human hand, but a cat's paw.

Then Pinocchio ran and ran across the fields, with the thieves behind him. At last they caught him. They tied him to a branch of a big oak tree and left him there in the wind.

"Tomorrow he is going to open his mouth," they said.

Not far away, in the forest, there was a little white house. In the window stood a beautiful girl with blue hair. She was a good fairy, more than a thousand years old. She saw the poor puppet, and she clapped her hands three times.

A big falcon flew to the tree and cut the rope with its beak. A fine carriage pulled by a hundred white mice brought Pinocchio to the little house. The Fairy put him to bed and called three doctors: a Crow, an Owl and the Talking Cricket.

"In my opinion, he is dead," said the Crow. "But if he is not dead, then he is alive."

"In my opinion, he is alive," said the Owl. "But if he is not alive, then he is dead."

"I know this puppet," said the Cricket. "He is a bad son, and his poor father is going to die of sadness."

Pinocchio started to cry under the sheet.

"When a dead person cries," said the Crow, "it is a sign that he is getting better."

The Fairy gave Pinocchio some medicine. It was very bitter. "I don't want it, give me some sugar first!" he said. He ate the sugar, but he refused the medicine.

Then the door opened, and four black rabbits came in. They carried a little coffin on their shoulders.

"We are here for you," they said.

"I am not dead yet!" cried Pinocchio. He drank the medicine in one second. Two minutes later he jumped out of bed, well again.

Chapter 5: A Very Long Nose

"Tell me," said the Fairy kindly, "where are your gold coins now?"

"I lost them," said Pinocchio. But it was a lie, because the coins were in his pocket.

As soon as he said the lie, his nose grew two fingers longer.

"Where did you lose them?"

"In the forest," he said, and his nose grew longer again.

"Then we can find them, because everything in this forest comes back to me."

"Oh, I remember now, I did not lose them, I swallowed them with the medicine."

At this third lie, his nose grew so long that he could not turn around. When he turned one way, he hit the bed. When he turned the other way, he hit the window. When he looked up, he hit the ceiling.

The Fairy laughed.

"Why are you laughing?" asked Pinocchio.

"I am laughing at your lies. There are two kinds of lies, Pinocchio: some lies have short legs, and other lies have long noses. Yours are the long-nosed kind."

Pinocchio felt very ashamed, and he wanted to hide, but he could not even leave the room. He cried for half an hour.

The Fairy opened the window, and a thousand woodpeckers flew in. They sat on his nose, and peck, peck, peck, they made it small again.

"You are so kind, my dear Fairy," said Pinocchio. "I love you very much."

"I love you too," she said. "Stay with me, and you can be my little brother, and I can be your good sister. I am going to send a message to your father. He can come here and live with us."

Pinocchio was very happy, and he ran into the forest to meet his father. But in the forest he met the Fox and the Cat again. The Cat had a bandage on one front leg.

"What happened to your paw?" asked Pinocchio.

"Oh, he gave it to a poor hungry wolf," said the Fox. "He is so generous."

Then they talked again about the Field of Miracles. And Pinocchio forgot his father and the Fairy, and he followed them.

They walked for half a day and arrived at Catch-Fools.

The Field of Miracles looked like any other field. Pinocchio dug a hole, put in his four coins and covered them with earth. He walked to the river, filled his shoe with water and watered the hole. Then he put a little salt on top.

"Now go away for twenty minutes," said the Fox. "When you come back, the tree is going to be ready."

Pinocchio walked around the town.

He came back to the field, but there was no tree. He dug and dug in the earth, but the coins were gone.

Then he heard a laugh from a parrot on a branch. "Poor fool," said the parrot. "Money does not grow in fields. While you walked in the town, the Fox and the Cat dug up your coins and ran away."

Pinocchio ran to the judge, an old gorilla with gold glasses, and told him the whole story. The gorilla listened carefully and nodded and nodded. Then he rang a bell.

"This poor boy lost four gold coins," he said to the guards. "Put him in prison."

So Pinocchio went to prison for four months, because thieves robbed him.

Chapter 6: The Land of Toys

When he came out of prison, Pinocchio ran back to the Fairy's house. But the house was gone, and in its place there was a white stone. On the stone were these words: "Here lies the girl with the blue hair. She died of sadness, because her little brother Pinocchio did not come back."

Pinocchio fell on the ground and cried all night. "My dear Fairy, why did you die and not me? I was bad, and you were good. And where is my father?"

A big pigeon flew down. "Your father is at the seaside," it said. "He is building a little boat. He wants to cross the sea and look for you in other countries."

The pigeon carried Pinocchio on its back to the sea. There, in the middle of the grey waves, Pinocchio saw a tiny boat with a tiny man inside. It was Geppetto.

"Father! Father!" he called, and Geppetto waved his hat. Then a huge wave came, and the boat disappeared.

Pinocchio jumped into the water to save him. He was made of wood, so he could not sink, and he swam all night. In the morning the waves threw him onto an island.

It was the Island of the Busy Bees, and everybody there worked. Pinocchio was hungry, but he did not want to work. A woman passed with two jugs of water. "If you carry one of my jugs," she said, "I am going to give you bread and soup."

Pinocchio carried the jug to her house and ate and ate. Then he looked at the woman's face, and she had blue hair.

"You are my Fairy, and you are not dead!" he cried.

The Fairy smiled. "I was a girl when you left me. Now I am a woman, so I can be your mother."

"Oh, yes! But I am tired of being a puppet. I want to be a real boy."

"Then you must be good, go to school, tell the truth and help your father."

So Pinocchio went to school and studied hard, and the teacher was happy with him. But some lazy boys did not like him. One morning they said, "There is a huge Dogfish on the beach, as big as a mountain. Let's go and see it!"

Pinocchio missed school and went with them. On the beach there was no Dogfish, because the boys only wanted to laugh at him. They threw books at his head, and one heavy book hit another boy, who fell down. Then a policeman came, and Pinocchio ran away.

At last Pinocchio got home, and the Fairy forgave him again. "Tomorrow you are going to become a real boy," she said. "Invite all your friends to a party."

Pinocchio went out to invite them, and on the way he met his best friend. His name was Candlewick, because he was very thin and tall, like the wick of a candle. He was the laziest boy in the school.

"Where are you going, Candlewick?"

"I am going to the Land of Toys, so come with me! There are no schools and no teachers there. Every week has six Thursdays and one Sunday. The holidays start on the first of January and end on the thirty-first of December."

"No, I must go home," said Pinocchio.

Just then a coach arrived. It was full of happy boys, and twelve pairs of little donkeys pulled it. The donkeys wore white shoes, like people. The coachman was short and fat, and he smiled all the time.

"Come in, children!" he said in a sweet voice. "There is room for everybody."

"Just one day," said Pinocchio to himself. And he climbed on the back of a donkey.

The Land of Toys was full of children. They played ball, rode bicycles, ate sweets and sang all day. Nobody read, wrote or worked.

Pinocchio and Candlewick stayed there for five months, and the days flew past like dreams.

Chapter 7: Donkey Ears

One morning Pinocchio woke up and scratched his head. His ears felt strange, so he ran to the mirror. His ears were long and hairy, like donkey ears!

He cried and shouted, and a little squirrel from next door came to see him.

"I am sorry," said the squirrel. "You have donkey fever, the illness of all lazy boys who never go to school. In two or three hours you are going to be a real donkey."

Pinocchio put a big cotton hat on his head, down to his nose. He went to see Candlewick, who was wearing a big cotton hat too.

"Do you have a headache?" asked Pinocchio.

"Yes, and you?" said Candlewick.

"Me too."

Then they agreed to take off their hats together. One, two, three, and under the hats were four long donkey ears. The two friends looked at each other, and they laughed and laughed. Then suddenly they stopped and fell on their hands. Their hands became hooves and their faces became long. Grey hair grew all over their bodies, and a tail grew behind them.

They wanted to cry, but all that came out was "Hee-haw! Hee-haw!"

The door opened, and it was the fat coachman, smiling as usual. "Good morning, my little donkeys," he said. "Now I am going to sell you at the market."

That was his secret: he took lazy boys to the Land of Toys. Later, when they turned into donkeys, he sold them, and that was how he became rich.

A farmer bought Candlewick, and the director of a circus bought Pinocchio. He taught him to dance, to jump through hoops and to stand on his back legs. The lessons were hard, and the whip hurt.

On the first night of the show, the circus was full. Pinocchio danced and jumped very well. Then, in the crowd, he saw a beautiful lady with blue hair. She wore a gold medal with his picture on it.

"My Fairy!" he wanted to shout. But he only said "Hee-haw!" so loudly that everybody laughed. The director hit him, and when Pinocchio looked again, and the lady was gone.

He was so sad that he jumped badly. He fell and hurt his leg, and a lame donkey was no good for the circus. So the director sold him for a few pennies to a man who wanted a donkey skin to make a drum.

The man took Pinocchio to the sea. He tied a big stone to his leg and a rope around his neck. Then he pushed him into the water. "In an hour he is going to be dead," he thought. "Then I can pull him out and take the skin."

After an hour, the man pulled the rope. But there was no donkey at the end, only a wooden puppet, moving like an eel.

"Where is my donkey?" shouted the man.

"I am your donkey," laughed Pinocchio. "When I went into the sea, a thousand little fish came. They ate my ears, my tail and all the donkey skin. And under the donkey there was me, the old puppet, as good as new. I think my good Fairy sent them."

The man was very angry, but Pinocchio jumped back into the sea and swam away.

He swam and swam. In the distance he saw a white rock. On the rock there was a little goat with blue hair. She was calling him, and he swam towards her as fast as he could.

But behind him a terrible head came out of the water. It was the giant Dogfish, as big as a five-storey house. Its mouth was open like a cave, with three rows of teeth.

"Hurry, Pinocchio!" cried the goat.

Pinocchio swam faster, but the Dogfish was faster. With one breath it swallowed him, like a little sweet.

Chapter 8: A Real Boy

Inside the Dogfish it was very dark, and Pinocchio could not see anything. It smelled terrible. Every time the Dogfish breathed, a great wind blew through its stomach.

"Help! Is anybody here?"

"Only a poor Tuna," said a voice. "The Dogfish swallowed me too."

"I don't want to die here!"

"Then you must escape, but how? I am going to wait and see."

In the far distance Pinocchio saw a small light. He walked towards it, in the dark, through puddles of water and pieces of fish. He walked and walked for a long time.

At last he arrived at a little table with a candle in a green bottle. At the table sat a little old man with white hair, who was eating a few small fish.

Pinocchio stopped, and then he ran to him and threw his arms around his neck.

"Father, my dear father, I found you at last!"

Geppetto rubbed his eyes. "Is it really you, my Pinocchio?"

"Yes, and I am never going to leave you again! Oh, Father, I was so bad. I sold my book, I believed the Fox and the Cat, and I became a donkey. But I always thought of you."

"The Dogfish swallowed me two years ago," said Geppetto. "It swallowed my little boat, and it also swallowed a ship full of food, candles and matches. I lived on that food, but now everything is finished, and this is my last candle."

"Then we must escape now, so come with me," said Pinocchio.

They walked to the mouth of the Dogfish. The Dogfish was old and it had a weak chest, so it slept with its mouth open. Through it they could see the stars and the moon.

"Climb on my shoulders, Father, because I can swim," said Pinocchio.

They walked quietly over the huge tongue. But just then, the Dogfish sneezed. They fell back into its stomach, and their candle went out.

They tried again, and this time they jumped into the sea. Pinocchio swam with his father on his back. The night was cold and the land was very far. After a long time Pinocchio was tired.

"I can't swim any more, and we are going to die."

"Who is going to die?" said a voice. It was the Tuna! "I escaped too, so hold on to me."

The Tuna carried them to the beach. Pinocchio kissed him on the nose, and the Tuna cried like a child.

On the road they met two beggars, the Fox and the Cat. The Cat was really blind now, and the Fox had no tail.

"Please give us a coin," they said.

"No," said Pinocchio. "You tricked me once, so goodbye, false friends." And he walked on with his father.

They found a little hut with straw on the roof. Inside lived the Talking Cricket, and he was alive!

Geppetto was ill and weak, and he needed milk. A farmer named Giangio had a cow. "You can have a glass of milk," he said, "if you turn my water wheel. You must bring up a hundred buckets of water."

Pinocchio worked very hard, and next to the wheel there was a sick donkey. Pinocchio bent down and whispered, "Who are you?"

"I am Candlewick," said the donkey. Then he closed his eyes and died, and Pinocchio cried for his friend.

For five months Pinocchio got up before the sun and worked at the water wheel. In the evenings he made baskets and sold them, and he learned to read by candlelight. He bought milk and soft bread for his father. Slowly Geppetto became stronger.

One day Pinocchio saved some money for a new suit. On the road he met a snail. "The good Fairy is very ill," said the snail, "and she is poor, and she has no bread."

"Give her this money," said Pinocchio, "because I don't need a new suit."

That night he dreamed of the Fairy. She smiled and kissed him. "You have a good heart, Pinocchio," she said. "You helped your father and you helped me, so I forgive you for everything."

In the morning Pinocchio woke up and looked at his hands, and they were not wooden. He touched his face, and it was soft and warm. He ran to the mirror. A happy boy with brown hair and blue eyes looked back at him.

He was a real boy!

The little hut was now a pretty room with nice furniture. Geppetto was well again, and he was singing and carving wood, like in the old days.

"Father, what happened?"

"It is all thanks to you, my son. When bad boys become good, the whole house becomes happy."

"And where is the old puppet?"

Geppetto pointed to a chair. There sat a wooden puppet, with his head to one side and his arms hanging down. He did not move.

Pinocchio looked at him for a long time, and then he smiled.

"How funny I was when I was a puppet!" he said. "And how happy I am now to be a real boy!"

The End
