Chapter 1: A Girl by the Fire

Once upon a time, a man lives in a big house in a small town. He is a good man, but he is not happy, because his wife is dead.

The man has one daughter. She is young and kind, with soft hair and big brown eyes, like her mother.

One day, the man brings home a new wife. The new wife is tall and proud, with a cold face and a hard voice. She has two daughters. Their names are Javotte and Mimi.

Javotte, the older sister, is loud and lazy. Mimi, the younger sister, is lazy too, but not so unkind.

The new wife does not like the man's daughter. The girl is too pretty and too kind. Next to her, Javotte and Mimi do not look very nice.

"This girl must work," says the stepmother. "She can clean the house. She can cook for us."

And so the girl works every day. She washes the floors, the plates and the stairs. She cooks breakfast, lunch and dinner, and she carries water from the well.

She works and works and works.

Javotte and Mimi do not work. They sleep in big beds with soft pillows. They look in their tall mirrors all day.

"Do I look pretty?" says Javotte.

"Yes, you look very pretty," says Mimi. "And me?"

"You look pretty too," says Javotte. "But not as pretty as me."

The girl sleeps at the top of the house, under the roof, on old straw. Her room is small and cold. There is no mirror. There is only a little window and a lot of spiders.

The father does not help her, because he is afraid of his new wife. The girl does not cry to him. She does not want to make him sad.

In the evening, the girl is very cold. She sits next to the kitchen fire, in the warm ashes. Her face, her hands and her old dress are all grey.

Javotte laughs at her.

"Look at her!" she says. "She sits in the ashes like a cat. She is a cinder girl. Her name is Cinderbottom!"

"Cinderbottom is not a nice name," says Mimi. "Let us call her Cinderella."

And so, from now on, everybody calls her Cinderella.

But she is in rags. Her sisters are in silk and velvet. And Cinderella, in her rags, is a hundred times more beautiful than her two sisters.

Chapter 2: A Letter from the Palace

One morning, there is a knock at the door. A man in a red coat is there, with a letter in his hand.

"A letter from the palace," he says. "For the ladies of the house."

Cinderella takes the letter to the stepmother. She reads it, and her eyes are big.

"Girls! Girls!" she says. "Come here! Quick!"

Javotte and Mimi run down the stairs.

"What is it, Mother?" says Javotte.

"The king's son gives a ball," says the stepmother. "A big ball at the palace! There is music and dancing for two nights. And all the young ladies of the town must come."

"A ball!" says Mimi. "With the prince!"

"The prince is young," says Javotte. "The prince is handsome. And the prince wants a wife!"

"Yes," says the stepmother. "And one of my girls can be his wife."

The two sisters jump up and down and dance around the room.

Cinderella stands at the door with the broom in her hand. She does not say anything, but her eyes are bright.

Now the house is very busy. The sisters want new dresses, new shoes, new hats and new gloves. They talk about clothes all day long.

"I want my red velvet dress," says Javotte. "With the gold buttons. Red is my colour."

"I want my long coat with the flowers," says Mimi. "And my silver hat. And my best necklace."

Cinderella washes and irons the dresses and sews the buttons until late at night. The sisters ask her about everything, because she knows a lot about colours and hair.

"Cinderella, do my hair," says Javotte.

Cinderella does their hair very well, tall and pretty. She is not angry with them.

Javotte looks at Cinderella in the mirror.

"Cinderella," she says, "do you want to go to the ball?"

"Oh, please do not laugh at me," says Cinderella softly. "A ball is not for a girl like me."

"That is true!" says Javotte. "Everybody can laugh at a cinder girl at a ball!"

Mimi laughs too. Cinderella says nothing. She makes Javotte's hair very pretty anyway.

For two days, the sisters do not eat, because they want small waists. They stand in front of the mirror and pull their dresses very tight.

"Pull harder!" says Javotte.

Snap! The laces break.

"Again!" says Javotte. "Pull harder!"

Snap! The laces break again. Twelve laces break in one day.

At last, the big evening is here.

Chapter 3: Tears in the Kitchen

The sisters are ready, in beautiful dresses and new shoes. A coach waits at the door.

"Goodbye, Cinderella!" says Javotte. "Clean the kitchen. Wash the floor. And do not eat our dinner!"

The three get into the coach. It goes down the road to the palace.

Cinderella watches and watches from the door. Now she cannot see the coach. It is dark and quiet.

Cinderella sits by the fire, in the ashes. And she cries and cries.

"I want to go," she says. "Oh, I want to go!"

Then there is a soft, silver light in the kitchen. Cinderella looks up.

An old woman stands by the fire. She has a long blue cloak, white hair and a kind face. In her hand, she has a little wand.

"Who are you?" says Cinderella.

"I am your godmother," says the old woman. "I am a fairy. Why do you cry, my dear?"

"I want... I want..." says Cinderella. But her tears do not stop.

"You want to go to the ball," says the godmother. "Is that it?"

"Yes," says Cinderella. "Oh, yes!"

"Then you are a good girl, and you can go," says the godmother. "But first, go to the garden. Bring me a pumpkin."

Cinderella does not understand. How can a pumpkin help? But she runs to the garden and brings back the biggest pumpkin.

The godmother makes the pumpkin empty with a knife. Then she touches it with her wand.

Whoosh! The pumpkin grows and grows. Now it is a beautiful golden coach, with windows and doors and big round wheels!

"Now," says the godmother, "go and look in the mousetrap."

In the mousetrap, there are six little grey mice. Cinderella opens the little door, and the mice run out one by one. The godmother touches each mouse with her wand. Whoosh! Whoosh! Whoosh! Now there are six beautiful grey horses in the kitchen.

"Good," says the godmother. "Now I need a coachman. Who can drive the coach?"

"Wait," says Cinderella. "There is a rat trap too. Maybe there is a rat in it."

"Good idea!" says the godmother. "Go and see."

In the rat trap, there are three rats. The godmother takes the fattest one, with very long whiskers.

Whoosh! The rat is a fat, happy coachman. He has a big hat and a very long moustache.

"Good evening, miss," he says, and he bows.

Chapter 4: A Dress of Gold

"Now," says the godmother, "go to the garden again. Look behind the watering can. There are six lizards there. Bring them to me."

Cinderella brings the six little green lizards to the kitchen. Whoosh! Now they are six footmen in green coats and white gloves. They stand behind the coach, very still, like lizards on a hot stone.

"There," says the godmother. "A coach, six horses, a coachman and six footmen. Now you can go to the ball. Are you happy?"

Cinderella looks at the golden coach, and then she looks down at her dress.

"Yes," she says. "But... can I go like this? In my old grey rags?"

The godmother smiles. She touches Cinderella with her wand.

Whoosh!

Now Cinderella has a dress of gold and silver, and it shines like the stars. There are jewels in her hair and pearls on her neck. Her face is clean and pink.

"Look at your feet," says the godmother.

On her feet, there are two little glass shoes. They shine like water in the sun.

"Glass slippers!" says Cinderella. "Oh, Godmother! Thank you, thank you!"

"Listen to me, my dear," says the godmother. Her voice is serious now. "This is very important. You must come home before midnight. You must leave the ball before twelve o'clock."

"Why?" says Cinderella.

"At twelve o'clock, my magic stops," says the godmother. "Your coach is a pumpkin again, and your horses are mice. And your beautiful dress is your old grey rags. Do you understand?"

"Yes, Godmother," says Cinderella. "I understand. Before midnight. I promise."

"Good girl," says the godmother. "Now go. And have a wonderful time."

A footman opens the door of the coach, and Cinderella gets in. "Ready, miss?" says the coachman.

"Ready!" says Cinderella.

The coach goes fast, fast, fast through the night. Cinderella sees the moon, the dark trees and the little houses of the town. And then she sees the palace.

The palace is big and white. It has a hundred windows, and there is a light in each one. Music comes out of the doors.

Cinderella's heart is very fast.

"Oh," she says. "I am so happy. And I am so afraid!"

The golden coach stops at the big stairs, and Cinderella gets out. Her glass slippers go click, click, click on the stairs.

Chapter 5: At the Ball

A servant runs to the prince.

"Your Highness!" he says. "A princess is here! A great princess! But nobody knows her!"

The prince goes to the stairs. When he sees Cinderella, he cannot move. He takes her hand and brings her into the big room.

In the big room, the music and the dancing stop. Everybody looks at Cinderella.

"Who is she?" says a lady.

"How beautiful she is!" says a man.

The old king looks at her too. He says to the queen, "I do not often see a girl so lovely."

"Nor do I," says the queen.

The music starts again. The prince bows to Cinderella.

"Can I have this dance?" he says.

"Yes," says Cinderella, and she smiles.

Cinderella dances very well, light like a feather. The prince does not look at anybody else.

At dinner, there are cakes and fruit and chocolate on the tables. But the prince does not eat. He only looks at Cinderella.

Cinderella sees her sisters at a table. They do not know her! Cinderella sits next to them.

"Good evening," she says. "Do you want some oranges? The prince gives them to me. There are too many for me."

"Oh! Thank you, Your Highness," says Javotte. Her face is red.

"You are very kind, Your Highness," says Mimi.

The sisters cannot believe it. A princess is kind to them! Cinderella almost laughs.

Then Cinderella hears a clock. Ding! It is a quarter to twelve.

"Oh! I must go," she says.

She bows to the king, the queen and the prince. Then she runs to her coach.

"Wait!" says the prince. "What is your name?"

But she is gone.

At home, Cinderella is in her rags again when the sisters knock at the door. She opens it. She rubs her eyes and yawns.

"Oh, you are so late," she says. "I am so sleepy."

But she is not sleepy at all.

"Cinderella!" says Javotte. "You must hear this! A princess comes to the ball. The most beautiful princess in the world! And she is very kind to us. She gives us oranges!"

"Nobody knows her name," says Mimi. "The prince wants to know it very much."

"Oh," says Cinderella, and she smiles a little. "Is she so beautiful? Oh, Javotte, please lend me your yellow dress. I want to go and see her."

"My dress? To you?" says Javotte. "To a dirty cinder girl? Never!"

Cinderella is glad. She does not want the dress at all.

Chapter 6: The Second Night

The next evening, the sisters go to the ball again. And the silver light comes back.

Whoosh! The pumpkin is a coach again. Whoosh! Cinderella has a new dress, blue and silver like the night sky. And on her feet, there are the little glass slippers.

"Remember," says the godmother. "Before midnight."

"Before midnight," says Cinderella. "I promise."

At the palace, the prince waits for her at the top of the stairs. When he sees her, his face is bright.

"You are here!" he says. "I am so happy."

All night, the prince stays next to Cinderella. He talks and dances only with her.

"Tell me your name," he says.

"Not tonight," says Cinderella, and she laughs.

"Where do you live?"

"Far away," says Cinderella.

They dance and talk and laugh. Cinderella is so happy that she forgets the time and her godmother's words.

Then the big clock in the tower begins.

Dong!

Cinderella stops. "What time is it?"

Dong! Dong!

"It is midnight," says the prince.

Dong! Dong! Dong!

"Oh no!" says Cinderella. "I must go! I must go now!"

She runs out of the big room and down the long hall to the stairs.

Dong! Dong! Dong!

The prince runs after her. "Wait! Please wait!"

But Cinderella runs faster. On the stairs, one glass slipper comes off her foot, but she cannot stop.

Dong! Dong! Dong!

The last bell rings. Twelve o'clock.

And now Cinderella is not a princess. She is in her old grey rags again, and her face is grey with ashes. There is no golden coach, only a pumpkin on the road. Six mice run into the grass. A fat rat runs into a hole. Six lizards run under a stone.

Cinderella runs home in the cold and the dark, with one glass slipper in her hand.

On the stairs, the prince finds the little glass slipper. He picks it up.

He asks the guards at the gate, "Where is the princess? Does a princess go out of the gate?"

"No, Your Highness," say the guards. "There is no princess. There is only a poor girl in grey rags. She runs very fast down the road."

The prince looks at the small, pretty slipper in his hand.

"I must find her," he says.

Chapter 7: The Glass Slipper

Late at night, the sisters come home.

"Is the princess there again?" asks Cinderella.

"Yes," says Mimi. "But at midnight, she runs away! She runs so fast! And she leaves one of her glass slippers on the stairs."

"The prince has the slipper now," says Javotte. "He looks at it all the time. He loves the girl with the glass slipper."

A few days later, a man with a trumpet stands in the town square.

"Listen, listen!" he says. "The prince has a message for all the people. He wants to marry the girl with the glass slipper. All the girls in the land must try the slipper. The prince marries the girl with the right foot!"

A gentleman from the palace carries the glass slipper on a soft red pillow. First the princesses try it, then the duchesses, then all the ladies of the court. But the slipper is too small for everybody.

The gentleman goes from house to house, in every street. Day after day, he looks and looks.

In one house, there is a big lady with very big feet. She wants to be a princess. She wants it very much.

"Give me the slipper!" she says. "It is my slipper. I know it!"

She pushes and pushes. Her face is red. Her foot is red. The slipper does not go on.

"Maybe it is a little small," she says. "Maybe my foot is a little big. Just today."

"Thank you, madam," says the gentleman. He takes the slipper. He goes out fast.

In the next house, there is an old lady. She is eighty years old. She has no teeth.

"I want to try it too," she says. "I am a girl! An old girl, but a girl!"

The gentleman smiles. He is a kind man. "Of course, madam," he says.

She tries. Her foot is too long. She laughs and laughs.

"Oh well," she says. "The prince is too young for me. Good luck, sir!"

The gentleman goes on. He is tired now. His feet hurt. But he must find the girl.

At last, he comes to the house of Cinderella's family.

Javotte and Mimi wait in the big room, in their best dresses.

"Me first!" says Javotte. "I am the oldest!"

Javotte puts her foot in the slipper and pushes hard. But her toes do not go in.

"It is a little small," she says. Her face is red. "My foot is tired today."

"No, madam," says the gentleman. "It is not your slipper."

"Now me!" says Mimi.

Mimi tries. Her toes go in, but her heel does not. She pulls and pushes and pulls.

"It is my slipper!" she says. "It is!"

"No, madam," says the gentleman. "Your heel is outside. It is not your slipper."

The stepmother is very angry. "There must be a mistake," she says.

In the corner of the room, Cinderella stands with her broom and smiles a little.

"Excuse me," she says softly. "Can I try it too?"

Chapter 8: The Slipper Fits

Javotte laughs very loudly.

"You?" she says. "The cinder girl? Ha, ha, ha!"

Mimi and the stepmother laugh too.

"Go back to the kitchen," says the stepmother. "This is not for you."

But the gentleman looks at Cinderella's face for a long time. It is grey with ashes, but it is very lovely.

"The prince's words are clear," he says. "All the girls in the land must try the slipper. All the girls. Please sit down, miss."

Cinderella sits on a chair, and the gentleman kneels with the glass slipper. Cinderella puts out her small foot.

The slipper goes on very easily. It fits like a glove!

The room is very quiet.

Then Cinderella takes the other glass slipper from her pocket. She puts it on.

"Oh!" says Mimi.

"Oh!" says Javotte.

"Oh, no," says the stepmother, very quietly.

Then the silver light comes into the room. The fairy godmother is there! She touches Cinderella with her wand.

Whoosh!

The grey rags are a dress of gold and silver again. Now Javotte and Mimi know her.

"The princess!" says Javotte. "The princess at the ball is... Cinderella!"

The two sisters fall on their knees and cry.

"Oh, Cinderella," says Mimi. "We are so unkind to you. Every day!"

"We laugh at you," says Javotte. "We give you all the work. We call you Cinderbottom. Please, please forgive us!"

Cinderella helps them stand up and kisses them.

"I forgive you," she says. "With all my heart. And I want you to love me. Always."

The gentleman bows very low. "Your Highness," he says, "the prince waits for you."

At the palace, the prince waits at the top of the stairs. His face is as bright as the sun.

"It is you," he says. "At last."

"It is me," says Cinderella.

The prince takes her hand. "Do you want to marry me?" he asks.

"Yes," says Cinderella. "With all my heart."

Soon there is a big wedding at the palace, with music and flowers everywhere. The bells ring and ring.

The fairy godmother is there too, in the first row, and she smiles all day.

Cinderella is good and kind, as always. She brings her sisters to live at the palace.

On the same day, Javotte and Mimi marry two great lords.

And Cinderella and her prince? They live in the big white palace. They dance every evening. And they are happy for the rest of their lives.

The End
