Chapter 1: A Small House and Three Big Pigs

Once upon a time, there is an old mother pig. She lives in a little house near a green hill. She has three little pigs, and their names are Pip, Pat and Polly.

Pip is the first pig. He is fat and pink, and he likes food and sleep. He does not like work.

Pat is the second pig. He is thin and brown, and he likes music. He plays a little fiddle all day.

Polly is the third pig. She is small and white, and she likes books and work. She asks a lot of questions.

The house is very small. It has one room, one bed and one table. Every night, the four pigs sleep in the one bed. Pip snores, Pat kicks and Polly falls on the floor.

"Ouch!" says Polly. "This bed is very small."

One morning, Mother Pig sits at the table. She is sad, but she smiles.

"My dear little pigs," she says. "Listen to me. You are big now, and this house is too small for us. I am old and poor, and I do not have much food. You must go out into the world and find your own way."

"Out into the world?" says Pip. "But I like it here. I like my bed."

"Each of you must make a house," says Mother Pig. "A good house. A strong house."

"Why strong?" asks Polly.

Mother Pig looks out of the window at the dark forest on the hill.

"Because the wolf lives in that forest," she says. "He is big and grey, and he has long teeth. He is always hungry, and he loves little pigs."

"Does he love little pigs like a friend?" asks Pip.

"No," says Mother Pig. "He loves little pigs like you love apple pie."

Pip opens his eyes very wide. Pat stops his music, and Polly holds her mother's hand.

"Do not open your door to the wolf," says Mother Pig. "Never, not for any reason. Do you understand?"

"Yes, Mother," say the three little pigs.

Mother Pig gives each pig a small bag. In each bag there is some bread, an apple and three coins.

"Use your coins well," she says.

Then she kisses Pip, Pat and Polly. She stands at the door and waves.

"Goodbye, my dear little pigs! Be good, be clever and be careful!"

"Goodbye, Mother!" say the three little pigs.

They walk down the road and look back at the little house. Their mother is very small now, but she is still at the door. She is still waving.

Chapter 2: A House of Straw

The three little pigs walk and walk. The sun is hot, and the road is long and dusty.

After an hour, Pip sits down at the side of the road.

He eats his bread and his apple, and then he lies down in the grass and closes his eyes.

Just then, a man comes down the road with a big cart. On the cart there is a lot of straw. The straw is yellow and dry and soft.

Pip opens one eye. Then he opens two eyes.

"Hello, sir!" he says. "Is that straw?"

"Yes, it is," says the man. "It is good straw for cows and horses. They sleep on it."

"Straw is soft and light," says Pip. "Can I make a house with straw?"

The man scratches his head. "A house of straw? Maybe. But it is not very strong."

"It is very fast," says Pip. "And I do not want to work all week. Please, sir, give me some straw. I can pay you." He gives the man one coin.

The man smiles. "One coin? Then take this straw. Take it all."

Polly pulls her brother's ear. "Pip, think about it! Mother says a strong house, and straw is not strong. The wind can blow it away."

"The wind is my friend," says Pip. "And look at the sun. It is a beautiful day."

Pat laughs and plays a happy song on his fiddle.

So Pip makes a house of straw. He puts straw on the ground, on the walls and on the roof. He makes a little door of straw and a little window of straw. It takes one hour, only one hour!

"Look!" says Pip. "My house is ready. It is yellow and soft, and it smells like summer."

The house moves a little in the wind. A bird lands on the roof, and some straw falls off.

"Hmm," says Polly.

"Do not say hmm," says Pip. "Say well done, Pip."

"Well done, Pip," says Polly. "But please be careful, and lock your door."

"A door of straw does not have a lock," says Pip. "But do not worry. The wolf is far away on the other side of the hill."

Pip goes into his house and lies down on the soft straw floor. He puts some straw on his tummy like a blanket.

Pat and Polly wave goodbye and walk down the road again. Polly looks back two times. In the window of the little yellow house, Pip waves his foot.

Chapter 3: Little Pig, Little Pig

That evening, the sun goes down and the sky is red and then grey. Pip is in his house of straw. Then he hears something outside. Step, step, step.

Pip looks out of his straw window. There, on the road, is a big grey animal with yellow eyes. It has a long nose and very long teeth.

It is the wolf.

The wolf sniffs the air and smiles a big smile.

"Mmm," he says. "I smell a pig. A fat, pink pig is my favourite dinner."

The wolf walks to the straw door and knocks. Knock, knock. Some straw falls off.

"Little pig, little pig," says the wolf in a sweet voice. "Let me come in."

Pip shakes, and his legs are like jelly. But he remembers his mother's words. Do not open the door to the wolf, never.

"No, no!" says Pip. "Not by the hair on my chinny chin chin!"

The wolf stops smiling, and his yellow eyes are very small now.

"Then I huff, and I puff, and I blow your house in!" he says.

The wolf takes a big breath, and his chest is very big. He huffs and he puffs.

WHOOSH!

The straw flies up into the air. The walls, the roof and the little door fly away. The straw falls on the road like yellow snow.

And there is Pip, alone on the ground with only his little bag.

"Oh dear," says Pip.

"Good evening," says the wolf. He opens his big mouth. Gulp!

And that is the end of Pip.

The wolf licks his lips. "Very nice," he says. "Fat and soft. But I am still hungry, and where there is one little pig, there are always more."

He sniffs the road and smells two more pigs.

Far away, Pat and Polly meet a man with a cart of sticks. The sticks are brown and long and thin.

"Good evening, sir," says Pat. "What a lot of sticks! Are they for a fire?"

"Sticks are stronger than straw," says Pat to Polly. "And they are easy. I want a house with time for music."

Pat gives the man one coin, and the man gives Pat all the sticks.

Chapter 4: A House of Sticks

Pat works fast. He puts long sticks in the ground and ties them with grass. He puts short sticks on the roof and leaves on top. Then he makes a little door of sticks. It takes one afternoon.

"There!" says Pat. "My house is ready. It is brown like me, and it is very pretty. And it is stronger than a house of straw."

Polly walks around the house and pushes one wall with her hand. The wall moves. Creak, creak.

"Hmm," says Polly.

"Oh, Polly," says Pat. "Birds make houses of sticks."

"Birds can fly," says Polly. "We cannot fly."

"Goodnight, Pat," says Polly. "I want my own house, a strong house. Please lock your door."

"I do not have a lock," says Pat. "But I have my fiddle." He smiles, but Polly does not smile. She walks on down the road.

Pat goes into his house and plays his fiddle. Then he hears something outside. Step, step, step.

He stops playing and looks out between the sticks. There are two yellow eyes in the dark.

Knock, knock.

"Little pig, little pig," says a sweet voice. "Let me come in."

Pat holds his fiddle very tight. "No, no!" he says. "Not by the hair on my chinny chin chin!"

"Then I huff, and I puff, and I blow your house in!"

The wolf takes a big breath, and he huffs and he puffs. The sticks shake and creak, but the house does not fall.

The wolf is angry. He takes a bigger breath, and he huffs and he puffs and he HUFFS and he PUFFS.

CRASH!

The sticks fly in all directions, and the leaves fly up to the moon. The little door flies into a tree.

And there is Pat, alone in the dark with his fiddle.

"Can I play you a song?" asks Pat in a very small voice.

"No, thank you," says the wolf. "I do not like music. I like dinner." He opens his big mouth. Gulp!

And that is the end of Pat. The wolf picks up the little fiddle, looks at it and throws it into the grass.

"Two little pigs," he says. "And I know there is a third, a small white one. Tomorrow, she is my breakfast."

Chapter 5: A House of Bricks

Polly does not know about her brothers. In the morning, she sees a man with a big cart of bricks. The bricks are red and hard and heavy.

"Good morning, sir," says Polly. "Those are beautiful bricks. Please, sir, I want to make a house of bricks. How much are they?"

"For a house? A lot of bricks is a lot of money."

Polly opens her bag and gives the man all her three coins.

The man looks at the coins and at the small white pig. He smiles a kind smile.

"Three coins is not much money," he says. "But you are a clever little pig. Here, take the bricks. And take this bucket of cement and this trowel, too."

"Thank you, sir! Thank you!" says Polly.

Then the man shows her the work. "One brick, then some cement, then one more brick," he says. "Always straight and always slow. A fast house is a weak house."

Polly works and works. She carries the heavy bricks and mixes the grey cement. She puts one brick on top of another. Her back and her feet hurt, but she does not stop.

On the first day, she makes the floor. On the second day, she makes the walls. On the third day, she makes a strong roof. On the fourth day, she makes a big chimney and a fireplace. On the fifth day, she makes a thick wooden door with a big iron lock.

At last, the house is ready. It is red and square and solid. Polly pushes a wall, and it does not move. She kicks a wall, and it does not move. She jumps on the roof, and it does not move.

"Now this is a strong house," says Polly.

Inside, she has a little table, a chair and a big black pot. She puts the pot on the fire and makes turnip soup. It smells very good.

That evening, she hears something outside. Step, step, step.

Polly puts down her spoon and looks out of the window. She sees a big grey animal with yellow eyes.

Knock, knock.

"Little pig, little pig," says the wolf. "Let me come in."

"No, no!" says Polly. "Not by the hair on my chinny chin chin!"

"Then I huff, and I puff, and I blow your house in!"

The wolf huffs and puffs, but nothing happens. He huffs and puffs again, and nothing happens. He HUFFS and he PUFFS until his face is red. The house does not move, not one brick.

The wolf sits down. He is very tired, and he has no breath.

"Hmm," says the wolf.

Chapter 6: The Turnip Field

The wolf thinks. He is not strong enough for this house, but maybe he is clever enough for this pig.

He stands up, smiles his sweet smile and knocks on the door again very softly.

"Little pig," he says. "You have a very fine house, and I want to be your friend. Do you like turnips?"

"Yes," says Polly. "I love turnips. I have turnip soup tonight."

"Then I have good news," says the wolf. "I know a field with big, sweet turnips. It is Mr Smith's field, down the road. Come with me tomorrow, and we can get some turnips together."

Polly thinks for a moment. "What time?" she asks.

"At six o'clock," says the wolf. "I come here at six o'clock, and we go together."

"Very well," says Polly. "Six o'clock."

The wolf goes away very happy and licks his lips. "Tomorrow at six o'clock," he says. "Turnips for her, and pig for me."

But Polly is a clever little pig. She does not go to sleep. She thinks.

The next day, she gets up at five o'clock. The sky is still dark, and the birds are still asleep. She takes a big basket, opens the door very quietly and runs to Mr Smith's field.

The turnips are big and white and purple. Polly pulls one, then two, then ten, and her basket is full. Then she runs home very fast. She closes the big wooden door and locks the big iron lock.

At six o'clock, the wolf comes. Knock, knock.

"Little pig! Are you ready? It is six o'clock, time for turnips!"

"Ready?" says Polly through the window. "Oh, Mr Wolf, I am sorry. I go to the field at five o'clock, and now I have a full basket of turnips. They are in my pot for lunch. Thank you for the good idea."

The wolf is very, very angry. His face and his ears are red, and he jumps up and down. But he does not shout. He smiles his sweet smile again.

"What a clever little pig you are," he says. "Well, well. Do you like apples?"

"Yes," says Polly. "I love apples."

"I know a big apple tree in the Merry Garden, near the river," says the wolf. "The apples are red and sweet. Come with me tomorrow. I come here at five o'clock, and this time, please wait for me."

"Five o'clock," says Polly. "Very well."

The wolf goes away, and Polly watches him. She laughs a little, but then she stops. Where are Pip and Pat? Why does the wolf come here, and not to them?

She looks down the road. There is yellow straw on the road, and there are brown sticks, too.

A big tear falls from her eye. "Oh, Pip," she says. "Oh, Pat."

"I am alone now," she says. "So I must be very clever."

Chapter 7: The Apple Tree and the Fair

The next day, Polly gets up at four o'clock and runs to the Merry Garden. It is far away, near the river, and the apple tree is very big and tall.

Polly climbs the tree, but it is not easy, because pigs are not good at climbing. She picks ten apples and puts them in her basket.

Then she looks down. Oh no! The wolf stands under the tree and looks up at her. He smiles with all his long teeth.

"Good morning, little pig," he says. "You are early again. But this time, I am early too."

Polly holds the branch very tight, and her heart goes boom, boom, boom. But she does not cry. She thinks.

"Good morning, Mr Wolf," she says. "These apples are wonderful. Do you want one?"

"Yes, please," says the wolf.

"Here! Catch!" says Polly. She throws an apple very far, and it rolls down the hill, far, far away.

The wolf runs after the apple. Polly jumps down from the tree and runs home with her basket. She closes the big wooden door and locks the big iron lock.

The wolf comes back with the apple in his mouth. He is very angry, and he knocks on the door.

"Little pig!" he says. "That is not nice."

"Wolves are not nice," says Polly. "Enjoy your apple."

Then he smiles his sweet smile one more time.

"Little pig," he says. "There is a big fair in the town tomorrow, with music and food and dancing. Do you want to come? I come here at three o'clock."

"Three o'clock," says Polly. "Very well."

The next day, Polly goes to the fair at one o'clock.

Polly buys a big wooden butter churn. It is round like a barrel, and she can make butter in it.

She walks home with her churn and comes to the top of a hill. Then she sees the wolf. He comes up the hill towards her!

Polly has no place to run, so she jumps into the butter churn. The churn falls over and rolls down the hill, faster and faster. It makes a lot of noise. BUMP! BUMP! BUMP!

The wolf sees a big wooden thing with a terrible noise. It rolls towards him, and he is very frightened. He jumps into the air and runs home as fast as he can.

The churn stops at the bottom of the hill. Polly climbs out, and she is dizzy. She laughs and laughs, and then she carries her churn home.

That evening, the wolf comes to the brick house. He is pale and sad.

"Little pig," he says. "I do not go to the fair today. A great round monster comes down the hill, and it runs after me."

Polly laughs. "That is not a monster, Mr Wolf. That is me in my butter churn!"

Chapter 8: Down the Chimney

The wolf stops. His mouth is open, and his eyes are very wide.

Then he is very, very, very angry. He jumps up and down and bites the grass. He kicks the bricks. Ouch! The bricks are hard.

"Little pig!" he shouts. "No more tricks, no more turnips, no more apples and no more fairs! I want you, and I want you now!"

"You cannot come in," says Polly. "My door, my lock and my walls are strong."

The wolf walks around the house one time, two times, three times. Then he looks up at the roof and the big chimney.

"Ha!" says the wolf. "Your chimney is open. I come down your chimney, little pig, and then I eat you up!"

The wolf climbs a tree near the house and jumps onto the roof. His claws make a noise on the roof. Scratch, scratch, scratch.

Inside, Polly hears the noise. She looks at the fireplace and at her big black pot. She does not cry and she does not run. She thinks very fast.

She fills the big black pot with water, bucket after bucket. She puts a lot of wood on the fire, and the fire is big and hot. Soon the water bubbles. Blub, blub, blub.

On the roof, the wolf looks down the chimney. It is dark, and he cannot see the pot.

"Here I come, little pig!" he shouts.

He jumps into the chimney, and down, down, down he goes. Then, SPLASH! He falls into the pot of hot water.

Quick as a flash, Polly puts the big lid on the pot and sits on it. The pot shakes and jumps. But Polly is heavy enough, and the lid is strong.

After a while, the pot is quiet. And that is the end of the wolf.

That night, Polly eats wolf soup for supper. It is not her favourite soup, but she is very hungry.

The next week, Polly walks back down the road to the little house near the green hill. Her mother is at the door.

"Polly! My dear little pig!" says Mother Pig. "Where are your brothers?"

Polly holds her mother's hand and tells her about the house of straw and the house of sticks. Mother Pig cries, and Polly cries too. Then Polly tells her about the turnips, the apples and the butter churn, and Mother Pig laughs.

"Come and live with me, Mother," says Polly. "My house has a strong door and a strong lock, and there is space for two."

So Mother Pig walks down the road with Polly to the house of bricks.

"What a fine house," says Mother Pig. "A good house. A strong house."

"It is a house with no wolf," says Polly.

Every evening, they sit by the fire and eat turnip soup and apple pie. Sometimes Polly takes an old fiddle from the grass and plays a little song for her brothers.

And Polly and her mother live happily ever after in the little house of bricks.

The End
