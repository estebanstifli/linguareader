Chapter 1: A Single Man in Possession of a Good Fortune

It is a truth universally acknowledged, that a single man in possession of a good fortune must be in want of a wife.

Nobody in the village of Longbourn believed this more firmly than Mrs Bennet. She had five daughters, no sons and very little patience, and she had spent the last twenty years looking for husbands for all of them.

"My dear Mr Bennet," she said one morning, as soon as she came into the room, "have you heard that Netherfield Park is let at last?"

Mr Bennet was reading. He did not look up from his book. "I have not."

"But it is! Mrs Long has just been here, and she told me everything."

Mr Bennet turned a page and said nothing.

"Do you not want to know who has taken it?" cried his wife impatiently.

"You want to tell me, and I have no objection to hearing it."

That was invitation enough. "Well, my dear, Mrs Long says that Netherfield is taken by a young man of large fortune from the north of England. His name is Bingley. He came down on Monday in a carriage and four to see the place, and he was so delighted with it that he agreed at once. His servants will be in the house by the end of next week."

"Is he married or single?"

"Oh, single, my dear, certainly! A single man of large fortune, four or five thousand a year. What a fine thing for our girls!"

"How so? How can it affect them?"

"My dear Mr Bennet," replied his wife, "how can you be so tiresome? You must know that I am thinking of his marrying one of them."

"Is that his reason for settling here?"

"Reason! Nonsense, how can you talk so! But it is very likely that he may fall in love with one of them, and so you must visit him as soon as he comes."

Mr Bennet had been married for more than twenty years, and he had discovered early that his wife was a woman of mean understanding, little information and uncertain temper. When she was unhappy, she imagined herself nervous. The business of her life was to get her daughters married; its comfort was visiting and news. Her husband had long ago stopped trying to change her. Instead, he found his pleasure in teasing her, and she never quite understood when he was doing it.

"I see no reason for it," he said. "You and the girls may go, or you may send them by themselves, which perhaps will be still better. You are as handsome as any of them, and Mr Bingley might like you the best of the party."

"My dear, you flatter me. But consider your daughters. Sir William and Lady Lucas are determined to go, only for that reason. You know that they never visit newcomers. Indeed, you must go, for it will be impossible for us to visit him if you do not."

"You are over-careful, surely. I dare say Mr Bingley will be very glad to see you, and I will send a few lines with you to tell him that he may marry whichever of the girls he chooses. Though I must put in a good word for my little Lizzy."

"I desire you will do no such thing. Lizzy is not a bit better than the others. She is not half so handsome as Jane, nor half so good-humoured as Lydia. But you are always giving her the preference."

"They have none of them much to recommend them," replied he. "They are all silly and ignorant, like other girls. But Lizzy has something more of quickness than her sisters."

"Mr Bennet, how can you abuse your own children in such a way? You take delight in annoying me. You have no compassion for my poor nerves."

"You mistake me, my dear. I have a high respect for your nerves. They are my old friends. I have heard you mention them with consideration these twenty years at least."

The five Miss Bennets were very different from one another. Jane, the eldest, was twenty-two and the beauty of the family. She was gentle and kind, and she could never believe that anyone in the world was truly bad. Elizabeth, who was twenty, was clever and lively, with fine dark eyes and a habit of laughing at anything foolish. She was her father's favourite, and she knew it.

Mary was the plain one. She read serious books, copied out long moral sentences from them, and practised the piano for hours without ever learning to play it well. Kitty and Lydia, the youngest, were seventeen and fifteen, and they thought of nothing but officers, ribbons and dancing. Lydia was tall, loud and full of high spirits. She was her mother's favourite, and Kitty followed her everywhere.

In spite of everything he had said, Mr Bennet was among the first to visit Mr Bingley. He had always intended to go, and he went. But he told his wife nothing until the evening of the visit, when he watched his second daughter decorating a hat.

"I hope Mr Bingley will like it, Lizzy," he said.

"We are not in a way to know what Mr Bingley likes," said her mother angrily, "since we are not to visit."

"But you forget, Mamma," said Elizabeth, "that we shall meet him at the assemblies, and Mrs Long has promised to introduce him."

"I do not believe Mrs Long will do any such thing. She has two nieces of her own. She is a selfish, false woman, and I have no opinion of her."

"I am sorry to hear that," said Mr Bennet calmly. "If I had known as much this morning, I certainly would not have called on him. It is very unlucky. But as I have actually paid the visit, we cannot escape the acquaintance now."

The astonishment of the ladies was just what he wished. Mrs Bennet was the loudest of all, but when the first noise of joy was over, she began to declare that it was what she had expected all the time.

"How good it was of you, my dear Mr Bennet! But I knew I should persuade you at last. I was sure you loved your girls too well to neglect such an acquaintance. Well, how pleased I am! And it is such a good joke, too, that you should have gone this morning and never said a word about it till now."

"Now, Kitty, you may cough as much as you choose," said Mr Bennet, and he left the room, tired of the raptures of his wife.

"What an excellent father you have, girls," said Mrs Bennet, when the door was shut. "I do not know how you will ever repay him for his kindness. Lydia, my love, though you are the youngest, I dare say Mr Bingley will dance with you at the next ball."

"Oh," said Lydia stoutly, "I am not afraid. For though I am the youngest, I am the tallest."

For the rest of the evening they talked of nothing but how soon he would return the visit, and when they should ask him to dinner.

Chapter 2: The Assembly at Meryton

Mr Bingley returned the visit a few days later and sat for ten minutes with Mr Bennet in his library. He had hoped to see the young ladies, of whose beauty he had heard a great deal, but he saw only the father. The ladies were luckier. From an upstairs window, they had the pleasure of seeing that he wore a blue coat and rode a black horse.

Soon afterwards, the news spread that he would come to the next assembly at Meryton, the small town a mile from Longbourn, and that he would bring a large party with him. In the end, the party that entered the ballroom was only five people: Mr Bingley, his two sisters, the husband of the eldest, and another young man.

Mr Bingley was good-looking and gentlemanlike. He had a pleasant face and easy, natural manners. His sisters were fine women with an air of decided fashion. But his friend, Mr Darcy, soon drew the attention of the whole room. He was tall, with handsome features and a noble look, and within five minutes everybody knew that he had ten thousand a year.

The gentlemen declared that he was a fine figure of a man. The ladies declared that he was much handsomer than Mr Bingley. He was looked at with great admiration for about half the evening, until his manners gave a disgust which turned the tide of his popularity. He was discovered to be proud, to be above his company, and above being pleased. Not all his large estate in Derbyshire could save him then from having a most disagreeable face.

Mr Bingley, on the other hand, soon became acquainted with all the principal people in the room. He was lively and open. He danced every dance, was angry that the ball closed so early, and talked of giving one himself at Netherfield. Such amiable qualities must speak for themselves. What a contrast between him and his friend! Mr Darcy danced only once with Mrs Hurst and once with Miss Bingley. He refused to be introduced to any other lady, and spent the rest of the evening walking about the room, speaking occasionally to one of his own party.

Because there were more ladies than gentlemen, Elizabeth Bennet had to sit down for two dances. During part of that time, Mr Darcy was standing near enough for her to hear a conversation between him and Mr Bingley, who came from the dance to press his friend to join it.

"Come, Darcy," said he, "I must have you dance. I hate to see you standing about by yourself in this stupid manner. You had much better dance."

"I certainly shall not. You know how I detest it, unless I am particularly acquainted with my partner. At such an assembly as this, it would be insupportable. Your sisters are engaged, and there is not another woman in the room whom it would not be a punishment to me to stand up with."

"I would not be so fussy as you are," cried Bingley, "for a kingdom! Upon my honour, I never met with so many pleasant girls in my life as I have this evening. Several of them are uncommonly pretty."

"You are dancing with the only handsome girl in the room," said Mr Darcy, looking at the eldest Miss Bennet.

"Oh, she is the most beautiful creature I ever saw! But there is one of her sisters sitting down just behind you, who is very pretty, and I dare say very agreeable. Do let me ask my partner to introduce you."

"Which do you mean?" Darcy turned round and looked for a moment at Elizabeth, until, catching her eye, he looked away and coldly said, "She is tolerable, but not handsome enough to tempt me. I am in no humour at present to give importance to young ladies who are slighted by other men. You had better return to your partner and enjoy her smiles, for you are wasting your time with me."

Mr Bingley followed his advice. Mr Darcy walked off, and Elizabeth remained with no very warm feelings towards him. She told the story, however, with great spirit among her friends. She had a lively, playful character, which delighted in anything ridiculous, and before the evening was over, half the room had heard how the proud Mr Darcy had found her merely tolerable.

The evening passed off pleasantly for the whole family. Mrs Bennet had seen her eldest daughter much admired by the Netherfield party. Mr Bingley had danced with Jane twice, and his sisters had paid her attention. Jane was as pleased by this as her mother could be, though in a quieter way. Mary had heard herself described to Miss Bingley as the most accomplished girl in the neighbourhood, and Kitty and Lydia had been fortunate enough never to be without partners, which was all that they had yet learnt to care for at a ball.

When Jane and Elizabeth were alone that night in their room, Jane, who had been careful in her praise of Mr Bingley before, at last told her sister how very much she admired him.

"He is just what a young man ought to be," said she. "Sensible, good-humoured, lively. And I never saw such happy manners! So much ease, with such perfect good breeding."

"He is also handsome," replied Elizabeth, "which a young man ought likewise to be, if he possibly can. His character is thereby complete."

"I was very much flattered by his asking me to dance a second time. I did not expect such a compliment."

"Did not you? I did for you. But that is one great difference between us. Compliments always take you by surprise, and me never. What could be more natural than his asking you again? He could not help seeing that you were about five times as pretty as every other woman in the room. Well, he certainly is very agreeable, and I give you leave to like him. You have liked many a stupider person."

"Dear Lizzy!"

"Oh, you are a great deal too apt to like people in general. You never see a fault in anybody. All the world are good and agreeable in your eyes. I never heard you speak ill of a human being in my life."

"I would not wish to be hasty in censuring anyone. But I always speak what I think."

"I know you do, and it is that which makes the wonder. With your good sense, to be so honestly blind to the follies and nonsense of others! And so you like his sisters too, do you? Their manners are not equal to his."

"Certainly not at first. But they are very pleasing women when you talk to them. Miss Bingley is to live with her brother and keep his house, and I am much mistaken if we do not find her a very charming neighbour."

Elizabeth listened in silence, but she was not convinced. The sisters had been polite to Jane because their brother admired her. To everybody else, they had been proud, cold and quick to laugh. She thought them fine ladies who were very pleased with themselves, and she suspected that they would not be so charming once their brother was out of the room.

The next day, the Lucases came to Longbourn to talk over the ball. Sir William Lucas had once been a tradesman in Meryton and had been made a knight after a speech to the King. Ever since, he had been too important to work and too happy to be anything but polite to everybody. His eldest daughter, Charlotte, was a sensible young woman of about twenty-seven, and Elizabeth's closest friend.

"Mr Darcy is not so well worth listening to as his friend, is he?" said Charlotte. "Poor Eliza! To be only just tolerable."

"I beg you would not put it into Lizzy's head to be vexed by his ill treatment," said Mrs Bennet, "for he is such a disagreeable man that it would be quite a misfortune to be liked by him. Mrs Long told me last night that he sat close to her for half an hour without once opening his lips."

"His pride," said Charlotte, "does not offend me so much as pride often does, because there is an excuse for it. One cannot wonder that so very fine a young man, with family, fortune, everything in his favour, should think highly of himself. If I may so express it, he has a right to be proud."

"That is very true," replied Elizabeth, "and I could easily forgive his pride, if he had not mortified mine."

Chapter 3: Three Miles Through the Mud

The ladies of Longbourn soon visited the ladies of Netherfield, and the visit was returned. Jane's pleasing manners grew on Miss Bingley and Mrs Hurst, and Mr Bingley admired her more each time they met. Elizabeth could see that Jane was falling in love, but she could also see that Jane hid her feelings so well that hardly anybody else would notice.

"It may perhaps be pleasant," said Charlotte, when Elizabeth mentioned this, "to be able to hide one's feelings from the world. But if a woman hides her affection too well from the man she likes, she may lose the opportunity of fixing him. In nine cases out of ten, a woman had better show more affection than she feels. Bingley likes your sister, without doubt. But he may never do more than like her, if she does not help him on."

"But she does help him on, as much as her nature will allow."

"Happiness in marriage is entirely a matter of chance," said Charlotte. "It is better to know as little as possible of the faults of the person with whom you are to pass your life."

"You make me laugh, Charlotte. But it is not sound. You know it is not sound, and that you would never act in this way yourself."

Meanwhile, Elizabeth did not notice that she herself was becoming an object of some interest to Mr Darcy. He had at first scarcely allowed her to be pretty. But no sooner had he told his friends that she had hardly a good feature in her face than he began to find it was rendered uncommonly intelligent by the beautiful expression of her dark eyes. He found her figure light and pleasing, and her manners caught him with their easy playfulness. Of this she was perfectly unaware. To her, he was only the man who made himself agreeable nowhere, and who had not thought her handsome enough to dance with.

One morning a note arrived for Jane from Netherfield. Miss Bingley invited her to dine with the two sisters, as the gentlemen were dining with the officers.

"Can I have the carriage?" asked Jane.

"No, my dear, you had better go on horseback," said Mrs Bennet, "because it seems likely to rain, and then you must stay all night."

Mrs Bennet's plan worked better than she had hoped. Jane had not been gone long before it rained hard, and it rained all evening without a pause. Jane could not come home. The next morning, a note arrived to say that she had caught a bad cold and was too unwell to leave Netherfield.

"Well, my dear," said Mr Bennet to his wife, "if your daughter should have a dangerous illness, if she should die, it would be a comfort to know that it was all in pursuit of Mr Bingley, and under your orders."

"Oh! I am not at all afraid of her dying. People do not die of little trifling colds."

Elizabeth, however, was anxious, and she was determined to go to her sister. The carriage was not available, and she was no horsewoman, so walking was her only choice.

"How can you be so silly," cried her mother, "in all this dirt! You will not be fit to be seen when you get there."

"I shall be very fit to see Jane, which is all I want."

She set off alone, crossing field after field at a quick pace, jumping over stiles and springing over puddles. At last she found herself in view of the house, with weary ankles, dirty stockings, and a face glowing with the warmth of exercise.

She was shown into the breakfast room, where all but Jane were assembled, and her appearance created a great deal of surprise. That she should have walked three miles so early in the day, in such dirty weather, and by herself, was almost incredible to Mrs Hurst and Miss Bingley. Elizabeth was convinced that they despised her for it. She was received, however, very politely by them. In their brother's manners there was something better than politeness: there was good humour and kindness. Mr Darcy said very little. He was divided between admiration of the brilliancy which exercise had given to her complexion and doubt whether the occasion justified her coming so far alone.

Jane was really ill, and Elizabeth stayed with her almost all day. When she left the room at last, the sisters began to abuse her.

"Her manners are very bad indeed," said Miss Bingley, "a mixture of pride and impertinence. She has no conversation, no style, no beauty. And her hair, so untidy!"

"Yes, and her petticoat," added Mrs Hurst. "I hope you saw her petticoat, six inches deep in mud."

"I am afraid, Mr Darcy," said Miss Bingley in a half whisper, "that this adventure has rather affected your admiration of her fine eyes."

"Not at all," he replied. "They were brightened by the exercise."

A short pause followed this speech.

Because Jane's cold was no better, Elizabeth was invited to stay at Netherfield too. In the evenings she joined the party in the drawing room. Miss Bingley spent most of these evenings watching Mr Darcy, praising his handwriting, and asking about his young sister, Georgiana. Mr Darcy wrote letters and answered as little as possible.

One evening, the talk turned to the accomplishments of young ladies.

"It is amazing to me," said Bingley, "how young ladies can have patience to be so very accomplished as they all are. They all paint tables, cover screens and make purses. I scarcely know anyone who cannot do all this."

"I cannot boast of knowing more than half a dozen, in the whole range of my acquaintance, that are really accomplished," said Darcy.

"Nor I, I am sure," said Miss Bingley quickly. "A woman must have a thorough knowledge of music, singing, drawing, dancing and the modern languages. And besides all this, she must possess a certain something in her air and manner of walking, in the tone of her voice, her address and expressions."

"All this she must possess," added Darcy, "and to all this she must yet add something more substantial, in the improvement of her mind by extensive reading."

"I am no longer surprised at your knowing only six accomplished women," said Elizabeth. "I rather wonder now at your knowing any."

Later, Miss Bingley got up and walked about the room. Her figure was elegant, and she walked well, but Mr Darcy, at whom it was all aimed, did not look up from his book. In despair, she invited Elizabeth to walk with her. Mr Darcy looked up at once.

"Mr Darcy is not to be laughed at!" cried Elizabeth, when he refused to join them. "That is an uncommon advantage, and uncommon I hope it will continue, for it would be a great loss to me to have many such acquaintances. I dearly love a laugh."

"The wisest and the best of men," said he, "may be rendered ridiculous by a person whose first object in life is a joke."

"Certainly there are such people, but I hope I am not one of them. I hope I never ridicule what is wise or good. Follies and nonsense do divert me, I own, and I laugh at them whenever I can. But these, I suppose, are precisely what you are without."

"I have faults enough, but they are not, I hope, of understanding. My temper I dare not vouch for. My good opinion once lost is lost for ever."

"That is a failing indeed!" cried Elizabeth. "Implacable resentment is a shade in a character. But you have chosen your fault well. I really cannot laugh at it."

"There is, I believe, in every disposition a tendency to some particular evil."

"And your defect is a propensity to hate everybody."

"And yours," he replied with a smile, "is wilfully to misunderstand them."

Mr Darcy began to feel the danger of paying Elizabeth too much attention. He resolved to be careful, and during the last day of her visit he scarcely spoke ten words to her. On Sunday, when Jane was well enough to travel, the sisters went home at last. Miss Bingley was delighted to see them go. Mr Bingley was sorry. Mr Darcy was relieved, and perhaps a little disappointed.

Chapter 4: Mr Collins

"I hope, my dear," said Mr Bennet to his wife, as they were at breakfast the next morning, "that you have ordered a good dinner today, because I have reason to expect an addition to our family party."

"Who do you mean, my dear? I know of nobody that is coming, I am sure, unless Charlotte Lucas should happen to call."

"The person of whom I speak is a gentleman and a stranger."

Mrs Bennet's eyes sparkled. "A gentleman and a stranger! It is Mr Bingley, I am sure!"

"No, it is a person whom I never saw in the whole course of my life. About a month ago I received a letter from my cousin, Mr Collins, who, when I am dead, may turn you all out of this house as soon as he pleases."

This needed some explanation. Longbourn was entailed: by an old family arrangement, it could only pass to a male heir. Since the Bennets had no son, the house and land would go after Mr Bennet's death to his nearest male relation, a distant cousin whom nobody had met. Mrs Bennet had never been able to understand the entail, and she complained of it bitterly whenever she was reminded.

"It is the hardest thing in the world," she cried, "that your estate should be taken away from your own children! I am sure, if I had been you, I should have tried long ago to do something about it."

Mr Bennet read them the letter. Mr Collins was a clergyman. He had recently been given a parish at Hunsford in Kent by the Right Honourable Lady Catherine de Bourgh, whose kindness he mentioned three times in a single page. He was sorry about the entail, he wished to heal the old quarrel between their two families, and he hoped to make amends to the daughters. He would arrive on Monday at four o'clock and stay until the Saturday week.

"He must be an oddity, I think," said Elizabeth. "There is something very pompous in his style. Can he be a sensible man, sir?"

"No, my dear, I think not. I have great hopes of finding him quite the reverse. There is a mixture of servility and self-importance in his letter which promises well. I am impatient to see him."

Mr Collins was punctual to his time. He was a tall, heavy-looking young man of twenty-five, serious and stately, with very formal manners. He had not been seated long before he complimented Mrs Bennet on having so fine a family of daughters. He had heard much of their beauty, he said, but in this instance fame had fallen short of the truth. He did not doubt that she would see them all well married in time. He praised the hall, the dining room and all its furniture. At dinner he praised the potatoes, and asked which of his fair cousins had cooked them. Mrs Bennet told him, rather sharply, that they kept a cook.

During dinner, Mr Bennet hardly spoke. But when the servants had gone, he turned the conversation to Lady Catherine, and Mr Collins was delighted.

"Lady Catherine has been most kind to me," he said. "She has asked me twice to dine at Rosings, and only last Saturday she sent for me to make up her pool of quadrille in the evening. She has even condescended to advise me to marry as soon as I can, provided I choose with discretion."

"You judge very properly," said Mr Bennet, "and it is happy for you that you possess the talent of flattering with delicacy. May I ask whether these pleasing attentions come from the impulse of the moment, or are the result of previous study?"

"They arise chiefly from what is passing at the time," said Mr Collins seriously, "and though I sometimes amuse myself with arranging such little elegant compliments, I always wish to give them as unstudied an air as possible."

Mr Bennet's expectations were fully answered. His cousin was as absurd as he had hoped. He listened to him with the keenest enjoyment, keeping a perfectly serious face, and only occasionally sharing a glance with Elizabeth.

After tea, Mr Collins was asked to read aloud to the ladies. He chose a book of sermons. Lydia stared at him, and before he had read three pages, she interrupted him to tell her mother that her uncle Phillips had heard of a new officer in town.

The truth was that Mr Collins had come to Longbourn with a plan. He had a good house and a sufficient income, and Lady Catherine had told him to find a wife. He intended to choose one of his cousins. This was his idea of making amends for the entail, and he thought it excessively generous. His first choice was Jane, for her beauty. But a quiet word from Mrs Bennet the next morning told him that her eldest daughter was likely to be very soon engaged. Mr Collins had only to change from Jane to Elizabeth, and it was soon done, while Mrs Bennet was stirring the fire. Elizabeth, next to Jane in birth and beauty, succeeded her of course.

Mr Bennet, desperate to be left alone in his library, persuaded Mr Collins to walk to Meryton with the girls. Lydia and Kitty's eyes searched the street for officers, for a regiment of the militia had been sent to spend the winter in the town, and since then the youngest Bennets had talked of nothing else.

Their eyes were soon caught by a young man whom they had never seen before, walking with an officer they knew. The officer was Mr Denny, and he introduced his friend, Mr Wickham, who had just accepted a commission in their regiment. This was exactly as it should be, for the young man wanted only a uniform to make him completely charming. His appearance was greatly in his favour. He had all the best part of beauty, a fine face, a good figure and very pleasing manners. He spoke easily and with perfect correctness, and the whole party was enjoying his conversation when they heard the sound of horses.

Darcy and Bingley were seen riding down the street. On seeing the ladies, the two gentlemen came towards them. Bingley was the principal speaker, and Jane the principal object. He was then, he said, on his way to Longbourn on purpose to ask after her health. Mr Darcy bowed and was beginning to decide not to look at Elizabeth, when his eyes were suddenly caught by the stranger.

Elizabeth happened to see the faces of both as they looked at each other, and she was astonished at the effect of the meeting. Both changed colour. One looked white, the other red. After a few moments Mr Wickham touched his hat, a greeting which Mr Darcy just deigned to return. What could be the meaning of it? It was impossible to imagine, and it was impossible not to long to know.

In another minute, Mr Bingley, who did not seem to have noticed anything, said goodbye and rode on with his friend.

Chapter 5: Mr Wickham's Story and the Netherfield Ball

The next evening, the Bennet girls and Mr Collins dined with their aunt, Mrs Phillips, in Meryton. Several officers had been invited, and to the delight of everyone, Mr Wickham was among them. When he came into the room, Elizabeth felt that she had not been thinking of him with unreasonable admiration. He was far beyond all the other officers in face, air and walk, and he was the happy man towards whom almost every female eye was turned. He chose to sit down next to Elizabeth.

Mr Collins played cards at another table and lost every game, but he assured everyone that it did not matter, as Lady Catherine had placed him far above such small concerns. Lydia talked so loudly that nobody could hear anything else. Under the cover of her noise, Mr Wickham began to speak quietly to Elizabeth.

He asked, in a hesitating manner, how far Netherfield was from Meryton, and how long Mr Darcy had been staying there.

"About a month," said Elizabeth. And then, unwilling to let the subject drop, she added, "He is a man of very large property in Derbyshire, I understand."

"Yes," replied Wickham. "His estate there is a noble one. You could not have met with a person more capable of giving you information on that head than myself. I have been connected with his family in a particular manner from my infancy."

Elizabeth could not help looking surprised.

"You may well be surprised, Miss Bennet, after seeing the very cold manner of our meeting yesterday. Are you much acquainted with Mr Darcy?"

"As much as I ever wish to be," cried Elizabeth warmly. "I think him very disagreeable."

Wickham then told her his story. His father had been the steward of the late Mr Darcy, and had managed his estate at Pemberley for many years. The old gentleman had loved Wickham like a son. He had been his godfather, had paid for his education, and had intended him for the Church. In his will, he had left him a valuable living, a position as a clergyman with a good income, which would become free when the old clergyman died.

"But when the living fell vacant two years ago," Wickham said, "it was given to another man."

"Good heavens!" cried Elizabeth. "But how could that be? How could his will be ignored? Why did you not seek legal redress?"

"There was just such an informality in the terms of the bequest as to give me no hope from law. A man of honour could not have doubted the intention, but Mr Darcy chose to doubt it. He claimed that I had given up all right to it by my extravagance and imprudence. In short, anything or nothing. The truth is that we are very different sort of men, and that he hates me."

"This is quite shocking! He deserves to be publicly disgraced."

"Some time or other he will be. But it shall not be by me. Till I can forget his father, I can never defy or expose him."

Elizabeth honoured him for such feelings, and thought him handsomer than ever as he expressed them. "But what can have been his motive? What can have induced him to behave so cruelly?"

"A thorough, determined dislike of me, a dislike which I cannot but attribute in some measure to jealousy. His father liked me too well. His son could not bear it."

Wickham added that Mr Darcy had a sister, Miss Georgiana Darcy, who was very proud too. She had been an affectionate child, he said, but now she thought of nothing but her own importance. He also explained that Lady Catherine de Bourgh was Darcy's aunt, and that her daughter, Miss de Bourgh, would one day have a very large fortune. Everyone expected her to marry her cousin, Mr Darcy.

Elizabeth went home with her head full of him. She could think of nothing but Mr Wickham, and of what he had told her, all the way home.

The next day she told everything to Jane, who listened with astonishment and concern. Jane could not believe that Mr Darcy could be so unworthy of Mr Bingley's friendship. But neither could she believe that a young man of such pleasing appearance as Wickham could be lying. The only thing to do, she decided, was to think well of them both.

"There must be some mistake," she said. "They have both been deceived, I dare say, in some way or other, of which we can form no idea."

"Very true, indeed. And now, my dear Jane, what have you got to say on behalf of the people who have probably been concerned in the business? Do clear them too, or we shall be obliged to think ill of somebody."

Soon afterwards, Mr Bingley and his sisters came in person to invite the family to a ball at Netherfield on the following Tuesday. Elizabeth was full of pleasant expectations. She meant to dance a great deal with Mr Wickham.

But when she entered the drawing room at Netherfield and looked in vain for his red coat among the crowd, a sudden doubt came to her. Mr Denny told Lydia that his friend had been called to London on business, and added with a meaning smile, "I do not imagine his business would have called him away just now, if he had not wished to avoid a certain gentleman here."

Elizabeth was resolved against any sort of conversation with Mr Darcy. Her first two dances were with Mr Collins, and they were dances of mortification. He was awkward and serious, often moved wrong without knowing it, and apologised instead of paying attention. The moment of her release from him was ecstasy.

She was talking to Charlotte when she found herself suddenly addressed by Mr Darcy. He asked for her hand in the next dance, and he took her so much by surprise that, without knowing what she did, she accepted him. He walked away again immediately, and she was left to fret over her own lack of presence of mind.

"I dare say you will find him very agreeable," said Charlotte.

"Heaven forbid! That would be the greatest misfortune of all! To find a man agreeable whom one is determined to hate! Do not wish me such an evil."

They stood for some time in silence. At last Elizabeth said something about the dance. He replied, and was again silent.

"It is your turn to say something now, Mr Darcy. I talked about the dance, and you ought to make some sort of remark on the size of the room, or the number of couples."

He smiled, and assured her that whatever she wished him to say should be said.

"Very well. That reply will do for the present. Perhaps by and by I may observe that private balls are much pleasanter than public ones. But now we may be silent."

"Do you talk by rule, then, while you are dancing?"

"Sometimes. One must speak a little, you know."

Then, when he asked her whether she and her sisters often walked to Meryton, she could not resist. "When you met us there the other day, we had just been forming a new acquaintance."

The effect was immediate. A deeper shade of pride spread over his face. "Mr Wickham is blessed with such happy manners as may ensure his making friends," he said coldly. "Whether he may be equally capable of keeping them is less certain."

"He has been so unlucky as to lose your friendship," replied Elizabeth, "and in a manner which he is likely to suffer from all his life."

Darcy made no answer. They finished the dance in silence and parted, both dissatisfied.

The rest of the evening brought Elizabeth little comfort. Miss Bingley came to tell her, with a look of polite contempt, that Mr Wickham was the son of old Wickham, the late Mr Darcy's steward, and that he had treated Mr Darcy in a most infamous manner. Elizabeth thought it was nothing but jealousy.

Then Mr Collins discovered that Mr Darcy was the nephew of Lady Catherine de Bourgh. He was determined to introduce himself, and Elizabeth could not stop him. She watched with horror as he bowed low before Darcy and began a long speech. Darcy listened with open wonder, replied with cold politeness, and turned away.

At supper, Mrs Bennet sat near Lady Lucas and talked loudly and freely of her expectation that Jane would soon be married to Mr Bingley. It was such a charming match, she said. He was so rich, and he lived only three miles away. Elizabeth tried in vain to stop her. Mr Darcy, sitting opposite, could hear every word.

"What is Mr Darcy to me, pray, that I should be afraid of him?" said her mother. "I am sure we owe him no such particular civility as to be obliged to say nothing he may not like to hear."

After supper, Mary was asked to sing. She sang one song, and then another, in a weak voice and an affected manner. Elizabeth was in agonies. She looked at her father, and at last he understood.

"That will do extremely well, child," he said loudly. "You have delighted us long enough. Let the other young ladies have time to exhibit."

To Elizabeth it seemed that, if her family had made an agreement to expose themselves as much as they could during the evening, they could not have played their parts with more spirit or finer success. Her only comfort was that Bingley had not noticed most of it. He had eyes only for Jane.

Chapter 6: Two Proposals

The next morning, soon after breakfast, Mr Collins asked Mrs Bennet for a private interview with Elizabeth. Mrs Bennet answered instantly, "Oh dear! Yes, certainly. I am sure Lizzy will be very happy. Come, Kitty, I want you upstairs."

"Dear Mamma, do not go. I beg you will not go," cried Elizabeth. "Mr Collins must excuse me. He can have nothing to say to me that anybody need not hear."

"Nonsense, Lizzy. I desire you will stay where you are." And when Elizabeth seemed ready to escape, she added, "Lizzy, I insist upon your staying and hearing Mr Collins."

The door closed. Mr Collins began.

"Believe me, my dear Miss Elizabeth, that your modesty only adds to your other perfections. Almost as soon as I entered the house, I singled you out as the companion of my future life. But before I am run away with by my feelings on this subject, perhaps it would be advisable for me to state my reasons for marrying."

The idea of Mr Collins being run away with by his feelings made Elizabeth so near laughing that she could not use the short pause he allowed in any attempt to stop him further.

"My reasons for marrying are, first, that I think it a right thing for every clergyman to set the example of matrimony in his parish. Secondly, that I am convinced it will add very greatly to my happiness. And thirdly, which perhaps I ought to have mentioned earlier, that it is the particular advice of the very noble lady whom I have the honour of calling patroness. Lady Catherine said to me, Mr Collins, you must marry. Choose properly, choose a gentlewoman for my sake, and for your own, let her be an active, useful sort of person."

He went on to explain that, as he was to inherit Longbourn, he had chosen a wife from among the daughters so that their loss would be as small as possible. He knew that she would have only a thousand pounds when her mother died, but he would never reproach her with it once they were married.

"You are too hasty, sir," cried Elizabeth. "You forget that I have made no answer. Accept my thanks for the compliment you are paying me. I am very sensible of the honour of your proposal, but it is impossible for me to do otherwise than decline it."

"I am not now to learn," replied Mr Collins, with a formal wave of the hand, "that it is usual with young ladies to reject the addresses of the man whom they secretly mean to accept, when he first applies for their favour. Sometimes the refusal is repeated a second or even a third time. I am therefore by no means discouraged."

"Upon my word, sir, your hope is a rather extraordinary one after my declaration. I am perfectly serious in my refusal. You could not make me happy, and I am convinced that I am the last woman in the world who could make you so."

But nothing she said could convince him. He was sure that her refusal was merely the usual practice of elegant females. He would speak to her parents, he said, and their authority would settle everything. At last Elizabeth gave up and left the room in silence.

Mrs Bennet had been waiting outside the door. When she heard what had happened, she hurried to the library.

"Oh, Mr Bennet, you are wanted immediately. We are all in an uproar. You must come and make Lizzy marry Mr Collins, for she vows she will not have him, and if you do not hurry he will change his mind and not have her."

Mr Bennet raised his eyes from his book calmly. "I have not the pleasure of understanding you. Of what are you talking?"

When it had been explained, he sent for Elizabeth.

"Come here, child," he said, as she appeared. "I understand that Mr Collins has made you an offer of marriage. Is it true?"

Elizabeth replied that it was.

"Very well. And this offer of marriage you have refused?"

"I have, sir."

"Very well. We now come to the point. Your mother insists upon your accepting it. Is it not so, Mrs Bennet?"

"Yes, or I will never see her again."

"An unhappy alternative is before you, Elizabeth. From this day you must be a stranger to one of your parents. Your mother will never see you again if you do not marry Mr Collins, and I will never see you again if you do."

Elizabeth could not but smile at such a conclusion. Mrs Bennet, who had been sure that her husband would take her side, was bitterly disappointed. For the rest of the day she complained to anybody who would listen, and Mr Collins went about the house in silent resentment.

Charlotte Lucas came to spend the day at Longbourn, and she very kindly listened to Mr Collins. She listened to him that day, and the next day too. Elizabeth was grateful to her friend for keeping him in good humour. But Charlotte's kindness had a purpose of which Elizabeth had no idea. Its object was nothing less than to secure his addresses for herself.

Within three days, Mr Collins had proposed to Charlotte, and Charlotte had accepted him.

When Charlotte told her the news, Elizabeth could not hide her astonishment. "Engaged to Mr Collins! My dear Charlotte, impossible!"

Charlotte's face showed a moment of confusion, but she soon recovered her calm. "Why should you be surprised, my dear Eliza? Do you think it incredible that Mr Collins should be able to procure any woman's good opinion, because he was not so happy as to succeed with you? I am not romantic, you know. I never was. I ask only a comfortable home. And considering Mr Collins's character, connections and situation in life, I am convinced that my chance of happiness with him is as fair as most people can boast on entering the marriage state."

Elizabeth quietly answered, "Undoubtedly." But when she was alone, she felt that her friend had lowered herself in her eyes. Charlotte, the wife of Mr Collins! It was a most humiliating picture. Charlotte was twenty-seven, she was not pretty, and she had no fortune. Marriage was the only honourable provision for a well-educated young woman of small fortune. Elizabeth understood all this. Still, she could not help feeling that her friend had sacrificed every better feeling to worldly advantage.

Chapter 7: Netherfield Is Empty

The day after the ball, Mr Bingley went to London on business. He meant to be back within a few days. But soon a letter came for Jane from Miss Bingley, and it destroyed all hope.

The whole party had left Netherfield, the letter said, and they had no intention of coming back that winter. Caroline Bingley added that her brother was very much looking forward to seeing Miss Georgiana Darcy again. Georgiana had no equal for beauty, elegance and accomplishments, and the whole family hoped that she would soon be their sister.

"Is it not clear enough?" said Jane sadly, when she had read it aloud. "Does it not plainly declare that Caroline neither expects nor wishes me to be her sister? That she is perfectly convinced of her brother's indifference, and that she means, most kindly, to put me on my guard?"

"No," said Elizabeth firmly. "Miss Bingley sees that her brother is in love with you, and wants him to marry Miss Darcy. She follows him to town in the hope of keeping him there. No one who has ever seen you together can doubt his affection."

Jane wished to believe her, but she was not able. She was gentle and patient, and she did not complain. But Elizabeth knew how much she suffered.

Mrs Bennet complained enough for both. Netherfield was empty, Jane was not engaged, and Charlotte Lucas would one day be mistress of Longbourn. She could not look at Charlotte without imagining her taking possession of the house, and turning them all out before Mr Bennet was cold in his grave.

Mr Bennet treated the matter differently. "So, Lizzy," said he one day, "your sister is crossed in love, I find. I congratulate her. Next to being married, a girl likes to be crossed in love a little now and then. It is something to think of, and it gives her a sort of distinction among her companions. When is your turn to come? Let Wickham be your man. He is a pleasant fellow, and would jilt you creditably."

Wickham, in fact, was often at Longbourn now. He openly told his story to everyone in Meryton, and the whole town agreed that Mr Darcy was the worst of men.

At Christmas, Mrs Bennet's brother, Mr Gardiner, came to stay with his wife. He was a sensible, gentlemanlike man who lived by trade in London, within view of his own warehouses. The Netherfield ladies would have had difficulty in believing that a man who lived by trade could be so well-bred and agreeable. Mrs Gardiner, who was several years younger than Mrs Bennet, was an amiable, intelligent, elegant woman, and a great favourite with all her nieces, especially the two eldest.

Mrs Gardiner invited Jane to come back with them to London for a few weeks. Perhaps, Elizabeth thought, Jane might meet Mr Bingley there by chance.

Before she left, Mrs Gardiner had a quiet word with Elizabeth. She had seen how much Wickham admired her, and how much Elizabeth liked him.

"You are too sensible a girl, Lizzy, to fall in love merely because you are warned against it. And therefore I am not afraid of speaking openly. Seriously, I would have you be on your guard. Do not involve yourself in an affection which the want of fortune would make so very imprudent. He has nothing, and neither have you."

"My dear aunt, this is being serious indeed. At present I am not in love with Mr Wickham. No, I certainly am not. But he is, beyond all comparison, the most agreeable man I ever saw. I will do my best."

Jane wrote often from London. She had called on Miss Bingley, who was out. Miss Bingley had returned the visit many weeks later, and had stayed only a few minutes, cold and formal. She had said nothing about her brother.

"I cannot understand it," Jane wrote, "but I will not think about it any more. My dearest sister will, I am sure, be very sorry for me. I confess myself to have been entirely deceived in Miss Bingley's regard for me."

Elizabeth read the letter with deep anger. As for Bingley, she was sure his sisters and his friend had kept him from Jane. She was hardly surprised when, soon afterwards, Wickham's attentions to herself came to an end. A young lady, Miss King, had recently come into ten thousand pounds, and Wickham was now the most charming of men to her. Elizabeth was too fond of him to be very angry. She told herself that a handsome young man must have something to live on, as well as a plain one.

Chapter 8: Rosings Park

In March, Elizabeth travelled into Kent to visit Charlotte at Hunsford, with Sir William Lucas and his younger daughter, Maria. Charlotte had been married for some months, and she had asked her friend so warmly to come that Elizabeth could not refuse.

Mr Collins received them at the garden gate, delighted to show them his house, his garden, his furniture and his view. Elizabeth could not help thinking that he wanted her to feel what she had lost by refusing him. Yet she saw that Charlotte managed him very cleverly. She had given him the front room for his books, and she sat in a small room at the back, where he was not tempted to visit her often. When Mr Collins said anything of which his wife might reasonably be ashamed, which certainly was not seldom, she wisely did not hear. Elizabeth had to admit that it was all done very well.

The great subject at Hunsford was Lady Catherine de Bourgh. Her house, Rosings Park, was just across the lane. Her carriage passed by almost every day. Her advice came in on every subject: the arrangement of the furniture, the management of the servants, the size of the pieces of meat. And within a few days, the whole party was invited to dine at Rosings.

"Do not make yourself uneasy, my dear cousin, about your dress," said Mr Collins to Elizabeth. "Lady Catherine is far from requiring that elegance of dress in us which becomes herself and her daughter. I would advise you merely to put on whatever of your clothes is superior to the rest. She will not think the worse of you for being simply dressed. She likes to have the distinction of rank preserved."

Sir William was frightened, and Maria was almost too frightened to speak. Elizabeth felt quite equal to the scene. She had heard nothing of Lady Catherine that spoke of any extraordinary talents or miraculous virtue, and the mere stateliness of money and rank she thought she could witness without trembling.

Lady Catherine was a tall, large woman with strongly marked features. Her manner was not friendly. Whatever she said was spoken in so authoritative a tone as to show her own importance. Her daughter, Miss de Bourgh, was pale, thin and sickly. She hardly spoke a word to anyone.

At dinner Lady Catherine talked without stopping and delivered her opinion on every subject in so decisive a manner as proved that she was not used to having her judgement contradicted. Then she turned to Elizabeth and asked her a great many questions. How many sisters had she? Were any of them likely to be married? Were they handsome? Where had they been educated?

"Do you play and sing, Miss Bennet?"

"A little."

"Oh! Then some time or other we shall be happy to hear you. Do your sisters play and sing? Do you draw? Did your governess teach you?"

"We never had any governess."

"No governess! How was that possible? Five daughters brought up at home without a governess! I never heard of such a thing. Your mother must have been quite a slave to your education."

Elizabeth could hardly help smiling, as she assured her that had not been the case.

"Are any of your younger sisters out, Miss Bennet?"

"Yes, ma'am, all."

"All! What, all five out at once? Very odd! And you only the second. The younger ones out before the elder are married! Your younger sisters must be very young?"

"Yes, my youngest is not sixteen. But really, ma'am, I think it would be very hard upon younger sisters that they should not have their share of society and amusement because the elder may not have the means or inclination to marry early."

"Upon my word," said her ladyship, "you give your opinion very decidedly for so young a person. Pray, what is your age?"

"With three younger sisters grown up," replied Elizabeth, smiling, "your ladyship can hardly expect me to own it."

Lady Catherine seemed quite astonished at not receiving a direct answer. Elizabeth suspected herself to be the first creature who had ever dared to trifle with so much dignified impertinence.

About two weeks later, Mr Darcy arrived at Rosings to visit his aunt. He brought with him his cousin, Colonel Fitzwilliam, the younger son of an earl. The colonel was about thirty, not handsome, but in person and address most truly the gentleman. He had a lively manner and talked easily with Elizabeth, and she liked him at once. Mr Darcy said very little, as usual. But Elizabeth noticed that he looked at her often.

One evening at Rosings, Elizabeth sat down at the piano, and Colonel Fitzwilliam drew a chair near her. Mr Darcy left his aunt and walked across to stand where he had a full view of the performer's face.

Elizabeth saw what he was doing and turned to him with an arch smile. "You mean to frighten me, Mr Darcy, by coming in all this state to hear me? But I will not be alarmed. There is a stubbornness about me that never can bear to be frightened at the will of others. My courage always rises with every attempt to intimidate me."

"I shall not say that you are mistaken," he replied, "because you could not really believe me to entertain any design of alarming you."

Elizabeth laughed heartily, and turned to the colonel. "Shall I tell you what I know of him in Hertfordshire? The first time I ever saw him was at a ball, and at this ball, what do you think he did? He danced only four dances, though gentlemen were scarce, and more than one young lady was sitting down in want of a partner."

"I had not at that time the honour of knowing any lady in the assembly beyond my own party," said Darcy. "I certainly have not the talent which some people possess of conversing easily with those I have never seen before."

"My fingers," said Elizabeth, "do not move over this instrument in the masterly manner which I see so many women's do. But then I have always supposed it to be my own fault, because I will not take the trouble of practising."

Darcy smiled. "You are perfectly right. You have employed your time much better. No one admitted to the privilege of hearing you can think anything wanting. We neither of us perform to strangers."

Elizabeth did not know what to make of this. Was it a compliment? From Mr Darcy, it seemed impossible.

During the following days he called at the parsonage quite often, sometimes with the colonel, sometimes alone. He would sit for ten minutes without opening his lips, and then leave. Charlotte began to wonder whether he was in love with her friend. Elizabeth only laughed at the idea. More than once, when she was walking alone in the park, she met Mr Darcy by chance. It seemed like wilful ill nature, or a voluntary penance, for on these occasions it was not merely a few formal enquiries and an awkward pause and then away. He actually thought it necessary to turn back and walk with her.

Chapter 9: In Vain Have I Struggled

One morning, as Elizabeth was walking in the park, she met Colonel Fitzwilliam instead of Mr Darcy. They walked on together and talked about his cousin.

"Mr Darcy is always glad to have someone at his disposal," said Elizabeth. "I wonder he does not marry, to secure a lasting convenience of that kind."

"He likes to have his own way very well," replied the colonel. "But so we all do. It is only that he has better means of having it than many others, because he is rich, and many others are poor. A younger son, you know, must be inured to self-denial and dependence. Younger sons cannot marry where they like."

Elizabeth wondered whether this was meant for her, and coloured. But she recovered quickly and turned the conversation. "I imagine Mr Darcy is a very kind friend. Mr Bingley seems to depend on him a great deal."

"Oh yes. Darcy takes great care of Bingley. I have reason to think Bingley very much in his debt. From something that he told me on our journey here, I believe Darcy congratulated himself on having lately saved a friend from the inconveniences of a most imprudent marriage. He mentioned no names, of course. But I think it was Bingley. There were some very strong objections against the lady."

"Did Mr Darcy give you his reasons for this interference?"

"I understood that there were some very strong objections against the lady," the colonel repeated, not noticing her face.

Elizabeth walked on, her heart swelling with indignation. There could not exist in the world two men over whom Mr Darcy could have such unbounded influence. He had been the cause, his pride and caprice were the cause, of all that Jane had suffered and still continued to suffer. The objections against the lady! Jane herself was all loveliness and goodness. Her understanding was excellent, her mind improved and her manners captivating. Could anything be urged against her father, who, though with some peculiarities, had abilities which Mr Darcy himself need not disdain? When she thought of her mother, her confidence gave way a little. But she would not allow that any objections there had much weight with Mr Darcy. His pride, she was sure, would be hurt more by the want of importance in his friend's connections than by their want of sense.

She returned to the parsonage with a violent headache, and when the others went to Rosings for tea that evening, she stayed at home alone. She was reading Jane's last letters again when, to her utter amazement, the doorbell rang and Mr Darcy walked into the room.

In a hurried manner he began to ask after her health. She answered him with cold civility. He sat down for a few moments, and then, getting up, walked about the room. Elizabeth was surprised, but said not a word. After a silence of several minutes, he came towards her in an agitated manner.

"In vain have I struggled. It will not do. My feelings will not be repressed. You must allow me to tell you how ardently I admire and love you."

Elizabeth's astonishment was beyond expression. She stared, coloured, doubted and was silent. He took this as sufficient encouragement, and the confession of all that he felt, and had long felt, for her immediately followed.

He spoke well. But there were feelings besides those of the heart to be described, and he was not more eloquent on the subject of tenderness than of pride. He spoke of her inferiority, of the degradation it would be for him, of the family obstacles which judgement had always opposed to inclination. He had fought against his love for her for months, he said, against his reason and his family and even his own character. But he had failed.

In spite of her deep-rooted dislike, she could not be insensible to the compliment of such a man's affection. For a moment she was even sorry for the pain he was about to receive. But his words soon turned her pity into anger. When he ended by expressing his hope that she would now accept his hand, she could easily see that he had no doubt of a favourable answer. He spoke of apprehension and anxiety, but his face expressed real security. Such a circumstance could only make her angrier, and when he stopped, the colour rose in her cheeks.

"In such cases as this," she said, "it is, I believe, the established mode to express a sense of obligation for the sentiments avowed. If I could feel gratitude, I would now thank you. But I cannot. I have never desired your good opinion, and you have certainly given it most unwillingly."

Mr Darcy, who was leaning against the fireplace with his eyes fixed on her face, seemed to catch her words with no less resentment than surprise. His face became pale with anger. He struggled for the appearance of calm, and would not open his lips till he believed himself to have attained it.

"And this is all the reply which I am to have the honour of expecting! I might, perhaps, wish to be informed why, with so little endeavour at civility, I am thus rejected."

"I might as well enquire," replied she, "why, with so evident a desire of offending and insulting me, you chose to tell me that you liked me against your will, against your reason, and even against your character? Was not this some excuse for incivility, if I was uncivil? But I have other provocations. You know I have. Do you think that any consideration would tempt me to accept the man who has been the means of ruining, perhaps for ever, the happiness of a most beloved sister?"

As she pronounced these words, Mr Darcy changed colour. But the emotion was short, and he listened without attempting to interrupt her.

"I have every reason in the world to think ill of you. Can you deny that you have done it?"

"I have no wish of denying that I did everything in my power to separate my friend from your sister," he replied with assumed calmness, "or that I rejoice in my success. Towards him I have been kinder than towards myself."

"But it is not merely this affair on which my dislike is founded. Long before it had taken place, my opinion of you was decided. Your character was unfolded in the story which I received many months ago from Mr Wickham. On this subject, what can you have to say?"

"You take an eager interest in that gentleman's concerns," said Darcy, in a less tranquil tone.

"Who that knows what his misfortunes have been, can help feeling an interest in him? You have reduced him to his present state of poverty. You have withheld the advantages which you must know to have been designed for him."

"And this," cried Darcy, as he walked with quick steps across the room, "is your opinion of me! I thank you for explaining it so fully. My faults, according to this calculation, are heavy indeed! But perhaps these offences might have been overlooked, had not your pride been hurt by my honest confession of the doubts that had long prevented my forming any serious design. Could you expect me to rejoice in the inferiority of your connections?"

Elizabeth felt herself growing more angry every moment. Yet she tried to speak with composure.

"You are mistaken, Mr Darcy, if you suppose that the mode of your declaration affected me in any other way than as it spared me the concern which I might have felt in refusing you, had you behaved in a more gentlemanlike manner."

She saw him start at this, but he said nothing, and she continued.

"You could not have made me the offer of your hand in any possible way that would have tempted me to accept it. From the very beginning, from the first moment of my acquaintance with you, your manners impressed me with the fullest belief of your arrogance, your conceit, and your selfish disdain of the feelings of others. I had not known you a month before I felt that you were the last man in the world whom I could ever be prevailed on to marry."

"You have said quite enough, madam. I perfectly comprehend your feelings, and have now only to be ashamed of what my own have been. Forgive me for having taken up so much of your time, and accept my best wishes for your health and happiness."

And with these words he hastily left the room, and Elizabeth heard him the next moment open the front door and quit the house.

The tumult of her mind was now painfully great. She did not know how to support herself, and from actual weakness sat down and cried for half an hour. That she should receive an offer of marriage from Mr Darcy! That he should have been in love with her for so many months! It was gratifying to have inspired such an affection. But his abominable pride, his shameless confession of what he had done with respect to Jane, and the unfeeling manner in which he had mentioned Mr Wickham, soon overcame the pity which the consideration of his attachment had for a moment excited.

Chapter 10: The Letter

Elizabeth awoke the next morning to the same thoughts with which she had at last closed her eyes. She could not recover from the surprise of what had happened, and soon after breakfast she went out for a walk. She was moving along the edge of the park when she saw a gentleman among the trees. It was Mr Darcy. He held out a letter, which she instinctively took.

"I have been walking in the grove some time in the hope of meeting you. Will you do me the honour of reading that letter?" And with a slight bow, he turned away again and was soon out of sight.

With no expectation of pleasure, but with the strongest curiosity, Elizabeth opened the letter. It contained two sheets of paper, written quite through, in a very close hand.

"Be not alarmed, madam, on receiving this letter, by the fear of its containing any repetition of those sentiments or renewal of those offers which were last night so disgusting to you. Two offences of a very different nature, and by no means of equal magnitude, you last night laid to my charge. The first was that I had separated Mr Bingley from your sister. The other, that I had ruined the prospects of Mr Wickham."

About Jane, he wrote that he had watched her carefully at the Netherfield ball. He had seen that Bingley loved her. But Jane's manner, though open and cheerful, had shown no sign of any special affection. He had believed that she did not care for his friend, and that she would accept him only because her mother wished it. He admitted that he might have been wrong. If Elizabeth knew her sister better, and he had been deceived, her anger was not unreasonable.

There were other reasons too. "The situation of your mother's family, though objectionable, was nothing in comparison to that total want of propriety so frequently, so almost uniformly betrayed by herself, by your three younger sisters, and occasionally even by your father. Pardon me. It pains me to offend you. But it is honourable to you and your eldest sister that you have always conducted yourselves so as to avoid any share of the like censure."

He confessed that he had joined Bingley's sisters in persuading him to stay in London, and that he had kept from Bingley the fact that Jane had been in town all winter. "Perhaps this concealment was beneath me. It is done, however, and it was done for the best."

The second part of the letter was about Wickham. The late Mr Darcy had indeed loved him and had hoped he would become a clergyman. But Wickham had grown up with vicious habits, which he was always careful to hide from the old gentleman. After his godfather's death, he had told Darcy that he had no intention of entering the Church. Instead, he asked for money to study law. Darcy gave him three thousand pounds, and Wickham gave up all claim to the living.

The money did not last long. Wickham lived a life of idleness and pleasure in London. When the living became free three years later, he wrote again, poor and angry, and demanded it. Darcy refused.

"Last summer he again most painfully obtruded on my notice. I must now mention a circumstance which I would wish to forget myself, and which no obligation less than the present should induce me to unfold to any human being." Darcy's sister Georgiana, then only fifteen, had been staying at Ramsgate on the sea. Wickham had followed her there and, with the help of her companion, Mrs Younge, had persuaded the girl that she was in love with him. She had agreed to elope with him. Darcy had arrived by chance only a day or two before they were to leave. Georgiana, unable to bear the idea of grieving a brother whom she almost looked up to as a father, confessed everything to him.

"Mr Wickham's chief object was unquestionably my sister's fortune, which is thirty thousand pounds. But I cannot help supposing that the hope of revenging himself on me was a strong motive. His revenge would have been complete indeed."

He added that Colonel Fitzwilliam, who was Georgiana's other guardian, knew all these facts and could confirm them. "I will only add, God bless you. Fitzwilliam Darcy."

Elizabeth read the account of Wickham with a strong wish not to believe it. "This must be false! This cannot be! This must be the grossest falsehood!" She put the letter away, determined never to look at it again. But within half a minute she unfolded it once more and read every line with the closest attention.

Slowly the truth came to her. She remembered every word Wickham had said on the night at her aunt's house. How strange it seemed now that he had told such a story to a stranger! He had boasted that he had no fear of seeing Mr Darcy, yet he had avoided the Netherfield ball that very week. He had told his story to nobody but her while the Netherfield family remained in the country, but as soon as they had left, he had told it to the whole town. And his attentions to Miss King had been for her money alone. Every circumstance which she had thought in Wickham's favour now seemed to speak against him.

She grew absolutely ashamed of herself. Of neither Darcy nor Wickham could she think without feeling that she had been blind, partial, prejudiced, absurd.

"How despicably I have acted!" she cried. "I, who have prided myself on my discernment! I, who have valued myself on my abilities! Had I been in love, I could not have been more wretchedly blind. But vanity, not love, has been my folly. Pleased with the preference of one, and offended by the neglect of the other, on the very beginning of our acquaintance, I have courted prepossession and ignorance, and driven reason away. Till this moment I never knew myself."

From herself to Jane, and from Jane to Bingley, her thoughts went on. She read again what he had said about her sister. Jane's feelings, though fervent, were little displayed. Charlotte had said the same thing months ago. And when she came to the part about her family, and the propriety they so often lacked, her sense of shame was severe. She could not deny the justice of the charge. The scene at the Netherfield ball came back to her: her mother's loud talk at supper, Mary's songs, Lydia's laughter.

She walked for two hours, until fatigue and a recollection of her long absence made her at length return home. Mr Darcy and Colonel Fitzwilliam had both called while she was out, to take leave of the party. They were to leave Rosings the next day.

Chapter 11: Lydia Goes to Brighton

In May, Elizabeth went home to Longbourn. Jane came back from London at the same time. As soon as they were alone, Elizabeth told her sister about Mr Darcy's proposal and his letter, though she kept silent about everything that concerned Bingley. She would not give Jane new pain for nothing.

Jane was astonished. She could hardly believe that there was so much wickedness in the world. Most of all, she was sorry for Mr Darcy. "Poor Mr Darcy! Dear Lizzy, only consider what he must have suffered. Such a disappointment! And with the knowledge of your ill opinion too!"

"There certainly was some great mismanagement in the education of those two young men," said Elizabeth. "One has got all the goodness, and the other all the appearance of it."

"But should we tell people in Meryton about Wickham?" asked Jane.

They decided against it. Wickham's regiment would leave Meryton in two weeks. Mr Darcy had not given her permission to make his letter public, and the story of his sister must remain secret. Besides, the whole town had such a strong prejudice against Mr Darcy that nobody would believe a word said in his favour.

The departure of the regiment brought despair to Kitty and Lydia. They were to go to Brighton, a fashionable town on the south coast, where there would be balls, uniforms and a camp full of soldiers. Only the two youngest Miss Bennets thought it a tragedy that they were to remain behind.

"Good heaven! What is to become of us? What are we to do?" cried Lydia. "How can you be smiling so, Lizzy?"

But Lydia's despair did not last. She received an invitation from Mrs Forster, the young wife of the colonel of the regiment, to go with them to Brighton as her particular friend. The rapture of Lydia, her adoration of Mrs Forster, the delight of Mrs Bennet and the mortification of Kitty are scarcely to be described.

Elizabeth was alarmed. She went privately to her father and begged him not to let Lydia go.

"Lydia will never be easy till she has exposed herself in some public place or other," said Mr Bennet, "and we can never expect her to do it with so little expense or inconvenience to her family as under the present circumstances."

"If you were aware," said Elizabeth, "of the very great disadvantage to us all which must arise from the public notice of Lydia's unguarded and imprudent manner, I am sure you would judge differently in the affair. Excuse me, for I must speak plainly. If you, my dear father, will not take the trouble of checking her wild spirits, she will soon be beyond the reach of amendment. Her character will be fixed, and she will, at sixteen, be the most determined flirt that ever made herself or her family ridiculous."

Mr Bennet saw that her whole heart was in the subject, and he took her hand affectionately. "Do not make yourself uneasy, my love. We shall have no peace at Longbourn if Lydia does not go to Brighton. Let her go, then. Colonel Forster is a sensible man, and will keep her out of any real mischief. Luckily she is too poor to be an object of prey to anybody. At Brighton she will be of less importance even as a common flirt than she has been here. The officers will find women better worth their notice."

With this answer Elizabeth had to be content. But her own opinion remained the same, and she left him disappointed and sorry.

On the day before the regiment left, Wickham dined at Longbourn. Elizabeth was polite, but she had lost all pleasure in his company. His gentleness, which had once charmed her, now seemed false and tiresome. When he asked her how she had passed her time at Hunsford, she mentioned that Colonel Fitzwilliam and Mr Darcy had both spent three weeks at Rosings.

He looked surprised and a little alarmed. "And pray, may I ask, is Mr Darcy improved?"

"Oh no!" said Elizabeth. "In essentials, I believe, he is very much what he ever was. I do not mean that his mind or manners were in a state of improvement. But from knowing him better, his disposition was better understood."

Wickham's face showed a sudden anxiety. He was silent for a while, and then said in a gentle voice that he hoped Mr Darcy would be wise enough to show his good manners at Rosings at least, out of fear of his aunt. Elizabeth could not repress a smile. They parted at last with mutual civility, and possibly a mutual desire of never meeting again.

The next morning, Lydia left for Brighton with Mrs Forster. Her farewell to her family was noisy rather than tender. She promised to write very often and in great detail, and her letters, when they came, were short and full of officers.

With the regiment gone, Meryton became quiet. Elizabeth found that the summer at home without Jane's first happiness, with a discontented mother and a sulking Kitty, was dull. Her only comfort was a plan for the summer. Mr and Mrs Gardiner had invited her to join them on a tour of the north. In the end, because of Mr Gardiner's business, it became a shorter journey to Derbyshire, the county where Mrs Gardiner had grown up. It was also, of course, the county of Pemberley.

Chapter 12: Pemberley

In Derbyshire, Mrs Gardiner expressed a wish to see Pemberley again. It was only five miles from Lambton, the small town where she had lived as a girl. Elizabeth was distressed. What if she should meet Mr Darcy there? But she discovered from a chambermaid at the inn that the family were away for the summer, and so she agreed to go.

They drove through a beautiful wood and climbed a hill. At the top, the wood ended, and Pemberley House came suddenly into view, on the opposite side of a valley. It was a large, handsome stone building, standing well on rising ground, with a ridge of high woody hills behind it. In front, a stream had been made larger, but without any artificial appearance. Elizabeth was delighted. She had never seen a place for which nature had done more, or where natural beauty had been so little spoiled by an awkward taste. At that moment she felt that to be mistress of Pemberley might be something.

The housekeeper, Mrs Reynolds, a respectable-looking elderly woman, showed them round. The rooms were lofty and handsome, and their furniture was neither gaudy nor uselessly fine. There was less of splendour and more real elegance than at Rosings.

"And of all this," thought Elizabeth, "I might have been mistress!"

In one room there was a small painting of Wickham, which Mrs Reynolds said had been done when he was a boy. "He has now gone into the army," she added, "but I am afraid he has turned out very wild."

Then Mrs Gardiner noticed a portrait of Mr Darcy, and Mrs Reynolds spoke of him with great warmth.

"He is the best landlord, and the best master, that ever lived. Not like the wild young men nowadays, who think of nothing but themselves. There is not one of his tenants or servants but will give him a good name. Some people call him proud, but I am sure I never saw anything of it. I have never had a cross word from him in my life, and I have known him ever since he was four years old."

Elizabeth listened, amazed. In the picture gallery upstairs she found a larger portrait of him, with such a smile over the face as she remembered to have sometimes seen when he looked at her. She stood before it for several minutes in earnest thought. As a brother, a landlord, a master, she considered how many people's happiness were in his guardianship. And she thought of his regard for her with a deeper sense of gratitude than it had ever raised before.

They left the house and walked down across the lawn towards the river. As they walked, the owner of the house suddenly came forward from the road behind it.

They were within twenty yards of each other, and his appearance was so sudden that it was impossible to avoid his sight. Their eyes instantly met, and the cheeks of both were overspread with the deepest blush. He absolutely started, and for a moment seemed unable to move from surprise. But soon recovering himself, he came towards the party and spoke to Elizabeth, if not in terms of perfect composure, at least of perfect civility.

Elizabeth hardly dared lift her eyes to his face. She was overpowered by shame. Her coming there was the most unfortunate, the most ill-judged thing in the world! How strange it must appear to him! It might seem as if she had purposely thrown herself in his way again!

But it was his manner that astonished her most. He asked after her family in a gentle voice. He spoke with perfect politeness to her uncle and aunt, the relations whom he would once have despised. When she introduced Mr and Mrs Gardiner, he walked back with them and talked to Mr Gardiner with real interest. He invited him to fish in the river as often as he chose while he remained in the neighbourhood, and offered to supply him with fishing tackle.

"What can be the meaning of it?" Elizabeth wondered. "It cannot be for me. It cannot be for my sake that his manners are thus softened. My reproofs at Hunsford could not work such a change as this. It is impossible that he should still love me."

Before they parted, he asked whether she would allow him to introduce his sister to her during her stay at Lambton. Elizabeth was surprised and pleased, and she agreed.

The very next morning, Mr Darcy brought Georgiana to the inn. Elizabeth had heard that Miss Darcy was exceedingly proud. But she saw at once that she was only exceedingly shy. Georgiana was tall, and though a little older than Lydia, her figure was formed and her appearance was womanly and graceful. She was less handsome than her brother, but there was sense and good humour in her face, and her manners were perfectly unassuming and gentle.

Bingley came too, and he was as warm and friendly as ever. Elizabeth watched him carefully. He asked whether all her sisters were at Longbourn, and he mentioned, in a quiet voice, that it had been a very long time since he had had the pleasure of seeing her. "It is above eight months. We have not met since the twenty-sixth of November, when we were all dancing together at Netherfield." Elizabeth was pleased to find his memory so exact.

The Gardiners and Elizabeth were invited to dine at Pemberley. When they called on Georgiana the next day, Miss Bingley, who was staying there, could not hide her jealousy. She asked, with sneering civility, whether the militia had left Meryton, and whether that was not a great loss to Elizabeth's family. She dared not mention Wickham by name. But Georgiana grew pale and could not lift her eyes, and Elizabeth saw how much Darcy wished to protect his sister. She answered calmly, and Miss Bingley's attack failed.

When the visitors had gone, Miss Bingley could not stop criticising Elizabeth's dress, her manners and her looks. "I remember, when we first knew her in Hertfordshire, how amazed we all were to find that she was a reputed beauty. And I particularly remember your saying one night, she a beauty! I should as soon call her mother a wit. But afterwards she seemed to improve on you, and I believe you thought her rather pretty at one time."

"Yes," replied Darcy, who could contain himself no longer, "but that was only when I first knew her, for it is many months since I have considered her as one of the handsomest women of my acquaintance."

He then went away, and Miss Bingley was left to all the satisfaction of having forced him to say what gave no one any pain but herself.

Chapter 13: Bad News from Brighton

The next morning, Elizabeth received two letters from Jane at once. One had been sent to the wrong place, and had arrived late. She sat down to read them while her uncle and aunt went out for a walk.

The first letter had been written five days before. It began with news of little parties and engagements, but the second half, dated a day later, was written in obvious haste.

"Something has occurred of a most unexpected and serious nature. I hardly know what I would write, but I have bad news for you, and it cannot be delayed. An express came at twelve last night from Colonel Forster, to tell us that Lydia was gone off to Scotland with one of his officers. To own the truth, with Wickham! Imagine our surprise. To Kitty, however, it does not seem so wholly unexpected. I am very, very sorry. So imprudent a match on both sides! But I am willing to hope the best, and that his character has been misunderstood."

With trembling hands Elizabeth opened the second letter.

"By this time, my dearest sister, you have received my hurried letter. I wish this may be more clear, but my head is so confused that I cannot answer for being coherent. Dearest Lizzy, I hardly know what I would write, but I have bad news for you. Imprudent as a marriage between Mr Wickham and our poor Lydia would be, we are now anxious to be assured it has taken place, for there is but too much reason to fear they are not gone to Scotland. Colonel Forster came yesterday. Lydia had left a note for Mrs Forster, but Denny believes that Wickham never intended to go there, or to marry Lydia at all. They were traced as far as London, and then all trace of them was lost."

Lydia was not yet sixteen. If Wickham did not marry her, she was ruined, and the shame would fall on the whole family. Jane begged her uncle to come home at once and help their father, who was leaving for London to search for them.

Elizabeth jumped up and rushed to the door to find her uncle. But as she reached it, the door was opened by a servant, and Mr Darcy appeared. Her pale face and impetuous manner made him start.

"I beg your pardon, but I must leave you," she cried. "I must find Mr Gardiner this moment, on business that cannot be delayed. I have not an instant to lose."

"Good God! What is the matter?" he cried, with more feeling than politeness. Then, recollecting himself, he added, "I will not detain you a minute. But let me, or let the servant, go after Mr and Mrs Gardiner. You are not well enough. You cannot go yourself."

Elizabeth hesitated, but her knees trembled under her. She called back the servant and sent him to fetch her uncle and aunt. Then she sat down, and looked so miserably ill that Darcy could not leave her.

"Let me call your maid. Is there nothing you could take to give you present relief? A glass of wine? You are very ill."

"No, I thank you," she replied, trying to recover herself. "There is nothing the matter with me. I am quite well. I am only distressed by some dreadful news which I have just received from Longbourn."

She burst into tears as she spoke, and for a few minutes could not say another word. Darcy watched her in silence.

At length she spoke again. "I have just had a letter from Jane, with such dreadful news. It cannot be concealed from anyone. My youngest sister has left all her friends, has eloped, has thrown herself into the power of Mr Wickham. You know him too well to doubt the rest. She has no money, no connections, nothing that can tempt him to marry her. She is lost for ever."

Darcy was fixed in astonishment.

"When I consider," she added, in a yet more agitated voice, "that I might have prevented it! I, who knew what he was. Had I but explained some part of it, only some part of what I learnt, to my own family! Had his character been known, this could not have happened. But it is all, all too late now."

"I am grieved indeed," cried Darcy, "grieved, shocked. But is it certain, absolutely certain?"

"Oh yes! They left Brighton together on Sunday night, and were traced almost to London, but not beyond. They are certainly not gone to Scotland."

Darcy made no answer. He seemed scarcely to hear her, and was walking up and down the room in earnest thought, his face grave and dark. Elizabeth soon observed it, and instantly understood it. Her power was sinking. Everything must sink under such a proof of family weakness, such an assurance of the deepest disgrace. And never had she so honestly felt that she could have loved him, as now, when all love must be in vain.

After a short pause, he said that he would not stay longer. He was sorry he could do nothing to help, and he hoped her family would soon be relieved. He would say nothing about the matter at Pemberley. Then, with only one serious, parting look, he went away.

As he left the room, Elizabeth felt how improbable it was that they should ever see each other again with such friendliness as had marked their meetings in Derbyshire.

Mr and Mrs Gardiner hurried back, and within an hour the whole party were on the road to Longbourn.

At home, they found everything as unhappy as Elizabeth had feared. Mrs Bennet stayed in her room, crying and blaming everybody except herself. "If I had been able to go to Brighton with all my family, this would not have happened. Poor dear child! And now here is Mr Bennet gone away, and I know he will fight Wickham, wherever he meets him, and then he will be killed, and what is to become of us all? The Collinses will turn us out before he is cold in his grave."

Kitty admitted, with tears, that Lydia had told her of her plans in her letters, but she had promised not to tell anyone. Mary offered moral reflections. "Unhappy as the event must be for Lydia," she said, "we may draw from it this useful lesson: that loss of virtue in a female is irretrievable."

Elizabeth lifted up her eyes in amazement, but was too much oppressed to make any reply.

Jane told Elizabeth that Lydia's note to Mrs Forster had been full of laughter. "What a good joke it will be!" she had written. "I can hardly write for laughing. I am going to Gretna Green, and if you cannot guess with who, I shall think you a simpleton, for there is but one man in the world I love, and he is an angel."

"Oh, thoughtless, thoughtless Lydia!" cried Elizabeth.

Mr Gardiner went to London to join the search. Mr Bennet came home some days later, quiet and tired, having found nothing. He said very little about it. But when Elizabeth tried to comfort him, he stopped her.

"Say nothing of that. Who should suffer but myself? It has been my own doing, and I ought to feel it. No, Lizzy, let me once in my life feel how much I have been to blame. I am not afraid of being overpowered by the impression. It will pass away soon enough."

Chapter 14: The Marriage of Lydia

Two days after Mr Bennet's return, a letter arrived from Mr Gardiner. Jane and Elizabeth were walking in the garden when they saw the housekeeper coming towards them. They ran to find their father.

"Read it aloud," said Mr Bennet, "for I hardly know myself what it is about."

The letter said that Lydia and Wickham had been found. They were not married, and Mr Gardiner could not discover that there had ever been any intention of being so. But he had made an agreement. If Mr Bennet would give Lydia her equal share of the five thousand pounds that would come to his daughters after his death, and a hundred pounds a year besides, Wickham would marry her. Mr Gardiner would arrange everything else. Lydia would stay at the Gardiners' house in London until the wedding.

"Is it possible?" cried Elizabeth. "Can it be possible that he will marry her?"

"Wickham is not so undeserving, then, as we thought him," said Jane.

"And they must marry! Yet he is such a man!" said Mr Bennet. "There are two things that I want very much to know: one is, how much money your uncle has laid down to bring it about; and the other, how I am ever to pay him."

"Money! My uncle!" cried Jane. "What do you mean, sir?"

"I mean that no man in his senses would marry Lydia on so slight a temptation as a hundred a year during my life, and fifty after I am gone. Wickham is a fool if he takes her with a farthing less than ten thousand pounds. I should be sorry to think so ill of him, in the very beginning of our relationship."

Mrs Bennet's spirits recovered at once. She came downstairs as if nothing had happened, and talked of nothing but wedding clothes and servants and houses. Her daughter would be married at sixteen! Married! She had no thought for the shame of the elopement, and no gratitude for her brother.

"My dear, dear Lydia! How merry we shall be together when we meet!"

Mr Bennet would not let the young couple come to Longbourn at first. But Jane and Elizabeth persuaded him that Lydia ought to be received by her family as a married woman, and he agreed. Wickham had accepted a new commission in a regiment in the north of England, and they were to visit Longbourn on their way there.

When they arrived, Elizabeth was disgusted, and even Jane was shocked. Lydia was Lydia still: untamed, unabashed, wild, noisy and fearless. She showed her ring to everyone and took her mother's place at the table. "Ah, Jane, I take your place now, and you must go lower, because I am a married woman." Wickham was all smiles, as easy and charming as ever. He seemed to feel no shame at all.

"Well, Mamma," said Lydia, "and what do you think of my husband? Is not he a charming man? I am sure my sisters must all envy me. I only hope they may have half my good luck. They must all go to Brighton. That is the place to get husbands."

One morning, Lydia began to describe her wedding to Elizabeth.

"We were married at St Clement's, you know. My uncle and aunt were horribly unpleasant all the time I was with them. I did not once put my foot out of doors, though I was there a fortnight. And I was so frightened that my uncle would be called away on business and not be there, and then who would give me away? But luckily he came back in ten minutes. However, I recollected afterwards that if he had been prevented going, the wedding need not be put off, for Mr Darcy might have done as well."

"Mr Darcy!" repeated Elizabeth, in utter amazement.

"Oh yes! He was to come there with Wickham, you know. But gracious me! I quite forgot! I ought not to have said a word about it. I promised them so faithfully! It was to be such a secret!"

Elizabeth could not rest. Mr Darcy had been at her sister's wedding! In the same church, among the very people whom he most disliked! She wrote at once to her aunt, begging for an explanation.

Mrs Gardiner's reply came quickly, and Elizabeth took it to the little wood where she was least likely to be interrupted.

The day after Elizabeth had left Derbyshire, Mrs Gardiner wrote, Mr Darcy had come to London. He had found Mrs Younge, the woman who had helped Wickham at Ramsgate, and through her he had found Lydia and Wickham. He had first tried to persuade Lydia to leave her lover and return to her friends. But Lydia would not go. She cared for none of her friends, she wanted no help of his, and she would not hear of leaving Wickham. She was sure they should be married some time or other, and it did not much signify when.

So he spoke to Wickham. Wickham had left Brighton because of his debts, and he had never meant to marry Lydia. He still hoped to make his fortune by marrying a rich woman somewhere else. But Darcy had paid his debts, which amounted to more than a thousand pounds. He had added another thousand to Lydia's own money, and he had bought Wickham his new commission. He had insisted that Mr Gardiner should take all the credit, and had said that it was his own fault: his pride had prevented him from making Wickham's character known to the world, and so the misfortune was partly his.

"Your uncle argued with him for a long time," Mrs Gardiner wrote, "but our visitor was very obstinate. I fancy, Lizzy, that obstinacy is the real defect of his character, after all. He has been accused of many faults at different times, but this is the true one. Nothing was to be done that he did not do himself."

At the end of the letter, her aunt added that she thought Mr Darcy had another reason for his kindness, which had nothing to do with Wickham. She did not need to say more. "Pray forgive me if I have been very presuming, or at least do not punish me so far as to exclude me from Pemberley. I shall never be quite happy till I have been all round the park."

The contents of this letter threw Elizabeth into a flutter of spirits. She had hoped, and yet she had hardly dared to hope, that he had done it for her. He had done it all for a girl whom he could neither regard nor esteem. He had done it for the sister of a woman who had refused him, and for the wife of a man he despised. Her heart did whisper that he had done it for her. Oh, how heartily did she grieve over every ungracious sensation she had ever encouraged, every saucy speech she had ever directed towards him! For herself she was humbled. But she was proud of him.

Chapter 15: The Happiest Creature in the World

Soon after the Wickhams had left for the north, the news reached Longbourn that Mr Bingley was coming back to Netherfield for the shooting season. Mrs Bennet was in a state of great excitement. Jane pretended to be calm.

"I saw him look at me, Lizzy, when we met in Meryton, and I assure you that his coming will give me neither pleasure nor pain. I am glad of one thing, that he comes alone, because we shall see the less of him."

A few days later, Kitty looked out of the window and cried that a gentleman was riding towards the house with Mr Bingley. "La! It looks just like that man that used to be with him before. Mr what-is-his-name. That tall, proud man."

"Good gracious! Mr Darcy! And so it does, I vow," cried Mrs Bennet. "Well, any friend of Mr Bingley's will always be welcome here, to be sure. But else I must say that I hate the very sight of him."

The visit was not a comfortable one for Elizabeth. Her mother was very warm to Mr Bingley and very cold to Mr Darcy, the man who had saved her favourite daughter from ruin, though she did not know it. Elizabeth was ashamed. Darcy said very little and looked more often at Elizabeth than at anyone else, but he did not sit near her. She could not tell what he was thinking.

Bingley, on the other hand, was soon talking to Jane, and he became more and more attentive to her each minute. When they were asked to dine at Longbourn a few days later, he took the seat beside her at once.

Then Darcy went to London on business, and Bingley stayed. He came every day. On the third evening, after a great deal of obvious arranging by Mrs Bennet, who kept winking at her daughters and calling Kitty out of the room, he was left alone with Jane in the drawing room.

When Elizabeth came back a little later, she found her sister and Bingley standing together over the fire, as if engaged in earnest conversation. Their faces, as they hastily turned round and moved away from each other, told it all. Bingley whispered a few words to Jane and ran out of the room to find Mr Bennet.

Jane instantly embraced her sister, and declared that she was the happiest creature in the world.

"It is too much!" she added. "By far too much. I do not deserve it. Oh! Why is not everybody as happy? Lizzy, if I could but see you as happy! If there were but such another man for you!"

"If you were to give me forty such men, I never could be so happy as you. Till I have your disposition, your goodness, I never can have your happiness. No, no, let me shift for myself. Perhaps, if I have very good luck, I may meet with another Mr Collins in time."

Jane told her that Bingley had known nothing of her stay in London in the winter. When he understood it, he had been astonished and hurt. It was his sisters who had kept it from him. But he had now discovered the truth, and he loved her more than ever.

Mrs Bennet could not find words warm enough for her joy. Mr Bennet's voice showed how really happy he was. "You are a good girl," he said to Jane, "and I have great pleasure in thinking you will be so happily settled. I have not a doubt of your doing very well together. You are each of you so complying that nothing will ever be resolved on, so easy that every servant will cheat you, and so generous that you will always exceed your income."

Within a short time the whole of Meryton declared the Bennets to be the luckiest family in the world, though only a few weeks before, when Lydia had first run away, they had been generally proved to be marked out for misfortune.

About a week later, as the family sat in the dining room, they heard the sound of a carriage. It was not one they knew. To their astonishment, the visitor was Lady Catherine de Bourgh.

She entered the room with an air more than usually ungracious, made no other reply to Elizabeth's greeting than a slight bow, and sat down without saying a word. After a short and cold conversation with Mrs Bennet, she asked Elizabeth to walk with her in the little wood beside the lawn.

"You can be at no loss, Miss Bennet, to understand the reason of my journey here," she began, as soon as they were alone. "A report of a most alarming nature reached me two days ago. I was told that you, Miss Elizabeth Bennet, would in all likelihood be soon afterwards united to my nephew, Mr Darcy. Though I know it must be a scandalous falsehood, I instantly resolved on setting off for this place, that I might make my sentiments known to you."

"If you believed it impossible to be true," said Elizabeth, colouring with astonishment and disdain, "I wonder you took the trouble of coming so far. What could your ladyship propose by it?"

"Miss Bennet, I insist on being satisfied. Has he, has my nephew, made you an offer of marriage?"

"Your ladyship has declared it to be impossible."

"It ought to be so. It must be so, while he retains the use of his reason. But your arts and allurements may, in a moment of infatuation, have made him forget what he owes to himself and to all his family. This match can never take place. Mr Darcy is engaged to my daughter. From their infancy, they have been intended for each other. It was the favourite wish of his mother, as well as of hers. And now, at the moment when the wishes of both sisters would be accomplished, is their marriage to be prevented by a young woman of inferior birth, of no importance in the world, and wholly unallied to the family?"

"If Mr Darcy is neither by honour nor inclination confined to his cousin, why is not he to make another choice? And if I am that choice, why may not I accept him?"

"Obstinate, headstrong girl! I am ashamed of you! Is this your gratitude for my attentions to you last spring? Tell me once for all, are you engaged to him?"

Though Elizabeth would not, for the mere purpose of obliging Lady Catherine, have answered this question, she could not but say, after a moment's thought, "I am not."

Lady Catherine seemed pleased. "And will you promise me never to enter into such an engagement?"

"I will make no promise of the kind."

"Miss Bennet, I am shocked and astonished. I expected to find a more reasonable young woman. Do not deceive yourself into a belief that I will ever give way. I am not used to submit to any person's whims. I am not in the habit of brooking disappointment."

"You have widely mistaken my character, if you think I can be worked on by such persuasions as these. I am only resolved to act in that manner which will, in my own opinion, constitute my happiness, without reference to you, or to any person so wholly unconnected with me."

"And is such a girl to be my nephew's sister? Is her husband, the son of his late father's steward, to be his brother? Heaven and earth! Of what are you thinking? Are the shades of Pemberley to be thus polluted?"

"You can now have nothing further to say," Elizabeth resentfully answered. "You have insulted me in every possible method. I must beg to return to the house."

"I take no leave of you, Miss Bennet. I send no compliments to your mother. You deserve no such attention. I am most seriously displeased."

And with that, her ladyship got into her carriage and drove away.

Chapter 16: Mr Darcy Asks Again

The next morning Mr Bennet called Elizabeth into his library, with a letter in his hand and a look of great amusement.

"Lizzy, I did not know I had two daughters on the brink of matrimony. Let me congratulate you on a very important conquest. This letter is from Mr Collins. He warns me that you are about to marry his patron's nephew, and that Lady Catherine does not look on the match with a friendly eye. Mr Darcy, of all men! Who never looks at any woman but to see a blemish, and who probably never looked at you in his life! It is admirable! For what do we live, but to make sport for our neighbours, and laugh at them in our turn?"

Elizabeth tried to join in her father's laughter, but she could only force one most reluctant smile. Never had his wit been directed in a manner so little agreeable to her. She was afraid that he had seen too little, or that she might have fancied too much.

A few days later, Bingley brought Darcy to Longbourn again. They all went out walking, and Elizabeth soon found herself alone with Mr Darcy. Bingley and Jane had let the others outstrip them, and Kitty had gone to call on the Lucases. Now was the moment for her resolution to be executed, and while her courage was high, she spoke.

"Mr Darcy, I am a very selfish creature. For the sake of giving relief to my own feelings, I care not how much I may be wounding yours. I can no longer help thanking you for your unexampled kindness to my poor sister. Ever since I have known it, I have been most anxious to acknowledge to you how gratefully I feel it. Were it known to the rest of my family, I should not have merely my own gratitude to express."

"I am sorry, exceedingly sorry," replied Darcy, in a tone of surprise and emotion, "that you have ever been informed of what may have given you uneasiness. I did not think Mrs Gardiner was so little to be trusted."

"You must not blame my aunt. Lydia's thoughtlessness first betrayed you. Let me thank you again and again, in the name of all my family."

"If you will thank me," he replied, "let it be for yourself alone. That the wish of giving happiness to you might add force to the other inducements which led me on, I shall not attempt to deny. But your family owe me nothing. Much as I respect them, I believe I thought only of you."

Elizabeth was too much embarrassed to say a word. After a short pause, her companion added, "You are too generous to trifle with me. If your feelings are still what they were last April, tell me so at once. My affections and wishes are unchanged, but one word from you will silence me on this subject for ever."

Elizabeth, feeling all the more than common awkwardness and anxiety of his situation, now forced herself to speak. She gave him to understand that her feelings had undergone so material a change since the period to which he alluded, as to make her receive with gratitude and pleasure his present assurances.

The happiness which this reply produced was such as he had probably never felt before. He expressed himself on the occasion as sensibly and as warmly as a man violently in love can be supposed to do. Had Elizabeth been able to meet his eye, she might have seen how well the expression of heartfelt delight suited him. But though she could not look, she could listen, and he told her of feelings which, in proving of what importance she was to him, made his affection every moment more valuable.

They walked on, without knowing in what direction. There was too much to be thought, and felt, and said, for attention to any other objects. She soon learnt that they owed their present happiness to Lady Catherine. On her way home, her ladyship had called on her nephew and told him every word of her conversation with Elizabeth, hoping to obtain from him the promise that Elizabeth had refused.

"It taught me to hope," said he, "as I had scarcely ever allowed myself to hope before. I knew enough of your disposition to be certain that, had you been absolutely, irrevocably decided against me, you would have acknowledged it to Lady Catherine frankly and openly."

Elizabeth coloured and laughed as she replied, "Yes, you know enough of my frankness to believe me capable of that. After abusing you so abominably to your face, I could have no scruple in abusing you to all your relations."

"What did you say of me that I did not deserve? Your reproof, so well applied, I shall never forget. Behave in a more gentlemanlike manner, you said. You know not, you can scarcely conceive, how they have tortured me. Though it was some time, I confess, before I was reasonable enough to allow their justice."

"And the letter? Did it make you think better of me?" he asked. "Did you, on reading it, give any credit to its contents?"

She explained what its effect on her had been, and how gradually all her former prejudices had been removed.

"I have been a selfish being all my life, in practice, though not in principle," he said. "As a child I was taught what was right, but I was not taught to correct my temper. I was given good principles, but left to follow them in pride and conceit. Such I was, from eight to eight and twenty. And such I might still have been but for you, dearest, loveliest Elizabeth! What do I not owe you! You taught me a lesson, hard indeed at first, but most advantageous. By you, I was properly humbled. I came to you without a doubt of my reception. You showed me how insufficient were all my pretensions to please a woman worthy of being pleased."

"How could you begin?" asked Elizabeth later, laughing. "I can comprehend your going on charmingly, when you had once made a beginning. But what could set you off in the first place?"

"I cannot fix on the hour, or the spot, or the look, or the words, which laid the foundation. It is too long ago. I was in the middle before I knew that I had begun."

That evening, Elizabeth told Jane, who at first would not believe her. "You are joking, Lizzy. This cannot be! Engaged to Mr Darcy! No, no, you shall not deceive me. I know it to be impossible."

"This is a wretched beginning indeed! My sole dependence was on you, and I am sure nobody else will believe me, if you do not. Yet, indeed, I am in earnest. I speak nothing but the truth. He still loves me, and we are engaged."

It was harder to tell her father. Mr Bennet was not pleased. He was worried.

"Lizzy," said he, "what are you doing? Are you out of your senses, to be accepting this man? Have not you always hated him? I know that you could be neither happy nor respectable, unless you truly esteemed your husband. My child, let me not have the grief of seeing you unable to respect your partner in life."

Elizabeth, still more affected, was earnest and solemn in her reply. She explained that she loved him. She told him what Mr Darcy had done for Lydia, and how her opinion of him had changed little by little over many months. At last her father was convinced.

"Well, my dear," said he, when she ceased speaking, "I have no more to say. If this be the case, he deserves you. I could not have parted with you, my Lizzy, to anyone less worthy."

Mrs Bennet, when she heard the news, sat quite still and was unable to say a word. Then she began to recover.

"Good gracious! Lord bless me! Only think! Dear me! Mr Darcy! Who would have thought it! And is it really true? Oh, my sweetest Lizzy! How rich and how great you will be! What pin money, what jewels, what carriages you will have! Jane's is nothing to it, nothing at all. Ten thousand a year! Oh, Lord! What will become of me? I shall go distracted."

Jane and Elizabeth were married on the same day. Mrs Bennet was happy for the rest of her life, though, perhaps luckily for her husband, she remained occasionally nervous and invariably silly. Mr Bennet missed his second daughter exceedingly, and he often visited Pemberley, especially when he was least expected.

Bingley and Jane stayed at Netherfield for only a year. So near a neighbourhood to her mother and the Meryton relations was not desirable even to his easy temper or her affectionate heart. He bought an estate in a county near Derbyshire, and Jane and Elizabeth, in addition to every other source of happiness, were within thirty miles of each other.

Kitty spent most of her time with her two elder sisters, and in society so superior to what she had generally known, she became less irritable, less ignorant and less insipid. Mary stayed at home and was obliged to mix more with the world. Lydia and Wickham moved from place to place, always living beyond their means. His affection for her soon sank into indifference, and hers lasted a little longer. Elizabeth sometimes sent Lydia a little money from her own private expenses, but Wickham was never welcome at Pemberley.

Georgiana loved her new sister, and the two became the closest of friends. At first she listened with astonishment, almost with alarm, to Elizabeth's lively, teasing way of talking to her brother. He, who had always inspired in herself a respect which almost overcame her affection, she now saw the object of open pleasantry. But she soon learnt that a woman may take liberties with her husband which a brother will not always allow in a sister more than ten years younger than himself.

Lady Catherine was extremely angry at the marriage of her nephew. She wrote him a letter so abusive, especially of Elizabeth, that for some time all communication was at an end. But at length, by Elizabeth's persuasion, he was prevailed on to forgive the offence. After a little further resistance on the part of his aunt, her resentment gave way. She condescended to visit them at Pemberley, in spite of the pollution which its woods had received, not merely from the presence of such a mistress, but the visits of her uncle and aunt from the city.

With the Gardiners they were always on the most intimate terms. Darcy, as well as Elizabeth, really loved them, and they were both ever sensible of the warmest gratitude towards the persons who, by bringing her into Derbyshire, had been the means of uniting them.
