Chapter 1: The Road to the Castle

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 3 May, Bistritz

I left Munich at 8.35 in the evening on the first of May and arrived in Vienna early the next morning. From there the train took me east, and the further we went, the less punctual it became. Buda-Pesth seemed a wonderful place from the window of the train, and I had the impression that I was leaving the West and entering the East. I write these notes so that I can tell Mina everything when I get home. She will laugh at my spelling of the names.

I am a solicitor, or almost one, since I passed my examinations only a few weeks ago. My employer, Mr Hawkins of Exeter, has sent me to Transylvania to meet a nobleman called Count Dracula. The Count wants to buy a house in London, and I am bringing him the papers. Before I left, I spent some hours in the British Museum looking for his castle on the maps. I could not find it. It lies, I believe, in the far east of the country, close to the borders of three states, in the middle of the Carpathian mountains. It is one of the wildest and least known parts of Europe.

At Bistritz I stayed at the Golden Krone Hotel, which the Count had chosen for me. When I arrived, an elderly woman in a white apron came to the door. She looked at me and said, "The Herr Englishman?"

"Yes," I said. "Jonathan Harker."

She smiled and gave a message to an old man in a white shirt, who brought me a letter. It said:

"My friend, welcome to the Carpathians. I am anxiously expecting you. Sleep well tonight. At three tomorrow the coach will start for Bukovina; a place on it is kept for you. At the Borgo Pass my carriage will await you and will bring you to me. Your friend, Dracula."

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 4 May

In the morning I asked the landlord if he knew the Count and could tell me something about his castle. Both he and his wife crossed themselves and pretended not to understand my German. They had understood it perfectly well the evening before.

Just before I left, the old woman came up to my room in a very nervous state.

"Must you go?" she asked. "Oh, young Herr, must you go?"

I told her that I had important business and must go at once.

"Do you know what day it is?" she asked, and when I answered that it was the fourth of May, she shook her head impatiently. "Oh, yes, I know that, but do you know what day it is? It is the eve of St George's Day. Do you not know that tonight, when the clock strikes midnight, all the evil things in the world will have full sway?"

She fell on her knees and implored me not to go, or at least to postpone my journey for a day or two. It was all extremely ridiculous, but I did not feel comfortable. When she realised that I would not change my mind, she took a small crucifix from her neck and offered it to me. As an English churchman, I have been taught to regard such things as in some measure idolatrous, and I hardly knew what to do, but it seemed so ungracious to refuse an old lady who meant well and was in such a state of mind. She placed the chain round my neck, said, "For your mother's sake," and left the room.

I am writing this part of my diary while I wait for the coach. The crucifix is still round my neck. Whether it is the old lady's fear or the many ghostly traditions of this place, I do not know, but I am not feeling nearly as easy in my mind as usual.

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 5 May, The Castle

When I climbed onto the coach, I noticed that a considerable crowd had gathered at the door of the inn, and that every one of them was regarding me with pity. I could distinguish many words which were frequently repeated, and when I consulted my little polyglot dictionary, I discovered that they were not at all encouraging: "Ordog" means Satan, "pokol" means hell, and two of the other words signified something like "vampire" and "werewolf". As we departed, the people made the sign of the cross and pointed two fingers towards me, and a fellow passenger explained that it was a charm or protection against the evil eye.

The scenery was magnificent: we passed green hills, isolated farms and orchards covered with white blossom, while above us rose the immense forests and the snowy peaks of the Carpathians. But as the evening approached, the temperature fell, and the passengers became increasingly nervous, continually urging the driver to accelerate. At every stop they presented me with little gifts, a wild rose, some garlic, a dried plum, without offering any explanation.

At last we arrived at the Borgo Pass, where the driver consulted his watch and announced that there was no carriage, and that the gentleman had better continue with them to Bukovina and return on the following day. While he was speaking, the horses began to tremble and snort, and a carriage drawn by four coal-black horses drew up behind us. The driver was a tall man with a long brown beard and an enormous black hat which concealed his face, so that I could distinguish only his eyes, which appeared extraordinarily bright and red in the lamplight.

"You are early tonight, my friend," he said to our driver. Then he looked at me. "The Herr's luggage, quickly."

His hand caught my arm in a grip of steel, and he lifted me into the carriage as if I weighed nothing. Behind me I heard the passengers praying.

We drove for a long time in the darkness. Once, I lit a match and looked at my watch. It was a few minutes before midnight, and I felt a sudden fear. Then a dog began to howl somewhere in a farmhouse far down the road, and another dog answered, and then another, until a wild howling seemed to come from all over the country. The horses began to rear, and the driver spoke to them softly until they were calm.

Soon a much louder and sharper howling began, close to us in the forest. It was the howling of wolves.

Several times during the night I noticed a faint blue flame flickering among the trees to the left of the road, and on each occasion the driver halted the carriage, jumped down and disappeared into the darkness, returning after a few minutes and continuing without a word. Once the flame appeared so close to the road that I could see him standing in front of it, and, curiously, his body did not seem to obstruct the light at all. I imagined that my eyes were deceiving me.

Then the moon came out from behind the clouds, and I saw that we were surrounded by a ring of wolves. They had white teeth and red tongues, and long, sinewy legs and shaggy hair. They stood silently in the moonlight, and that silence was a hundred times more terrible than their howling.

I shouted and beat the side of the carriage, hoping to frighten them away. At that moment the driver returned. He stood on the road, raised his long arms and spoke a few words in a commanding voice. The wolves moved back, slowly, and disappeared into the forest. A cloud covered the moon, and we were in darkness again.

I cannot estimate how long we continued after that. It seemed to me that we were travelling repeatedly round the same stretch of road, as if the driver were deliberately delaying our arrival. At last we entered the courtyard of a vast ruined castle, whose tall black windows showed no light and whose broken battlements stood out in a jagged line against the moonlit sky.

Chapter 2: The Count

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 5 May, continued

The driver jumped down and helped me out of the carriage. Again I noticed his prodigious strength; his hand was like a steel vice that could have crushed mine if he had chosen. He put my bags on the ground beside me, drove away and vanished with the horses into a dark opening.

I stood in silence in front of a great old door studded with large iron nails. There was no bell or knocker. I began to wonder whether I was dreaming. Was this a normal part of a solicitor's work? Mina would not like it. A solicitor's clerk sent out to explain the purchase of a London house to a foreigner, standing alone at night outside a ruined castle in the mountains! I pinched myself. It was no dream.

Then I heard heavy steps behind the door, and the sound of chains and bolts being drawn back. A key turned with a loud grating noise, and the great door swung open.

Inside stood a tall old man, clean-shaven except for a long white moustache, and dressed in black from head to foot. There was not a single speck of colour about him anywhere. He held an antique silver lamp in his hand, and its flame threw long, trembling shadows on the walls.

"Welcome to my house," he said in excellent English, but with a strange accent. "Enter freely and of your own will!"

He made no movement towards me, but stood motionless like a statue, as if his gesture of welcome had petrified him. The instant I had stepped over the threshold, however, he moved forward impulsively and, holding out his hand, grasped mine with a strength which made me wince, an effect which was not lessened by the fact that it seemed as cold as ice, more like the hand of a dead than a living man.

"Count Dracula?" I asked.

He bowed politely. "I am Dracula, and I bid you welcome, Mr Harker, to my house. Come in. The night air is chill, and you must need to eat and rest."

Although it was late, he insisted on carrying my luggage himself, explaining, "You are my guest, and at this hour my servants are not available." He conducted me up a great winding staircase to an apartment where a brilliant fire was blazing and a table was prepared for supper.

"I have already dined," he said, "and I do not sup."

So I ate alone while he sat by the fire and asked me questions. The meal was excellent: a roast chicken, some cheese and a salad, and a bottle of old Tokay wine. When I had finished, I had a chance to look at him properly.

His face was strong, extremely strong, with a high bridge to the thin nose and peculiarly arched nostrils, and with a lofty, domed forehead. His eyebrows were massive, almost meeting over the nose, and beneath the heavy moustache the mouth appeared fixed and rather cruel-looking, with peculiarly sharp white teeth which protruded over lips whose remarkable ruddiness showed astonishing vitality in a man of his years. His ears were pale and extremely pointed at the tops. When he placed his hands on his knees, I noticed that they were broad, with squat fingers, and, strangely enough, there were hairs in the centre of the palms, while the nails were long and fine and cut to a sharp point. Once, as he leaned towards me, his breath touched my face; it was rank and foul, and a horrible nausea came over me which, do what I would, I could not conceal. The Count, evidently noticing it, drew back with a grim smile.

We were both silent for a while. Then, from far down the valley, I heard the howling of many wolves. The Count's eyes gleamed.

"Listen to them," he said, "the children of the night. What music they make!"

He must have seen something strange in my face, because he added, "Ah, sir, you dwellers in the city cannot enter into the feelings of the hunter."

Then he rose. "But you must be exhausted. Your bedroom is prepared, and tomorrow you shall sleep as late as you desire, since I am obliged to be absent until the afternoon. Sleep well and dream well!"

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 7 May

I have spent two days at the castle, and I have hardly seen the Count by daylight. In the morning I find breakfast ready for me, but I never see a servant. The castle has every luxury: the furniture is old but magnificent, the curtains and the coverings of the chairs are of the richest material, and the plates on my table are of gold. Yet there is not a single mirror anywhere, not even in my room. I have had to use the little shaving glass from my own bag.

In the library I found a large number of English books, newspapers and magazines, and even the London Directory and the timetables of English trains. The Count came in while I was reading.

"These friends," he said, laying his hand on the books, "have been good friends to me, and for some years past, ever since I had the idea of going to London, have given me many, many hours of pleasure. Through them I have come to know your great England, and to know her is to love her. I long to go through the crowded streets of your mighty London, to be in the midst of the whirl and rush of humanity, to share its life, its change, its death, and all that makes it what it is. But I know your language only through books. Here I am noble, and the common people recognise me as their master; but a stranger in a strange land is no one. I desire to speak so perfectly that nobody in London will turn to look at me, or pause in his conversation when he hears my voice."

Then we talked about business. I had brought the papers for an old house called Carfax at Purfleet, on the edge of London. I described it to him: a large property with high walls, many trees, a dark pond and an old chapel beside the house. Near it there is only a private lunatic asylum and a few other houses.

"I am glad that it is old and big," he said. "I myself am of an old family, and to live in a new house would kill me. And I am glad there is a chapel. We nobles of Transylvania do not like to think that our bones may lie among the common dead." He signed the papers and wrote a letter, and then he asked me many questions about the laws of England, about shipping goods by sea, and about how a man could employ a solicitor in one town and another in a different town.

Later that evening he described the history of his family and the innumerable battles they had fought against the Turks, speaking of these ancient wars as though he had personally been present at every one of them. "We Szekelys have a right to be proud," he said, "for in our veins flows the blood of many brave races." He continued talking until dawn, and when the first cock crowed, he sprang to his feet.

"Why, there is the morning again! How careless I am to keep you up so long."

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 8 May

This morning a very peculiar incident occurred. I had hung my shaving glass beside the window and was beginning to shave, when suddenly I felt a hand on my shoulder and heard the Count's voice saying, "Good morning." I started, because I had not seen him enter, although the glass reflected the whole room behind me, and in my surprise I cut myself slightly. I answered the Count's greeting and turned again to the glass, but there was no reflection of him in it. The man was standing close behind me, I could see him over my shoulder, and yet the mirror displayed only myself and the empty room.

At that moment I noticed that the cut had bled a little, and a drop of blood was trickling down my chin. When the Count saw my face, his eyes blazed with a sort of demoniac fury, and he suddenly reached for my throat. I drew away, and his hand touched the string of beads which held the crucifix. An instant change came over him. The fury passed so quickly that I could hardly believe it had ever been there.

"Take care," he said, "take care how you cut yourself. It is more dangerous than you think in this country." Then he seized the shaving glass and continued, "And this is the wretched thing that has done the mischief. It is a foul bauble of man's vanity. Away with it!" With one wrench of his terrible hand he opened the heavy window and flung the glass out, so that it shattered into innumerable fragments on the stones of the courtyard far below, and then he withdrew without another word.

Chapter 3: A Prisoner

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 8 May, later

When I had finished breakfast, I set out to explore the castle, and I discovered doors, doors, doors everywhere, every one of them locked and bolted. Only in the rooms that the Count had shown me could I move freely, and the great front door was also locked, while the key was nowhere to be found. From the windows I observed that the castle stands on the very edge of a terrible precipice, so that a stone falling from my window would descend a thousand feet before touching anything. As far as the eye can reach, there is a sea of green treetops, occasionally broken by deep valleys and silver rivers.

The castle is a veritable prison, and I am a prisoner.

When I understood this, a sort of wild feeling came over me. I ran up and down the stairs, trying every door and looking out of every window. After a while I felt helpless, and I sat down quietly to think. I decided that I would say nothing to the Count about my discoveries. He knew I was a prisoner, because he had done it himself. If I showed my fear, he would only deceive me more.

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 12 May

Tonight the Count asked me a strange question. "Have you written since your first letter to our friend Mr Hawkins, or to any other?"

I told him I had not, because I had not yet had a chance to send a letter.

"Then write now, my young friend," he said, laying a heavy hand on my shoulder, "write to our friend and to any other, and say, if it will please you, that you shall stay with me until a month from now."

"Do you wish me to stay so long?" I asked, and my heart grew cold.

"I desire it much. Nay, I will take no refusal." He then requested me to compose three letters, dated on different days in the future: in the first I was to state that my work was nearly completed, in the second that I was departing the following morning, and in the third that I had already left the castle. I comprehended his intention immediately, for when the letters arrived, everyone in England would believe that I had left Transylvania safely. But I had no alternative, and I wrote the letters exactly as he desired.

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 15 May

Last night, when I was alone, I looked out of my window at the moonlit courtyard and saw the Count's head emerging from a window below me. Then the whole man slowly appeared and began to crawl down the castle wall, face downwards, with his cloak spreading out around him like enormous wings. He moved precisely as a lizard moves along a wall, using his fingers and toes in the crevices between the stones, and I could scarcely believe my eyes. What manner of man is this, or what manner of creature in the semblance of man? I feel the dread of this horrible place overpowering me.

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 16 May

The Count had gone out again, so I took a lamp and once more examined all the doors, until at last, in the right wing of the castle, I discovered one which was not really locked. When I pushed it vigorously, it opened into a beautiful old room carpeted with dust, where, I imagined, the ladies of the castle had sat in former centuries. The moonlight streamed in through the windows, and, feeling somewhat calmer, I seated myself at a little oak table and continued my diary.

The Count had warned me never to sleep in any other part of the castle. "Let me advise you, my dear young friend," he had said. "Should you leave these rooms, you will not by any chance go to sleep in any other part of the castle. It is old and has many memories, and there are bad dreams for those who sleep unwisely." Nevertheless, I was so exhausted that I lay down on a sofa and immediately fell asleep.

I do not know whether what followed was a dream. I suppose I must have been asleep; I hope so, but I fear that it was all too real.

I was not alone. In the moonlight opposite me stood three young women, ladies by their dress and manner, who cast no shadows upon the floor. Two were dark, with high aquiline noses like the Count's and great dark, piercing eyes that seemed almost red, while the third was fair, with masses of golden hair and eyes like pale sapphires. All three had brilliant white teeth that shone like pearls against the ruby of their voluptuous lips. They whispered together and then laughed, a silvery, musical laugh, but as hard as the sound of water glasses touched by a skilful hand.

"Go on!" said one of the dark women to the fair one. "You are first, and we shall follow. Yours is the right to begin."

"He is young and strong," said the other. "There are kisses for us all."

The fair woman came forward and bent over me until I could feel her breath. It was sweet, like honey, but under the sweetness there was something bitter, the smell of blood. I lay quiet, looking out from under my eyelashes, in an agony of delightful anticipation. She licked her lips like an animal. I could feel the hard touch of her sharp teeth against the skin of my throat. I closed my eyes and waited, with beating heart.

At that instant another presence swept into the room like a tempest, and when I opened my eyes, I saw the Count seize the fair woman by the neck and hurl her backwards with gigantic strength, his eyes positively blazing with a red light and his face deathly pale with fury.

"How dare you touch him, any of you?" he said in a low, terrible whisper. "How dare you cast eyes on him when I had forbidden it? Back, I tell you all! This man belongs to me!"

The fair girl laughed. "You yourself never loved. You never love!"

The Count looked at her face attentively and replied in a soft whisper, "Yes, I too can love; you yourselves can tell it from the past, is it not so? Well, now I promise you that when I am done with him, you shall kiss him at your will. Now go, for I must awaken him, and there is work to be done."

"Are we to have nothing tonight?" enquired one of them, pointing to a bag which he had thrown on the floor and which was moving as though some living thing were inside it. I heard a gasp and a low wail, like that of a half-smothered child, and as the women surrounded the bag, I was overwhelmed with horror. Then the room became dark, and I sank down unconscious.

Chapter 4: The Boxes of Earth

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 18 May

I awoke in my own bed. If it was not a dream, the Count must have carried me there, and several small details convinced me of this: my clothes were folded in a manner that was not my habit, and my watch had not been wound. I am now certain that the women are waiting to suck my blood.

A few days later a company of Szgany arrived at the castle. They are gipsies who inhabit this region and who appear to be devoted to the Count. I wrote two letters, one to Mr Hawkins and another to Mina, and threw them, together with a gold coin, from my window to one of the men, who bowed and placed them in his cap. An hour afterwards the Count entered my room with the letters in his hand, perfectly calm and smiling.

"The Szgany have given me these," he said. "One is from you to my friend Peter Hawkins. The other," he opened it and his eyes grew dark, "the other is a vile thing, an outrage upon friendship and hospitality! It is not signed. Well, so it cannot matter to us." And he calmly held the letter and envelope in the flame of the lamp until they were consumed. He had been unable to decipher it, because I had written to Mina in shorthand.

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 24 June

Last night the Count left me early and locked himself in his room. Later I saw him crawling down the wall again, and this time he was wearing my clothes, the very suit in which I had travelled here, and he had on his back the terrible bag which I had seen the women take. Now I understood his plan. He wants the people of the villages to believe that it is I, the Englishman, who is posting letters and doing evil things.

As I sat at the window, I saw tiny specks floating in the moonbeams like dust. They gathered in clusters and slowly took the shapes of the three women. I ran in terror to my room.

Some time later I heard a woman sobbing in the courtyard. She was standing beside the gate with dishevelled hair, pressing her hands against her heart, and screaming, "Monster, give me my child!" as she threw herself against the door again and again. Then, high above, the Count called out in his harsh, metallic whisper, and from far away the wolves answered him. Within a few minutes a pack of them poured through the wide gateway into the courtyard. There was no cry from the woman, and the howling of the wolves was brief; before long they streamed away singly, licking their lips.

I could not pity her, for I knew now what had become of her child, and she was better dead.

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 25 June

I have determined to act. The Count is invariably invisible by day, and he must sleep while others are awake, so that he may be awake while they sleep. If only I could penetrate into his room! But the door is perpetually locked, and there is only one possible route: I must go where he goes, along the exterior of the castle wall.

The stones are large and roughly cut, and the mortar has been washed away by time. I removed my boots and climbed out of the window without looking down. I knew approximately the direction of his window, and I made for it as well as I could, although I cannot tell how long the attempt lasted. At last I found myself standing on the window sill and climbing into his room.

The room was empty. There was a heap of gold in one corner, coins of every kind: Roman, British, Austrian, Hungarian, Greek and Turkish, all covered with dust, as if they had lain a long time in the ground. In another corner was a heavy door which led to a stone staircase. I went down, and the air became heavy with the smell of old earth. At the bottom I found myself in a ruined chapel which had evidently been used as a graveyard.

About fifty enormous wooden boxes lay scattered on the floor, each of them filled with freshly dug earth, and in one of them, on a heap of newly turned soil, lay the Count himself. Whether he was dead or asleep I could not determine, because his eyes were open and stony, although without the glassiness of death. Through all their pallor his cheeks possessed the warmth of life, and his lips were as red as ever; yet there was no sign of movement, no pulse, no breath, no beating of the heart. I bent over him and searched in vain for any indication of life, and then I fled from that terrible place and climbed back to my room, where I threw myself, panting, upon the bed.

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 29 June

Today the Count informed me that I should depart tomorrow, explaining with elaborate courtesy, "Tomorrow, my friend, we must part. You return to your beautiful England, and I to some work which may have such an end that we may never meet." I did not believe a syllable of it. That night I heard him outside my door, and I distinctly heard the women whispering and laughing, while he commanded them: "Back! Back to your own place! Your time is not yet come. Wait! Have patience! Tonight is mine. Tomorrow night is yours!"

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 30 June, morning

These may be the final words I ever write in this diary. This morning I heard a tremendous commotion below: the Szgany had arrived with carts and were loading the boxes of earth in order to transport them elsewhere, and I realised immediately that the Count was departing for England.

I descended the wall once more to his room and went down into the chapel, where the boxes were still standing and the Count was lying in one of them. But his appearance had altered. His white hair and moustache had changed to a dark iron grey, and his cheeks were fuller. The mouth was redder than ever, for on the lips were drops of fresh blood, which trickled from the corners of the mouth and ran down over the chin and neck. Even the deep, burning eyes seemed set among swollen flesh, and he resembled a filthy leech, exhausted with his repletion.

A terrible desire came upon me to deliver the world from such a monster. Seizing a shovel which the workmen had abandoned, I struck at his hateful face; but as I did so, the head turned, and the eyes fell full upon me with all their blaze of basilisk horror. The sight seemed to paralyse me, the shovel twisted in my hand, and instead of destroying him I merely inflicted a deep gash above his forehead. Then I heard the gipsies approaching, and I fled.

I heard the lids of the boxes being nailed down, and afterwards the heavy carts rolling away down the mountain road, so that I am now abandoned in the castle with those abominable women. I shall not remain here. I intend to descend the castle wall further than I have previously ventured, and I shall carry some of the gold with me, in case I should require it later. At least God's mercy is preferable to that of these monsters, and the precipice is steep and high. At its foot a man may sleep, as a man. Goodbye, all. Mina!

Chapter 5: Three Proposals in One Day

Letter from Miss Mina Murray to Miss Lucy Westenra, 9 May

My dearest Lucy,

Forgive me for my long delay in writing, but I have simply been overwhelmed with work. The life of an assistant schoolmistress is sometimes exhausting. I am longing to be with you, and by the sea, where we can talk together freely and build our castles in the air. I have been working very hard lately, because I want to keep up with Jonathan's studies, and I have been practising shorthand very assiduously. When we are married, I shall be able to be useful to him, and if I can stenograph well enough, I can take down what he wants to say in this way and write it out for him on the typewriter.

I have just had a few hurried lines from Jonathan from Transylvania. He is well and will be returning in about a week. I am longing to hear all his news. It must be so nice to see strange countries. I wonder if we, I mean Jonathan and I, shall ever see them together.

Tell me all the news of yourself. There is a rumour that you have been seen with a tall, handsome, curly-haired man. Is it true? Your loving Mina.

Letter from Lucy Westenra to Mina Murray, 24 May

My dearest Mina,

Thanks, and thanks, and thanks again for your sweet letter; it was so delightful to be able to tell you everything. My dear, it never rains but it pours. Here I am, who shall be twenty in September, and yet I had never received a proposal until today, not a genuine proposal, and today I received three. Just imagine! Three proposals in a single day! Isn't it awful? I feel sorry, really and truly sorry, for two of the poor fellows.

The first arrived just before lunch. It was Dr John Seward, the director of the lunatic asylum, with the strong jaw and the intelligent forehead. He appeared perfectly calm on the surface, but he was evidently nervous, for he kept playing with a surgical lancet in a manner that almost made me scream. He explained how precious I had become to him, although our acquaintance was so short, and he described what his existence would be if I were there to encourage and support him. When he saw that I was crying, he called himself a brute and said that he would not add to my present distress. Then he enquired whether I could ever learn to love him, and when I shook my head, his hands trembled visibly. He asked me whether I already cared for anyone else, and I considered it my duty to confess that there was someone. He rose, took both my hands in his, and said that he hoped I would be happy, and that if I ever needed a friend, I must consider him one of my most devoted.

Oh, Mina, I can't help crying. Why are men so noble when we women are so little worthy of them?

The second came after lunch. He is an American from Texas, called Quincey P. Morris, and he looks so young and so fresh that it seems almost impossible that he has been to so many places and has had such adventures. He was very cheerful, and he talks in a delightful American slang, which I am sure he exaggerates on purpose to make me laugh.

"Miss Lucy," he said, "I know I ain't good enough to regulate the fixin's of your little shoes, but won't you just hitch up alongside of me and let us go down the long road together, driving in double harness?"

He was so good-humoured that it seemed not half as difficult to refuse him as it had been to refuse poor Dr Seward. When I explained, as gently as I could, that my heart was already promised, he became quite serious, took my hand and said, "Little girl, your honesty and pluck have made me a friend, and that's rarer than a lover. Won't you give me one kiss? It'll be something to keep off the darkness now and then." I kissed him, and he said, "Little girl, I hold your hand, and you've kissed me, and if these things don't make us friends, nothing ever will. Goodbye." Then he picked up his hat and walked directly out of the door, without a tear, a quiver or a moment's hesitation.

Why can't they let a girl marry three men, or as many as want her, and save all this trouble? But this is heresy, and I must not say it.

Well, my dear, number three arrived in the evening, and I need not tell you who it was. It was Arthur Holmwood, the son of Lord Godalming. He came in, and before I realised what was happening, he had his arms round me and was kissing me. I am very, very happy, and I cannot imagine what I have done to deserve it. I must only endeavour in the future to demonstrate that I am not ungrateful to God for all His goodness in sending me such a lover, such a husband and such a friend. Goodbye. Ever your loving Lucy.

Dr Seward's Diary (kept in phonograph), 25 May

Ebb tide in appetite today. I cannot eat, I cannot rest, so I take refuge in my work. Since yesterday's refusal, I have felt a kind of emptiness; nothing in the world seems important enough to be worth the doing. I know that the only cure for this sort of thing is work, so I went down amongst the patients. I picked out one who has afforded me a study of much interest. He is so quaint in his ideas, and so unlike the normal lunatic, that I have determined to understand him as well as I can.

His name is R. M. Renfield, and he is fifty-nine years old. He has a sanguine temperament, great physical strength, and periods of gloom which end in some fixed idea that I cannot yet discover. I think that he is possibly dangerous, and probably dangerous if he is unselfish. In selfish men caution is as secure an armour for their foes as for themselves.

Dr Seward's Diary, 5 June

The case of Renfield grows more interesting the more I get to understand the man. He has certain qualities very largely developed: selfishness, secrecy and purpose. I wish I could find out what the object of the purpose is. He seems to have some settled scheme of his own, but what it is I do not know. His redeeming quality is a love of animals, though, indeed, he has such curious turns in it that I sometimes imagine he is only abnormally cruel. His pets are of odd sorts. Just now his hobby is catching flies. He has at present such a quantity that I have had myself to complain to him.

Dr Seward's Diary, 18 June

He has turned his mind now to spiders, and has got several very big fellows in a box. He keeps feeding them with his flies, and the number of the latter is becoming sensibly diminished, although he has used half his food in attracting more flies from outside to his room.

Dr Seward's Diary, 1 July

His spiders are now becoming as considerable a nuisance as his flies, and today I informed him that he must dispose of them. He appeared extremely distressed at this, so I suggested that he should at least reduce their number, and he cheerfully acquiesced. While I was with him, a horrid blowfly, bloated with some carrion food, buzzed into the room; he captured it, held it exultantly for a few moments between his finger and thumb, and, before I realised what he intended, put it in his mouth and swallowed it.

I scolded him for it, but he argued quietly that it was very good and very wholesome; that it was life, strong life, and gave life to him. This gave me an idea, or the rudiment of one. I must watch how he gets rid of his spiders.

Dr Seward's Diary, 19 July

We are progressing. My friend has now a whole colony of sparrows, and his flies and spiders are almost obliterated. When I came in, he ran to me and said he wanted to ask me a great favour, a very, very great favour. "A kitten," he said, "a nice little, sleek, playful kitten, that I can play with, and teach, and feed, and feed, and feed!" I told him that I would think about it, and I asked him if he would not prefer a cat to a kitten. His eagerness betrayed him as he answered, "Oh, yes, I would like a cat! I only asked for a kitten in case you should refuse me a cat." I refused, and he went away into a corner, gnawing his fingers, with a look of danger in his eyes.

Dr Seward's Diary, 20 July

Visited Renfield very early, before the attendant went his rounds. All his sparrows were gone. There were a few feathers about the floor, and on his pillow a drop of blood. The attendant reported that he had been very sick in the night, and had brought up a whole lot of feathers. My homicidal maniac is of a peculiar kind. I shall have to invent a new classification for him and call him a zoophagous, or life-eating, maniac. What he desires is to absorb as many lives as he can, and he has laid himself out to achieve it in a cumulative way. He gave many flies to one spider, many spiders to one bird, and then wanted a cat to eat the many birds. What would have been his later steps? I almost wish I could follow the experiment to its end.

Chapter 6: The Storm at Whitby

Mina Murray's Journal, 24 July, Whitby

Lucy met me at the station, looking sweeter and lovelier than ever, and we drove up to the house in the Crescent which she and her mother have taken for the summer. This is a lovely place. The little river, the Esk, runs through a deep valley, which broadens out as it comes near the harbour. The houses of the old town are all red-roofed and seem piled up one over the other. Right over the town is the ruin of Whitby Abbey, which was sacked by the Danes, and between it and the town there is another church, the parish one, round which is a big graveyard, all full of tombstones.

This is to my mind the nicest spot in Whitby, for it lies right over the town and has a full view of the harbour. It descends so steeply over the harbour that part of the bank has fallen away, and some of the graves have been destroyed. There are walks, with seats beside them, through the churchyard, and people go and sit there all day long, looking at the beautiful view and enjoying the breeze. I shall come and sit here very often myself and work.

Mina Murray's Journal, 1 August

I came up here an hour ago with Lucy, and we had a most interesting talk with my old friend Mr Swales, who always comes and sits beside us. He must be awfully old, for his face is all gnarled and twisted like the bark of a tree. He tells me that he is nearly a hundred, and that he was a sailor on a Greenland whaling ship in the year of Waterloo. He is, I am afraid, a very sceptical person, and when I asked him about the old legends of the place, he said they were all foolishness.

"Look here, my dear," he said, gesturing with his stick towards the tombstones, "all these stones, holding up their heads as well as they can out of pure pride, are lying. Half of them have nobody beneath them, for they carry the names of sailors drowned at sea whose bodies were never recovered. Do you imagine that on the Day of Judgement they will come tumbling up here, all dripping, with their tombstones on their backs, to demonstrate how virtuous they were?"

Then he pointed at the seat on which we sat. "That seat," he said, "is over the grave of a man who killed himself. Do you not feel it under you?"

Lucy turned pale, and I hastily changed the subject. The old man laughed, but there was something melancholy in his laughter. "Do not fret, my dear," he said. "I am an old man, and death is not far from me; I can smell it on the wind, coming in from the sea."

I am anxious and unhappy, because I have had no news from Jonathan for a whole month.

Mina Murray's Journal, 8 August

The weather had been strange all day. In the evening the sea was calm and the sky glowed red and gold over the West Cliff, but a few fishermen said that a storm was coming. Shortly before ten, the air became perfectly still, and then, without warning, the tempest broke.

Cutting from The Dailygraph, 8 August, pasted in Mina Murray's Journal

One of the greatest and suddenest storms on record has just been experienced here, with results both strange and unique. The wind roared like thunder, and the waves rose in growing fury, each overtopping its fellow, till in a very few minutes the lately glassy sea was like a roaring and devouring monster. White-crested waves beat madly on the level sands and rushed up the shelving cliffs; others broke over the piers, and with their spume swept the lanterns of the lighthouses. Masses of sea fog came drifting inland, so dank and damp and cold that it needed but little effort of imagination to think that the spirits of those lost at sea were touching their living brethren with the clammy hands of death.

In the midst of the storm, the searchlight on the East Cliff discovered a schooner with all her sails set, rushing towards the harbour at a terrible speed. Between her and the port lay a great flat reef on which many good ships have been wrecked. It seemed impossible that she would reach the entrance of the harbour. But, mirabile dictu, the wind suddenly changed, and the schooner swept between the piers and ran straight onto the sand under the East Cliff.

The searchlight followed her, and a shudder ran through all who saw her. Lashed to the wheel was a corpse, with drooping head, which swung horribly to and fro at each motion of the ship. No other form could be seen on deck at all. And then, the very instant the ship touched the sand, an immense dog sprang up on deck from below, as if shot up by the shock, and running forward, jumped from the bow onto the sand. Making straight for the steep cliff, where the churchyard hangs over the harbour, it disappeared into the darkness.

The coastguard was the first to climb on board. The dead man had tied his own hands to the wheel, one over the other, with a crucifix and a set of beads between them. The ship was a Russian schooner called the Demeter, from Varna, and she carried only a small amount of cargo: a number of great wooden boxes filled with mould. A solicitor in the town, Mr S. F. Billington, of 7 The Crescent, came on board the next morning to take possession of them, according to the papers he had been sent.

Mina Murray's Journal, 10 August

The log of the Demeter, which was discovered in the captain's pocket, has been translated and published in the newspapers, and it is the most dreadful document I have ever read. The ship departed from Varna with a crew of nine, and soon after passing through the Bosphorus the men began to complain that there was someone, or something, concealed on board. One by one the sailors disappeared during the night, and no trace of them was ever discovered. The mate declared that he had seen a tall, thin man, who was certainly not one of the crew, standing on the deck before vanishing like smoke. At last, almost insane with terror, the mate descended into the hold with a knife to search for the stranger among the boxes; he returned screaming that he had seen it, and immediately threw himself into the sea. Only the captain remained, and his final words in the log were these: "I shall bind my hands to the wheel when my strength begins to fail, and along with them I shall tie that which He, It, dare not touch. Then, come good wind or foul, I shall save my soul and my honour as a captain."

The funeral of the unfortunate captain took place this morning, and the entire town followed him to the churchyard. The same morning poor old Mr Swales was discovered dead on our seat, with his neck broken and an expression of fear and horror on his face; the doctors concluded that he must have fallen back on the seat in some sudden fright. Lucy is extremely upset. She has begun to walk in her sleep again, as she used to do in childhood, and I am obliged to lock the door of our room at night and to keep the key attached to my wrist.

Chapter 7: A White Figure in the Churchyard

Mina Murray's Journal, 11 August, 3 a.m.

No sleep now, so I may as well write. I am too agitated to sleep. We have had such an adventure, such an agonising experience.

I awoke suddenly, with a horrible sense of fear upon me and a feeling of emptiness around me. The room was so dark that I could not distinguish Lucy's bed, so I crept across and felt for her, only to discover that the bed was empty. The door was closed, but not locked as I had left it, and since all her clothes remained in their place, she must have gone out in her nightdress. I threw on a heavy shawl and hurried out of the house. The clock was striking one as I reached the Crescent, and there was not a living creature in sight.

When I reached the end of the street, I looked across the harbour towards the East Cliff, illuminated by a brilliant full moon across which heavy black clouds were driving. For a moment I could see nothing, because the shadow of a cloud obscured the churchyard; then, as the cloud passed, the ruins of the abbey came into view, and on our favourite seat, in the silver moonlight, I perceived a half-reclining figure, snowy white. Something dark was standing behind the seat and bending over it, but whether it was man or beast I could not determine.

I flew down the steep steps to the pier and along by the fish market to the bridge, which provided the only means of reaching the East Cliff. The town appeared deserted, for I did not encounter a single person. There are a hundred and ninety-nine steps up to the churchyard, and I ascended them as rapidly as I could, although my knees were trembling and I was gasping for breath. When I had almost reached the top, I could distinguish the seat and the white figure, and there was undoubtedly something, long and black, bending over the half-reclining figure.

I called out in terror, "Lucy! Lucy!", and something raised its head, so that even from where I stood I could distinguish a white face and red, gleaming eyes. When I reached the seat, the dark shape had vanished, and Lucy was alone and asleep. Her lips were parted, and she was breathing, not gently as usual, but in long, heavy gasps, as though struggling to fill her lungs at every breath. In her sleep she raised her hand and pulled the collar of her nightdress tightly around her throat. I wrapped my shawl around her and fastened it with a large safety pin, and I believe that in my haste I must have pricked her with it, because afterwards I noticed two tiny red points on her throat, like pinpricks, and a single drop of blood on the band of her nightdress.

I woke her as gently as I could and brought her home. She was trembling, and she clung to me, but she did not seem to remember anything. We met nobody on the way, and I was thankful for it, because I did not want anyone to see Lucy walking through the town in her nightdress and bare feet.

Mina Murray's Journal, 13 August

Another quiet day, and to bed with the key on my wrist as before. Again I woke in the night and found Lucy sitting up in bed, still asleep, pointing to the window. I got up quietly, pulled aside the blind and looked out. It was brilliant moonlight, and between me and the moon flitted a great bat, coming and going in great whirling circles. Once or twice it came quite close, but was, I suppose, frightened at seeing me, and flitted away across the harbour towards the abbey. When I came back from the window, Lucy had lain down again and was sleeping peacefully.

Mina Murray's Journal, 15 August

Lucy is growing weaker, although she suffers no pain and cannot explain her illness. She sleeps badly, and in the mornings she is pale and exhausted. The tiny wounds on her throat have not healed; on the contrary, they are still open and, if anything, larger than before, with edges that are faintly white. Her mother is not well either, for she has confided to me that she has a weak heart and that her physicians have warned her that she may die at any moment. She does not want Lucy to know.

Letter from Sister Agatha, Hospital of St Joseph and Ste Mary, Buda-Pesth, to Miss Wilhelmina Murray, 12 August

Dear Madam,

I write by desire of Mr Jonathan Harker, who is himself not strong enough to write, though progressing well, thanks to God and St Joseph and Ste Mary. He has been under our care for nearly six weeks, suffering from a violent brain fever. He wishes me to convey his love, and to say that he is sorry for his delay, and that all of his work is completed. He came to us on the train from Klausenburg, and the station master there told us that he had rushed into the station shouting for a ticket for home.

In his delirium his ravings have been dreadful: of wolves and poison and blood, of ghosts and demons, and I fear to say of what. Be careful with him always that there may be nothing to excite him of this kind for a long time to come. He would have written long ago, but we knew nothing of his friends, and there was nothing on him that anyone could understand. Believe me, yours, with sympathy and all blessings, Sister Agatha.

Mina Murray's Journal, 19 August

Joy, joy, joy! Although not entirely joy, for although I have at last received news of Jonathan, the dear fellow has been seriously ill, and that is why he did not write. I am to leave tomorrow morning to join him, to help to nurse him if necessary, and to bring him home. Lucy cried when I told her, but she is considerably better than she was, and her mother will take care of her. She has promised to write to me every day.

Letter from Mina Harker to Lucy Westenra, Buda-Pesth, 24 August

My dearest Lucy,

I found my dear one, oh, so thin and pale and weak-looking. All the resolution has gone out of his dear eyes, and that quiet dignity which I told you was in his face has vanished. He is only a wreck of himself, and he does not remember anything that has happened to him for a long time past.

When I asked him what had caused his illness, he gave me his notebook and said, "Mina, you know, dear, my ideas of the trust between husband and wife: there should be no secret, no concealment. I have had a great shock, and when I try to think of what it is, I feel my head spin round. The secret is here, and I do not want to know it. Take it and keep it, read it if you will, but never let me know." I took the notebook and sealed it with wax, and I have promised never to open it unless some solemn duty should come upon me.

And now, dear Lucy, I must tell you the great news. We were married this afternoon, by his bedside, by the English chaplain. I could not speak for happiness, and Jonathan held my hand as if he would never let it go again. I am Mrs Harker now. Your ever-loving Mina Harker.

Chapter 8: Professor Van Helsing

Letter from Arthur Holmwood to Dr Seward, 31 August

My dear Jack,

I want you to do me a favour. Lucy is ill; that is, she has no special disease, but she looks awful, and is getting worse every day. I have asked her if there is any cause, but she cannot explain it, and I do not dare to ask her mother, because in her present state of health any anxiety about her daughter would be fatal. I am sure that there is something preying on my dear girl's mind. I am almost distracted when I think of her. Please come and examine her as soon as possible, and come to lunch at Hillingham tomorrow, so that Mrs Westenra suspects nothing. Your friend, Arthur.

Letter from Dr Seward to Professor Abraham Van Helsing, Amsterdam, 2 September

My dear old friend and master,

I have examined Miss Westenra with great care, and I am completely puzzled. Her colour is pale and her blood seems poor, but I can find no functional cause for her condition. Her organs are healthy, her appetite is reasonable, and the chemical analysis of her blood shows nothing abnormal. Yet she complains of difficulty in breathing, of heavy, lethargic sleep, and of dreams that frighten her but which she cannot remember. You know more about obscure diseases than anyone in the world. I am in doubt, and so I have done the best thing I know of: I have written to my old friend and master, Professor Van Helsing of Amsterdam. Please come at once. Arthur Holmwood, the fiancé of the patient, will gladly pay any expense you incur. Yours, John Seward.

Dr Seward's Diary, 3 September

Van Helsing has come and gone. He is a seemingly arbitrary man, but this is because he knows what he is talking about better than anyone else. He is a philosopher and a metaphysician, and one of the most advanced scientists of his day; and he has, I believe, an absolutely open mind. This, with an iron nerve, a temper of the ice-brook, an indomitable resolution, self-command and the kindliest and truest heart that beats, form his equipment for the noble work that he is doing for mankind.

He examined Lucy with extraordinary thoroughness and questioned her at length, and afterwards he came to my room looking extremely grave. "This is no jest," he said, "but life and death, perhaps more. She has lost much blood, but there is no anaemia, and no sign of any wound or illness that can explain it. I must return to Amsterdam tonight, where there are books and documents I wish to consult. But you must send me a telegram every day, and if there is any change, I shall come immediately."

Dr Seward's Diary, 7 September

A telegram from Arthur's house brought me to Hillingham at full speed. Van Helsing arrived at the same moment. Lucy was ghastly, chalkily pale; the red seemed to have gone even from her lips and gums, and the bones of her face stood out prominently. Her breathing was painful to see or hear. Van Helsing's face grew set as marble, and his eyebrows converged till they almost touched over his nose.

"It is too late?" I whispered.

"No," he answered. "It is not too late, but we must act at once. There must be a transfusion of blood without delay. Is it you or me?"

At that moment Arthur entered, having travelled directly from his father's sickbed, and Van Helsing examined him keenly. "You have come in time," he said. "You are the lover of our dear miss, and she is bad, very, very bad. Nay, my child, do not go like that. You are to help her. You can do more than any that live, and your courage is your best help."

"What can I do?" asked Arthur hoarsely. "Tell me, and I shall do it, for my life belongs to her, and I would willingly sacrifice the last drop of blood in my body for her."

"My young sir," said the Professor, "I do not ask so much as that, not the last!"

The operation was performed with professional rapidity. Van Helsing gave Lucy a narcotic, and then, with a small apparatus of tubes and needles, he took blood from Arthur's arm and transferred it into the veins of the unconscious girl. It was wonderful to see the colour come back to her cheeks. When it was over, Van Helsing noticed a black velvet band which Lucy wore round her neck. He moved it gently aside and pointed out to me the two little wounds on her throat. They were not large, but they did not look healthy. There was no sign of disease, but the edges were white and worn-looking, as if by some trituration.

"Well?" said Van Helsing.

"Well," said I, "I can make nothing of it."

Dr Seward's Diary, 10 September

Unfortunately the improvement was only temporary. Twice more during the following days Lucy was discovered in the morning white and exhausted, as though all the blood had been drained from her body during the night, and twice more we were obliged to perform a transfusion, first from my own veins and then, when I was too weak, from Van Helsing's. On every occasion the same mysterious loss of blood occurred while she was sleeping, and on every occasion the wounds on her throat appeared fresher. Van Helsing made me promise never to inform Arthur. "It would frighten him and enjealous him too," he said with a curious smile. "There must be none of that."

On the fourth occasion the donor was Quincey Morris, who had arrived in London to visit his friend and who, without being told anything except that Lucy needed it, offered his blood willingly. Afterwards he took me aside and said, "Jack Seward, I don't want to shove myself in anywhere where I've no right to be, but this is no ordinary matter. What took the blood out of her? That poor pretty creature that we all love has had put into her veins within that time the blood of four strong men. Man alive, her whole body wouldn't hold it." He described how, in the Pampas, he had once seen a mare drained of blood in a single night by one of the enormous bats which the natives call vampires. "If I can only discover who or what is responsible," he declared, "you can count on me."

Dr Seward's Diary, 11 September

This afternoon Van Helsing arrived with a large parcel. He opened it and filled the room with a mass of white flowers. "These are for you, Miss Lucy," he said.

"For me? Oh, Dr Van Helsing!"

"Yes, my dear, but not for you to play with. These are medicines." He rubbed the flowers carefully over the window sashes and round the door, and finally he made a wreath of them and placed it round her neck. They were garlic flowers. Lucy laughed and made a face, but she promised to wear them. When we had left the room, I said to him, "Well, Professor, I know you always have a reason for what you do, but this certainly puzzles me. If a sceptic were present, he would declare that you were working some spell to exclude an evil spirit."

"Perhaps I am!" he answered quietly, as he began to make the knot which she was to wear round her neck.

Dr Seward's Diary, 13 September

The next morning we returned and found Mrs Westenra in the hall. "You will be pleased with our dear child," she said. "She is still asleep. I looked into her room, and the room was so terribly stuffy, with those horrid, strong-smelling flowers everywhere, that I took them all away and opened the window to let in a little fresh air."

The Professor said nothing, but as soon as she had left us, he threw himself into a chair, covered his face with his hands and began to sob with a despair which I had never previously witnessed in him. "God! God! God!" he said. "What have we done, what has this poor thing done, that we are so sore beset? This poor mother, all unknowing, and all for the best as she thinks, does such a thing as lose her daughter body and soul!" We hurried upstairs, where Lucy lay white and motionless, and the wounds on her throat had opened once again.

Chapter 9: The Wolf at the Window

Dr Seward's Diary, 17 September

An extraordinary event occurred at the asylum this evening. I was engaged quietly in my study, posting up my books, when suddenly the door was burst open and Renfield rushed in, his face distorted with passion. He had a dinner knife in his hand, and before I could get to my feet, he struck at me and cut my left wrist rather severely. Before he could strike again, I hit him with my right fist, and he fell on his back on the floor. My wrist bled freely, and quite a little pool trickled onto the carpet. When the attendants rushed in and we turned our eyes on him, his employment positively sickened me. He was lying on his belly on the floor, licking up, like a dog, the blood which had fallen from my wound. He was easily secured, and to my surprise went with the attendants quite placidly, simply repeating over and over again, "The blood is the life! The blood is the life!"

A few weeks earlier he had twice escaped and run to the deserted house next door, called Carfax, where we discovered him pressed against the heavy oak door of the old chapel, addressing someone who was invisible to us. "I am here to do your bidding, Master," he said. "I am your slave, and you will reward me, for I shall be faithful. I have worshipped you long and afar off. Now that you are near, I await your commands." At the time I could not comprehend it.

Memorandum left by Lucy Westenra, 17 September, night

I write this and leave it to be discovered, so that nobody may by any chance get into trouble through me. This is an exact record of what occurred tonight. I feel that I am dying of weakness and have scarcely sufficient strength to write, but it must be done, even if I die in the attempt.

I went to bed as usual, taking care that the flowers were placed as Dr Van Helsing directed, and soon fell asleep. I was waked by the flapping at the window, which had begun after that sleepwalking on the cliff at Whitby when Mina saved me. I was not afraid, but I did wish that Dr Seward was in the next room, as Dr Van Helsing said he would be. Then outside in the shrubbery I heard a sort of howl like a dog's, but more fierce and deeper.

My poor mother, frightened by the noise, came into my room and lay down beside me. Suddenly there was a crash at the window, and a quantity of broken glass was hurled upon the floor; the blind blew back with the wind that rushed in, and in the aperture of the broken panes appeared the head of a great, gaunt grey wolf. My mother cried out in terror and clutched wildly at the wreath of flowers round my neck, tearing it away from me. For a second or two she sat upright, pointing at the wolf, and a strange and horrible gurgling sound came from her throat. Then she collapsed, as if struck by lightning, and her head struck my forehead so violently that I was dizzy for several moments. Her heart, which had long been weak, had failed, and she was dead.

The room and all round seemed to spin round. I kept my eyes fixed on the window, but the wolf drew his head back, and a whole myriad of little specks seemed to come blowing in through the broken window, and wheeling and circling round like the pillar of dust that travellers describe when there is a simoom in the desert.

The maids entered, terrified, and I sent them to the dining room to drink a glass of wine; but they did not return, and when I went to investigate, I discovered all four lying helpless on the floor, breathing heavily. The decanter of sherry was standing half full on the table, but there was a peculiar, acrid odour about it, and when I examined it suspiciously, I recognised the smell of laudanum. Someone had deliberately drugged them.

I am alone in the house with my dead mother. The atmosphere seems full of specks, floating and circling in the draught from the window, and the lamps are burning blue and dim. What am I to do? God shield me from harm this night! I shall conceal this paper in my breast, where they will discover it when they come to prepare my body for burial. My beloved mother has departed, and it is time that I should follow her. Farewell, dearest Arthur, if I should not survive this night. May God protect you, my darling, and may God help me!

Dr Seward's Diary, 18 September

Van Helsing and I arrived at Hillingham early in the morning and discovered the house closed and silent, for nobody answered the bell. We forced an entrance through a back window, and in the bedroom we found Lucy lying unconscious beside the body of her mother, her throat exposed, with the two little wounds looking horribly white and mangled. Without a word the Professor bent over the bed; then he started up and exclaimed, "It is not yet too late! Quick! Quick! Bring the brandy!"

Quincey Morris arrived soon afterwards, and once again he gave his blood. But we all knew that this time the end was near. We telegraphed to Arthur, whose father had died a few days before, so that he was now Lord Godalming.

Dr Seward's Diary, 20 September

Arthur came in the afternoon and sat by her bedside. As the evening came on, Lucy's breathing became stertorous, and her mouth opened, showing the pale gums drawn back. Her teeth looked longer and sharper than before. In a sort of sleep-waking, vague, unconscious way, she opened her eyes, which were now dull and hard at once, and said in a soft, voluptuous voice, such as I had never heard from her lips:

"Arthur! Oh, my love, I am so glad you have come! Kiss me!"

Arthur bent eagerly over to kiss her, but at that instant Van Helsing seized him by the neck with both hands and dragged him back with a fury of strength which I never thought he could have possessed, and actually hurled him almost across the room.

"Not for your life!" he said. "Not for your living soul and hers!"

A spasm of rage flitted like a shadow over Lucy's face, and the sharp teeth clamped together; then her eyes closed, and she breathed heavily. Very shortly afterwards she opened her eyes in all their softness and, extending her poor, pale, thin hand, took Van Helsing's great brown one, drew it towards her and kissed it. "My true friend," she said, in a faint voice, but with untellable pathos, "my true friend, and his! Oh, guard him, and give me peace!"

"I swear it!" he said solemnly, kneeling beside her and holding up his hand.

Then he turned to Arthur. "Come, my child, take her hand in yours, and kiss her on the forehead, and only once." Their eyes met instead of their lips, and so they parted. Lucy's eyes closed, and her breathing became gradually fainter. Then it ceased altogether.

"It is all over," said Van Helsing. "She is dead!"

I took Arthur by the arm and led him away to the drawing room, where he sat down and covered his face with his hands, sobbing in a way that nearly broke me down to see. When I went back into the room, Van Helsing was looking at poor Lucy, and his face was sterner than ever.

"Ah well, poor girl, there is peace for her at last. It is the end!" I said.

He turned to me and said with grave solemnity, "Not so; alas, not so. It is only the beginning!" When I asked him what he meant, he only shook his head and answered, "We can do nothing as yet. Wait and see."

Chapter 10: The Bloofer Lady

Mina Harker's Journal, 22 September

It seems only yesterday that the last entry was made, and yet how much between then and now. Jonathan and I are home in Exeter. Poor Mr Hawkins, who was like a father to us both, died suddenly last week and left us his house and his business. And today we came up to London for his funeral.

After the service Jonathan and I walked down Piccadilly. Suddenly he gripped my arm so tightly that he hurt me, and he said under his breath, "My God!" He was staring at a tall, thin man, with a beaky nose, black moustache and pointed beard, who was observing a pretty girl in a carriage. His face was not a good face; it was hard and cruel and sensual, and his big white teeth, which looked all the whiter because his lips were so red, were pointed like an animal's.

"Do you see who it is?" whispered Jonathan. "It is the man himself, but he has grown young again. My God, if this be so! If only I knew! If only I knew!" He was so pale and so distressed that I conducted him to a seat in the Green Park, where he immediately fell asleep with his head on my shoulder. When he awoke, he appeared to have no recollection whatever of what he had seen. I must open that sealed notebook and examine what he wrote in Transylvania, for I believe that the moment has arrived.

Telegram from Van Helsing, London, to Mrs Harker, Exeter, 23 September

Terrible news. Mrs Westenra died five days ago, and Lucy died the day before yesterday. They were both buried today. Your letters to Lucy have come to me. I must see you. Van Helsing.

Cutting from The Westminster Gazette, 25 September

A Hampstead Mystery. The neighbourhood of Hampstead is just at present exercised with a series of events which seem to run on lines parallel to those of what was known to the writers of headlines as "The Kensington Horror" or "The Stabbing Woman". During the past two or three days several cases have occurred of young children straying from home or neglecting to return from their playing on the Heath. In all these cases the children were too young to give any properly intelligible account of themselves, but the consensus of their excuses is that they had been with a "bloofer lady". It is always late in the evening when they have been missed, and on two occasions the children have not been found until early in the following morning.

There is, however, a serious side to the question, for some of the children have been slightly torn or wounded in the throat. The wounds seem such as might be made by a rat or a small dog, and although of not much importance individually, would tend to show that whatever animal inflicts them has a system or method of its own.

Mina Harker's Journal, 25 September

Van Helsing visited me this morning. I had expected an elderly man, but he is of medium weight and strongly built, with his shoulders set back over a broad, deep chest. I showed him the diary I had kept at Whitby, which I had typed out, and he studied it with intense attention. "Oh, Madam Mina," he said, taking both my hands, "how can I say what I owe to you? This paper is as sunshine. It opens the gate to me."

Then I gave him Jonathan's journal from Transylvania, which I had also copied on my typewriter. He promised to read it that night.

Letter from Van Helsing to Mrs Harker, 25 September, 6 o'clock

Dear Madam Mina,

I have read your husband's so wonderful diary, and you may sleep without doubt, for, strange and terrible as it is, it is true! I will pledge my life on it. It may be worse for others, but for him and for you there is no dread. He is a noble fellow, and let me tell you, from long experience of men, that one who would do as he did, in descending that wall and entering that room, ay, and going a second time, is not one to be permanently injured by a shock. His brain and his heart are entirely sound. Yours, with the most sincere admiration, Abraham Van Helsing.

Dr Seward's Diary, 26 September

Van Helsing came into my room this evening with the Westminster Gazette in his hand and asked me what I made of it.

"The children were bitten by some animal," I said, "or perhaps by the same thing that took Lucy's blood."

"You are a clever man, friend John," he answered. "You reason well, and your wit is bold, but you are too prejudiced. You do not let your eyes see nor your ears hear, and whatever lies outside your daily experience is of no account to you. Do you not think that there are things which you cannot understand, and yet which exist?" He paused, and then he continued very gravely: "These wounds in the throats of the children were made by Miss Lucy!"

I stood up in anger. "Dr Van Helsing, are you mad?"

"Would I were!" he said. "Madness were easy to bear compared with truth like this. Tonight you shall come with me, and you shall see for yourself."

That night we went to the churchyard where Lucy lay in the tomb of her family. Van Helsing opened the door of the vault with a key he had obtained from the undertaker, and we went in. It was very dark, and the air was heavy with the odour of dead flowers. He opened the coffin with a screwdriver and a small saw, and held the candle over it.

The coffin was empty.

"Perhaps a body snatcher," I suggested, but I did not believe it myself. We waited outside among the yew trees until dawn. Just before morning I saw a white figure moving among the graves, and then a dark shape that seemed to be a child. When we reached the spot, we found a little boy lying asleep on the path. We examined his throat and found that it was unharmed. We left him where a policeman would find him, and went home in silence.

The next day we returned at noon. We opened the coffin again, and this time Lucy was lying in it, more radiantly beautiful than ever. I could not believe that she was dead. The lips were red, nay redder than before, and on the cheeks was a delicate bloom.

"Are you convinced now?" enquired the Professor, drawing back the dead lips to reveal the white teeth. "See," he continued, "they are even sharper than before. With this and this," he indicated one of the canine teeth, "the little children can be bitten. Are you of belief now, friend John?"

Once more argumentative hostility woke within me. I could not accept such an overwhelming idea. "She may have been placed here since last night."

"Indeed? That is so, and by whom?"

"I do not know. Someone has done it."

"And yet she has been dead one week. Most peoples in that time would not look so." I had no answer to this.

Chapter 11: The Tomb

Dr Seward's Diary, 28 September

Van Helsing has now explained his terrible purpose. Lucy, he says, has become one of the Un-Dead, and with that transformation comes the curse of immortality: such creatures cannot die, but must continue age after age, adding new victims and multiplying the evils of the world, since all who die from the preying of the Un-Dead become Un-Dead themselves. The only method of giving her soul rest is to drive a stake through her heart, to cut off her head and to fill her mouth with garlic. Nevertheless, he refused to proceed without Arthur's consent.

Tonight Arthur and Quincey came with us to the churchyard. Arthur had listened to the Professor's explanation with growing horror and indignation, and only his love for Lucy, and his trust in his old friend, made him come at all. Van Helsing showed him the empty coffin, and then he took from his bag a mass of what looked like thin, wafer-like biscuit, which was rolled up in a white napkin. He crumbled it into fine powder and, working it into a kind of putty, filled the cracks of the door of the tomb with it.

"What is that which you are doing?" asked Quincey.

"I am closing the tomb, so that the Un-Dead may not enter," he answered. "It is the Host. I brought it from Amsterdam. I have an indulgence."

It was an answer that appalled the most sceptical of us, and we felt individually that in the presence of such earnest purpose as the Professor's, a purpose which could thus use the to him most sacred of things, it was impossible to distrust. We took our places, hidden among the trees.

Never did tombs look so ghastly white; never did cypress, or yew, or juniper so seem the embodiment of funereal gloom. Then, far down the avenue of yews, we saw a white figure advance, a dim white figure which held something dark at its breast. It was Lucy Westenra, but yet how changed. The sweetness was turned to adamantine, heartless cruelty, and the purity to voluptuous wantonness. Her lips were crimson with fresh blood, and the stream had trickled over her chin and stained the purity of her lawn death robe.

When she perceived us, she recoiled with an angry snarl, such as a cat gives when taken unawares, and then her eyes ranged over us until they rested on Arthur. With a careless motion she flung to the ground, callous as a devil, the child that until that moment she had clutched strenuously to her breast, growling over it as a dog growls over a bone, and the unfortunate child gave a sharp cry and lay there moaning.

She advanced to Arthur with outstretched arms and a wanton smile. "Come to me, Arthur," she said, in a diabolically sweet voice. "Leave these others and come to me. My arms are hungry for you. Come, and we can rest together. Come, my husband, come!"

There was something diabolically sweet in her tones, something of the tingling of glass when struck, and Arthur seemed under a spell. He moved his hands from his face and opened wide his arms. She was leaping for them, when Van Helsing sprang forward and held between them his little golden crucifix. She recoiled from it, and with a suddenly distorted face, full of rage, dashed past him as if to enter the tomb.

When within a foot or two of the door, however, she stopped, as if arrested by some irresistible force. The holy wafer was in the cracks, and she could not pass. She turned, and her face was shown in the clear burst of moonlight. Never did I see such baffled malice on a face, and I trust that such will never be seen again by mortal eyes.

"Answer me, oh my friend!" said Van Helsing to Arthur. "Am I to proceed in my work?"

Arthur threw himself on his knees and hid his face in his hands. "Do as you will, friend. Do as you will. There can be no horror like this ever any more." Van Helsing removed a little of the putty from the door, and we watched with horrified amazement as the woman, with a corporeal body as real at that moment as our own, passed through the narrow gap where scarcely a knife blade could have gone. The Professor immediately sealed the gap again, and after carrying the child to a location where the police would certainly discover it, we returned home in silence.

Dr Seward's Diary, 29 September

At noon the next day we returned to the tomb. Lucy lay in her coffin, looking as she had looked the night before, a nightmare of herself, with the pointed teeth, the bloodstained, voluptuous mouth, and the whole carnal and unspiritual appearance.

Van Helsing opened his bag and took out a round wooden stake, some two and a half or three inches thick and about three feet long. One end of it was hardened by charring in the fire and was sharpened to a fine point. With this stake came a heavy hammer, such as in households is used in the coal cellar for breaking the lumps.

"Brave lad," he said to Arthur, "a moment's courage, and it is done. This stake must be driven through her, and although it will be a fearful ordeal, it will last only a short time, and afterwards your rejoicing will be greater than your pain. From this grim tomb you will emerge as though you tread on air. But you must not falter once you have begun."

Arthur took the stake and the hammer, and once his mind was set on action, his hands never trembled nor even quivered. Van Helsing opened his missal and began to read, and Quincey and I followed as well as we could. Arthur placed the point over the heart, and as I looked, I could see its dint in the white flesh. Then he struck with all his might.

The thing in the coffin writhed, and a hideous, blood-curdling screech came from the opened red lips. The body shook and quivered and twisted in wild contortions; the sharp white teeth champed together till the lips were cut, and the mouth was smeared with a crimson foam. But Arthur never faltered. He looked like a figure of Thor as his untrembling arm rose and fell, driving deeper and deeper the mercy-bearing stake, whilst the blood from the pierced heart welled and spurted up around it. His face was set, and high duty seemed to shine through it. At last the writhing and quivering of the body became less, and the teeth ceased to champ, and the face to quiver. Finally it lay still. The terrible task was over.

The hammer fell from Arthur's hand, enormous drops of perspiration appeared on his forehead, and his breath came in broken gasps. When we looked once more into the coffin, there lay Lucy as we had known her in her life, with her face of unequalled sweetness and purity. It is true that there remained, as we had observed them in life, the traces of anxiety and pain and exhaustion; but these were all precious to us, and every one of us felt that the holy calm which lay like sunshine over the wasted face and form was only an earthly token and symbol of the calm that was to reign for ever.

Van Helsing laid his hand on Arthur's shoulder. "And now, Arthur, my friend, dear lad, am I not forgiven?"

Arthur took the old man's hand in his and raised it to his lips. "Forgiven! God bless you that you have given my dear one her soul again, and me peace." Then he bent and kissed her, after which we sent him and Quincey out of the tomb. The Professor and I sawed off the top of the stake, leaving the point of it in the body, and then we cut off the head and filled the mouth with garlic. Finally we soldered the leaden coffin, screwed on the lid and, gathering up our belongings, departed.

Outside, the atmosphere was sweet, the sunshine was brilliant and the birds were singing, and it seemed as if all nature had been tuned to a different pitch. Before we separated, Van Helsing drew us aside. "Now, my friends, one step of our work is completed, the one most harrowing to ourselves. But there remains a greater task: to discover the author of all this our sorrow and to stamp him out. Will you all assist me? We have learned to believe, all of us, is it not so? And since this is so, do we not recognise our duty?" Each of us in turn grasped his hand, and the solemn promise was made.

Chapter 12: The Hunters Gather

Mina Harker's Journal, 30 September

We are all assembled at Dr Seward's house, which adjoins the asylum at Purfleet, and the strange coincidence that it stands beside Carfax, the very property which Jonathan sold to the Count, no longer seems a coincidence to any of us. Jonathan has recovered his courage completely since he learned that his experiences in Transylvania were real and not the hallucinations of a diseased brain. "I was in doubt, and then I was impotent and cowardly," he told me. "But now that I know, I am not afraid, even of the Count."

I have spent the whole day typing. Dr Seward keeps his diary on a phonograph, and when I listened to the cylinders that described Lucy's illness and death, I was so horrified and so deeply moved that I insisted on transcribing them immediately. Now everything we possess, Jonathan's journal, my own diary, the letters, the newspaper cuttings and Dr Seward's records, has been arranged in chronological order, so that each of us can read the complete history. Van Helsing says that this collection of documents is our most powerful weapon. "In this matter dates are everything," he said, "and I think that if we get all our material ready, and have every item put in chronological order, we shall have done much."

Jonathan has made an important discovery. He visited the offices of the railway and the shipping agents and traced the fifty boxes of earth which were landed at Whitby from the Demeter. All fifty were sent by goods train to King's Cross, and from there carried by carriers to Carfax, where they were deposited in the old chapel.

Dr Seward's Diary, 30 September

Tonight we held a council in my study, with Van Helsing at the head of the table. When we were all seated, he explained to us, with the precision of a scientific lecture, everything that is known about the enemy we have to fight.

"My friends," he said, "the nosferatu do not die like the bee when he sting once. He is only stronger; and being stronger, have yet more power to work evil. This vampire which is amongst us is of himself so strong in person as twenty men; he is of cunning more than mortal, for his cunning be the growth of ages. He can, within limitations, appear at will when and where, and in any of the forms that are to him; he can, within his range, direct the elements: the storm, the fog, the thunder. He can command all the meaner things: the rat, and the owl, and the bat, the moth, and the fox, and the wolf. He can grow and become small; and he can at times vanish and come unknown.

"But he has his limitations. He casts no shadow, and he makes in the mirror no reflection, as Jonathan observed. He may not enter anywhere at the first, unless there be someone of the household who bid him to come; though afterwards he can come as he please. His power ceases, as does that of all evil things, at the coming of the day. Only at certain times can he have limited freedom: at noon, or at exact sunrise or sunset. He must sleep in the earth of his native land, which is sanctified by the memories of his people, and that is why he brought with him those fifty boxes of earth. Then there are things which so afflict him that he has no power, as the garlic that we know of, and as for things sacred, as this symbol, my crucifix, to them he is nothing, but in their presence he take his place far off and silent with respect. The branch of wild rose on his coffin keep him that he move not from it; a sacred bullet fired into the coffin kill him so that he be true dead; and as for the stake through him, we know already of its peace, or the cut-off head that giveth rest.

"I have asked my friend Arminius, of Buda-Pesth University, to make his record, and he tell me of what he has been. He must, indeed, have been that Voivode Dracula who won his name against the Turk, over the great river on the very frontier of Turkey-land. If it be so, then was he no common man; for in that time, and for centuries after, he was spoken of as the cleverest and the most cunning, as well as the bravest of the sons of the land beyond the forest. That mighty brain and that iron resolution went with him to his grave, and are even now arrayed against us."

Then he surveyed the faces round the table. "Well, you know what we have to contend against, and I put it to you: shall we continue? Remember that to fail here is not mere life or death; it is that we become as him, that we henceforward become foul things of the night like him, without heart or conscience, preying on the bodies and the souls of those we love best."

Jonathan was the first to answer. He reached across the table and took the Professor's hand. Quincey Morris said simply, "I answer for Texas." Arthur and I followed, and Mina placed her hand on ours with a quiet determination that made me proud of her. Thus was our solemn compact made.

Afterwards it was decided, at the urgent request of the men, that Mina must take no further part in our dangerous expedition. Van Helsing said that she had a man's brain and a woman's heart, but that this terrible work was no place for a woman. Mina accepted our decision, though I thought I saw a shadow of hurt in her face. We could not then imagine how wrong our good intentions would prove.

Dr Seward's Diary, 1 October

Late last night we entered Carfax. Arthur had brought his terriers, and each of us carried a crucifix, garlic, a revolver, a knife and an electric lamp, while Van Helsing carried a small envelope containing a fragment of the Sacred Wafer. The house was full of dust, and the air in the old chapel was heavy with an odour of decay, as if the corruption of centuries had concentrated in that confined space. We counted the boxes. Of the original fifty, only twenty-nine remained.

Suddenly the whole place began to shimmer with tiny points of light, and we realised that hundreds of rats were swarming into the chapel from every direction. Arthur blew a shrill whistle, and his terriers rushed in and killed them in dozens, until the rest fled. We sterilised the twenty-nine boxes by placing a fragment of the Host in each, so that the Count could never again use them as a refuge, and returned to the asylum before dawn.

Chapter 13: The Visitor in the Night

Dr Seward's Diary, 1 October

This afternoon Renfield sent an urgent message asking to see me. When I entered his room, I found him perfectly composed and rational, and he spoke with the courtesy and dignity of a gentleman. Quincey, Arthur and Van Helsing came with me, and to my astonishment he discussed politics and philosophy with them in a most intelligent manner.

Then he made a strange and earnest request. He begged me to release him from the asylum immediately, that very night, without explanation. "I can only ask you to trust me," he said. "If I could tell you everything, you would understand, and you would thank me for it. Oh, let me go! Let me go! Do not keep me here, for the sake of your own souls!" I refused, because I suspected that this sudden sanity was only a cunning trick, but his despair was so profound that I have since asked myself, with deep regret, whether he was trying to warn us.

Dr Seward's Diary, 3 October

Let me put down with exactness all that happened, as well as I can remember it. Not a detail that I can recall must be forgotten. In all calmness I must proceed.

Late in the night an attendant summoned me urgently to Renfield's room. He was lying on the floor in a pool of blood. His face was horribly bruised, as though it had been beaten against the floor, and his back was broken. Van Helsing performed an emergency operation on his skull, and for a few minutes the dying man regained consciousness. In broken, gasping sentences he told us what had happened.

"He came up to the window in the mist," he said, "as I had seen him often before, but this time he was solid, not a phantom, and his eyes were ferocious, like a man's when furious. He was laughing with his red mouth, and his sharp white teeth glittered in the moonlight. Then he began promising me things, not in words but by performing them. He sent rats, hundreds, thousands, millions of them, every one a life, and dogs to devour them, and cats too. All lives! All red blood, with years of life in it! And he said, All these lives will I give you, and many more and greater, through countless ages, if you will fall down and worship me! So I opened the window and said to him, Come in, Lord and Master!"

He paused, gasping for breath. "But tonight, when he returned, I realised that Mrs Harker had been with him. She visited my room this afternoon, and she was not the same; she was like tea after the teapot has been watered. I don't care for the pale people; I like them with plenty of blood in them. Then I understood what he had been doing to her, and so, when he appeared tonight, I refused to admit him. I seized him and held him, for I knew I was strong, but his eyes burned into me like fire, and he flung me down." His voice failed completely, and the death rattle began in his throat.

Van Helsing sprang to his feet. "We know the worst now," he declared. "He is here, and we understand his purpose. It may not be too late. Let us arm ourselves exactly as we were armed the other night, but we must lose no time, for there is not an instant to spare."

We rushed upstairs to the Harkers' room. The door was locked, and we threw ourselves against it until it burst open, and we almost fell headlong into the room.

The moonlight was so bright that through the thick yellow blind the room was light enough to see. On the bed beside the window lay Jonathan Harker, his face flushed and breathing heavily, as though in a stupor. Kneeling on the near edge of the bed, facing outwards, was the white-clad figure of his wife. By her side stood a tall, thin man, clad in black. His face was turned from us, but the instant we saw it we all recognised the Count, even to the scar on his forehead. With his left hand he held both Mrs Harker's hands, keeping them away with her arms at full tension; his right hand gripped her by the back of the neck, forcing her face down on his bosom. Her white nightdress was smeared with blood, and a thin stream trickled down the man's bare breast, which was shown by his torn-open shirt. The attitude of the two had a terrible resemblance to a child forcing a kitten's nose into a saucer of milk to compel it to drink.

As we burst into the room, the Count turned his face, and the hellish look that I had heard described seemed to leap into it. His eyes flamed red with devilish passion; the great nostrils of the white aquiline nose opened wide and quivered at the edges; and the white sharp teeth, behind the full lips of the blood-dripping mouth, clamped together like those of a wild beast. With a wrench which threw his victim back upon the bed as though hurled from a height, he turned and sprang at us. But by this time the Professor had gained his feet and was holding towards him the envelope which contained the Sacred Wafer. The Count suddenly stopped and cowered back, and as we advanced, lifting our crucifixes, he moved backwards and backwards.

The moonlight suddenly failed, as a great black cloud sailed across the sky, and when Quincey lit the gas, we saw nothing but a faint vapour. This, as we looked, trailed under the door, which with the recoil from its bursting open had swung back to its old position. The Count had vanished.

Dr Seward's Diary, 3 October, continued

Mrs Harker gave a scream so wild, so ear-piercing, so despairing, that it seems to me now that it will ring in my ears till my dying day. Her face was ghastly, with a pallor which was accentuated by the blood which smeared her lips and cheeks and chin; from her throat trickled a thin stream of blood. Jonathan awoke from his stupor, and when he understood what had happened, his face became grey with horror.

Later, when she was able to speak, Mina related her experience. The Count had entered the room in the form of a mist and, by some terrible fascination, had paralysed her will; he had drunk from her throat, and afterwards he had opened a vein in his own breast with his long, sharp nails and pressed her mouth against the wound. "And you, their best beloved one," he had said, "are now to me flesh of my flesh, blood of my blood, kin of my kin; my bountiful wine-press for a while; and shall be later on my companion and my helper. When my brain says Come! to you, you shall cross land or sea to do my bidding."

We discovered also that he had been in Dr Seward's study. He had thrown all the manuscripts and the phonograph cylinders into the fire, but fortunately another copy of Mina's typed transcription was locked in the safe.

When morning came, Van Helsing, wishing to protect her, touched Mina's forehead with a fragment of the Sacred Wafer. There was a fearful scream. The wafer had seared her flesh as though it had been a piece of white-hot metal, and on her forehead there was a red scar. She sank on her knees on the floor in an agony of abasement and cried, "Unclean! Unclean! Even the Almighty shuns my polluted flesh! I must bear this mark of shame upon my forehead until the Judgement Day."

Jonathan threw himself beside her and held her tightly in his arms. "Then I too will share it," he said, "until God himself shall take it away." And Van Helsing, kneeling beside them, promised solemnly that the red scar would disappear when the Count was destroyed.

Chapter 14: The Count's Lairs in London

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 3 October

We have determined to waste no time. The Count possesses, according to my investigations, several other properties in London: a house in Piccadilly, which he bought in the name of Count de Ville, and houses in Mile End and in Bermondsey, south of the river. Each of them holds some of the twenty-one missing boxes, and as long as a single box remains intact, he has a refuge where he can sleep in safety. Our plan is simple: we must find every box and sterilise it with the Host while he is powerless during the hours of daylight.

Arthur and Quincey went to Mile End and Bermondsey, where they discovered and sterilised twelve boxes. Meanwhile Van Helsing, Dr Seward and I entered the house in Piccadilly with the help of a locksmith, who believed that we had lost our keys. In the dining room we found eight boxes, together with the Count's title deeds, his clothes, his brushes and a bunch of keys. Again we sterilised them, one by one. That left only one box unaccounted for.

Jonathan Harker's Journal, 3 October, later

At about five o'clock we heard a key in the lock of the front door. The Count entered with a quick and stealthy step, with something of a panther in his movements, something so unhuman that it seemed to sober us all from the shock of his coming.

He seemed to realise instantly that he was surrounded, and he sprang forward with the agility of a wild animal. I had taken out my great Kukri knife, and I made a fierce and sudden cut at him. The blow was a powerful one; only the diabolical quickness of his leap back saved him. A second less and the trenchant blade would have shorn through his heart. As it was, the point just cut the cloth of his coat, making a wide gap, whence a bundle of banknotes and a stream of gold coins fell out on the floor.

The expression of the Count's face was so hellish that for a moment I feared for myself. Instinctively I moved forward with a protective impulse, holding the crucifix and wafer in my left hand. I felt a mighty power fly along my arm, and it was without surprise that I saw the monster cower back before a similar movement made spontaneously by each one of us. It would be impossible to describe the expression of hate and baffled malignity, of anger and hellish rage, which came over his face. His waxen hue became greenish-yellow by the contrast of his burning eyes, and the red scar on the forehead showed on the pallid skin like a palpitating wound.

The next instant, with a sinuous dive, he swept under my arm, grasped a handful of the money from the floor, dashed across the room and threw himself at the window. Amid the crash and glitter of the falling glass, he tumbled into the flagged area below. Through the sound of the shivering glass I could hear the tinkle of gold, as some of the sovereigns fell on the stones.

When we ran to the window, we saw him spring unhurt from the ground. He rushed up the steps, crossed the yard and turned to speak to us.

"You think to baffle me, you, with your pale faces all in a row, like sheep in a butcher's. You shall be sorry yet, each one of you! You think you have left me without a place to rest; but I have more. My revenge is just begun! I spread it over centuries, and time is on my side. Your girls that you all love are mine already; and through them you and others shall yet be mine, my creatures, to do my bidding and to be my jackals when I want to feed. Bah!"

With a contemptuous sneer he passed quickly through the door, and we heard the rusty bolt creak as he fastened it behind him.

"We have learned something, much!" said Van Helsing gravely. "Notwithstanding his brave words, he fears us; he fears time, he fears want! For if not, why he hurry so? His very tone betray him, or my ears deceive. Why take that money? He has only one earth-box left, and he must flee or perish."

Mina Harker's Journal, 5 October

This morning, at the exact moment of sunrise, I asked the Professor to hypnotise me. I have discovered that at dawn and at sunset, when the Count's power is weakest, I can share his senses through the terrible bond which he has forged between us. When I was in the trance, Van Helsing asked me where I was and what I could hear.

"I do not know," I answered, in a voice that did not sound like my own. "It is all strange to me. All is dark. I can hear the lapping of water. It is gurgling by, and little waves leap. I can hear them on the outside. Men are running overhead, and there is the creaking of a chain, and the loud tinkle as the check of the capstan falls into the ratchet."

"He is on a ship!" cried Van Helsing. "He is leaving England with his last box of earth!"

Jonathan made enquiries at Lloyd's and in the docks, and discovered that a ship called the Czarina Catherine had sailed from the Thames the day before for Varna, on the Black Sea. A tall, thin gentleman with very red lips and sharp teeth, dressed in black, had asked about it, and had brought a large box to the quay himself, carrying it with an ease that astonished the dockers.

Chapter 15: The Chase to the East

Dr Seward's Diary, 5 October

We have decided to follow the Count to his own country, since he has fled there to escape us, and since Mina will never be free until he is destroyed. Van Helsing calculates that a ship cannot reach Varna in less than three weeks, whereas we can travel by land to Varna in three days. We shall therefore be waiting for him when the Czarina Catherine arrives.

Mina has made a strange request. She insisted that we should take her with us, because her mysterious connection with the Count may be the only means of tracing him. But she also made us promise something more terrible. "If I should change," she said, "and the time comes when I am no longer myself, you must kill me, as you killed poor Lucy. Promise me that, Jonathan, and all of you." With heavy hearts we promised.

Mina Harker's Journal, 28 October

The Czarina Catherine never came to Varna. Every morning and evening the hypnotic trances revealed the same lapping of water and creaking of timbers, and we waited in an agony of impatience. Then, at last, we received a telegram from Lloyd's: the ship had entered the port of Galatz, further up the Danube. The Count, aware of our presence through my mind, had evidently commanded the fog and the winds to carry the ship past Varna. We rushed to Galatz by train, but we arrived too late. The box had been delivered to an agent called Immanuel Hildesheim, who had passed it to a Slovak boatman, Petrof Skinsky. Skinsky himself was found dead in a churchyard, his throat torn open as if by some wild animal.

Van Helsing asked me to study the problem with my methodical mind, and I examined every map and timetable. I concluded that the Count must be travelling by boat up the river Sereth and then up the Bistritza, towards the Borgo Pass, because water is the safest road for one who cannot cross running water by his own will.

We divided our forces. Jonathan and Arthur bought a steam launch and followed the boat up the river. Dr Seward and Quincey Morris took horses and rode along the bank, in case the Count's servants should land and continue by road. Van Helsing and I took the train to Veresti and from there a carriage towards the castle, to meet the others at the end.

Mina Harker's Journal, 2 November

We travelled for days through a magnificent and desolate country. As we drew nearer to the mountains, I became strangely sleepy and lethargic, and Van Helsing observed with anxiety that my hypnotic answers were becoming fainter and more confused. He also told me later that I had eaten nothing for a whole day, and that I had a sharpness in my teeth that he did not like.

On the evening of the fourth day we stopped near the Borgo Pass, very close to the castle. Van Helsing drew a circle around the place where I lay, and he crumbled a fragment of the Host along it. When I tried to leave the circle, I found that I could not pass it, and the Professor looked at me with deep sadness.

In the middle of the night the snow began to fall, and amid the swirling eddies three women materialised, whom I immediately recognised from Jonathan's description: two dark and one fair, with the same voluptuous lips and pearly teeth. They smiled at me and extended their arms, and their sweet, terrible voices called, "Come, sister. Come to us. Come!"

Memorandum by Abraham Van Helsing, 5 November

I write this for my old and true friend John Seward, in case I may not see him. In the morning, when Madam Mina was asleep and safe within the holy circle, I went alone to the castle. I knew that the three sisters lay in their tombs by day, and I must do my butcher work. I found them in the ruined chapel, and they were so radiantly beautiful, so exquisitely voluptuous, that the very instinct of man in me almost paralysed me. It was with difficulty that I tore myself from the fascination of their beauty and remembered my duty.

Then I did my terrible task. The fair sister writhed and moaned and shrieked as the stake went through her heart, and then the others in their turn. But as soon as each one was destroyed, the body crumbled into dust, as though death, delayed for centuries, had at last asserted his rights. I also found a great tomb, more lordly than the rest, on which was inscribed one word only: DRACULA. This, then, was the Un-Dead home of the King-Vampire, to whom so many more were due. I laid in it a piece of the Wafer and so banished him from it, Un-Dead, for ever.

Chapter 16: Sunset

Mina Harker's Journal, 6 November

It was late in the afternoon when the Professor and I proceeded towards the east, from which direction I knew Jonathan was approaching. Far away on the plain below, through my field glasses, I distinguished a group of mounted men surrounding a long cart, upon which rested a great square chest. My heart leaped when I saw it, for I sensed that the end was approaching. Behind them, from the south, two horsemen were galloping desperately, and I recognised Quincey and Dr Seward; and from the north came two more riders, whom I knew to be Jonathan and Arthur.

The sun was sinking lower and lower, and the shadows of the mountains were growing long on the snow. If the cart reached the castle before sunset, the Count would be free and powerful once more. The gipsies saw their pursuers, and they whipped their horses furiously. All around us the howling of wolves came nearer and nearer.

The four men caught up with the gipsies at the same moment. "Halt!" cried Jonathan, and the leader of the gipsies, a splendid-looking fellow who sat his horse like a centaur, waved them back with a fierce gesture. Then Jonathan and Quincey forced their way through the ring of gipsies towards the cart. Jonathan, with a strength which seemed incredible, raised the great box and flung it to the ground, and Quincey, though I saw him wounded in the side by a gipsy knife, attacked the lid furiously with his great bowie knife until it gave way and the nails drew with a screeching sound.

The Count was lying within the box upon the earth, some of which the rude falling from the cart had scattered over him. He was deathly pale, just like a waxen image, and the red eyes glared with the horrible vindictive look which I knew too well. As I looked, the eyes saw the sinking sun, and the look of hate in them turned to triumph.

But, on the instant, came the sweep and flash of Jonathan's great knife. I shrieked as I saw it shear through the throat, whilst at the same moment Mr Morris's bowie knife plunged into the heart.

It was like a miracle; but before our very eyes, and almost in the drawing of a breath, the whole body crumbled into dust and passed from our sight. I shall be glad as long as I live that even in that moment of final dissolution there was in the face a look of peace, such as I never could have imagined might have rested there.

The castle of Dracula now stood out against the red sky, and every stone of its broken battlements was articulated against the light of the setting sun. The gipsies, taking us as in some way the cause of the extraordinary disappearance of the dead man, turned without a word and rode away as if for their lives. The wolves, which had withdrawn to a safe distance, followed in their wake, leaving us alone.

Mr Morris had sunk to the ground, holding his hand pressed to his side; the blood still gushed through his fingers. I flew to him, and the two doctors knelt beside him. With a sigh he took, with a feeble effort, my hand in that of his own which was unstained.

"I am only too happy to have been of any service," he said. "Oh, God! It was worth this to die! Look! Look!" He was pointing at me. The sun was now resting on the mountain top, and its red gleams illuminated my face, bathing it in rosy light. With one impulse the men sank on their knees, and a deep and earnest "Amen" broke from them all as their eyes followed the direction of his finger, for the red scar had entirely disappeared from my forehead.

"Now God be thanked that all has not been in vain!" said the dying man. "See! The snow is not more stainless than her forehead! The curse has passed away!" And, to our bitter grief, with a smile and in silence, he died, a gallant gentleman.

Note by Jonathan Harker, seven years later

Seven years ago we all went through the flames, and the happiness of some of us since then is, we think, well worth the pain we endured. It is an added joy to Mina and to me that our boy's birthday is the same day as that on which Quincey Morris died. His mother holds, I know, the secret belief that some of our brave friend's spirit has passed into him. His bundle of names links all our little band of men together, but we call him Quincey.

In the summer of this year we made a journey to Transylvania and went over the old ground which was, and is, to us so full of vivid and terrible memories. It was almost impossible to believe that the things which we had seen with our own eyes and heard with our own ears were living truths. Every trace of all that had been was blotted out. The castle stood as before, reared high above a waste of desolation.

When we got home, we were talking of the old time, which we could all look back on without despair, for Godalming and Seward are both happily married. I took the papers from the safe where they had been ever since our return so long ago. We were struck with the fact that, in all the mass of material of which the record is composed, there is hardly one authentic document; nothing but a mass of typewriting, except the later notebooks of Mina and Seward and myself, and Van Helsing's memorandum. We could hardly ask anyone, even did we wish to, to accept these as proofs of so wild a story.

Van Helsing summed it all up as he said, with our boy on his knee: "We want no proofs; we ask none to believe us! This boy will some day know what a brave and gallant woman his mother is. Already he knows her sweetness and loving care; later on he will understand how some men so loved her, that they did dare much for her sake."
