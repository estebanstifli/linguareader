Chapter 1: Letters from the North

St Petersburg, 11th December

My dear Margaret,

You will be glad to hear that nothing bad has happened so far. I arrived here yesterday, and my first task is to tell my dear sister that I am well and that I am more and more certain my journey will succeed.

I am already far north of London. As I walk in the streets of St Petersburg, I feel a cold northern wind on my cheeks, and it fills me with joy. This wind comes from the lands I am going to, and it gives me a taste of their icy weather. I try in vain to believe that the North Pole is a place of frost and darkness. In my imagination it is always a land of beauty, where the sun never sets in summer and the sea is calm.

You know my dream, Margaret. I want to walk where no man has walked before. I want to find a passage through the northern seas, or at least discover the secret of the magnet that moves every compass in the world. Can you blame me? Since I was a boy, I have read every book about voyages of discovery in our uncle's library. Our father, on his deathbed, told him not to let me go to sea. But I am twenty-eight now, and I have made my own choice.

For six years I prepared myself. I went with the whale fishers to the North Sea. I suffered cold, hunger, thirst and lack of sleep. I studied mathematics, medicine and the science of the sea. Twice I worked as a common sailor on a Greenland whaler, and the captain offered me the position of second mate. I was proud of that.

There is only one thing I need now, and I cannot buy it with money. I have no friend. When I am full of hope, there is nobody to share my joy. When I am sad, there is nobody to comfort me. My sailors are brave men, but they are not companions for the mind. I need a man who understands me, a man with gentle manners and a strong heart. You will laugh at me, but I feel this lack bitterly.

Archangel, 28th March

Time passes slowly here, surrounded by frost and snow. I have hired a ship and I am collecting my sailors. The master is a person of excellent character, famous for his gentleness. He once gave all his money to a young man so that the young man could marry the woman he loved. My lieutenant is a man of wonderful courage. I could not wish for better men.

I shall sail in June. When shall I return? Ah, dear sister, I cannot answer that question. If I succeed, many months, perhaps years, will pass before we meet again. If I fail, you will see me soon, or never. Do not worry about me. I shall not rush into danger, but I shall not turn back from it either.

The ship, 31st July

My dear sister,

Something so strange has happened that I must write it down, although this letter may never reach you.

Last Monday our ship was almost surrounded by ice. Great flat pieces of it, like white fields, closed around us on every side, and there was only a little water left in which we could float. Our situation was dangerous, especially because a thick fog covered us. So we waited, hoping for a change in the weather.

At about two o'clock the fog cleared. In every direction we saw wide plains of ice which seemed to have no end. Some of my men groaned, and I was beginning to feel anxious myself, when suddenly we saw something very strange.

About half a mile away, a low sledge pulled by dogs was crossing the ice towards the north. A being which had the shape of a man, but was clearly of enormous size, sat in the sledge and drove the dogs. We watched him through our telescopes until he disappeared among the distant hills of ice.

We were amazed. We had believed that we were hundreds of miles from any land. Who could this giant be, and where could he be going in such a wild place?

That night the ice broke up, and in the morning, as soon as it was light, I went on deck. I found all the sailors on one side of the ship, talking to someone in the sea. A second sledge had drifted towards us on a large piece of ice during the night. Only one dog remained alive, and inside the sledge there was a man.

He was not a savage from some unknown island, as the first traveller had seemed to be. He was a European. When I came on deck, the master said, "Here is our captain, and he will not allow you to die on the open sea."

The stranger answered me in English, with a foreign accent. "Before I come on board your ship," he said, "will you be kind enough to tell me where you are going?"

You can imagine my surprise. Here was a man close to death, and he wanted to know our direction before he would accept our help! I told him that we were on a voyage of discovery towards the North Pole.

This seemed to satisfy him, and he agreed to come on board. Good God, Margaret, if you had seen him! His arms and legs were almost frozen, and his body was terribly thin from hunger and hard work. I have never seen a man in such a miserable condition. We carried him into the cabin, rubbed him with brandy and gave him a little soup. Slowly he came back to life.

Two days passed before he was able to speak. Often I feared that his sufferings had taken away his reason. When he was a little better, my lieutenant asked him why he had come so far upon the ice in such a strange vehicle.

His face became very dark, and he replied, "To look for one who ran away from me."

"And did the man you were following travel in the same way?"

"Yes."

"Then I think we have seen him. The day before we picked you up, we saw some dogs pulling a sledge across the ice, with a man in it."

This woke the stranger's interest. He asked a great many questions about the direction in which the demon, as he called him, had gone. Later, when we were alone, he said to me, "I have certainly made your sailors curious. But you are too polite to ask me questions."

"It would be cruel of me to disturb you with my curiosity," I answered.

"And yet you rescued me from a strange and dangerous situation. You have kindly brought me back to life."

Soon after this he asked whether I thought that the breaking of the ice had destroyed the other sledge. I said that I could not be sure. From that moment a new spirit seemed to live in him. He wanted to stay on deck all day to watch for the sledge. I persuaded him to remain in the cabin, because he was far too weak for the cold air. But I promised that someone would watch for him and tell him at once if anything new appeared.

Chapter 2: A Stranger on the Ice

The ship, 13th August

My affection for my guest grows every day. He makes me feel admiration and pity at the same time. How can I see such a noble creature destroyed by misery without feeling the deepest sorrow? He is so gentle, and yet so wise. His mind is educated, and when he speaks, his words are chosen with great skill and flow with wonderful power.

He is now much recovered from his illness and spends a lot of time on deck, watching for the sledge that went before his own. Although he is unhappy, he does not think only of himself. He is deeply interested in the plans of others. He has often talked with me about mine, and I have told him everything without hiding anything.

One evening I spoke to him with great passion about my hopes. I told him that I would gladly give my fortune, my life and every hope I had for the success of my voyage. One man's life, I said, was a small price to pay for the knowledge I wanted to win for mankind.

As I spoke, a dark shadow passed over his face. At first I saw that he was trying to control his feelings. He put his hands in front of his eyes, and tears ran between his fingers. A groan came from his chest. I stopped. At last he spoke, in a broken voice.

"Unhappy man! Do you share my madness? Have you drunk also of that poisonous drink? Listen to me. Let me tell you my story, and you will throw the cup away from your lips!"

You can imagine how these words made me curious. But the effort had exhausted him, and many hours of rest and quiet conversation were needed before he was calm again.

When he had controlled himself, he seemed to despise himself for being a slave to his feelings. He led me to talk about myself again. He asked me about my early years. I told him the story quickly, but it raised many thoughts in him. I spoke of my wish to find a friend, a mind that could share my own, and I said that a man who had no such friend could not be truly happy.

"I agree with you," he replied. "We are unfashioned creatures, only half made up, if one wiser, better, dearer than ourselves does not help us. I once had a friend, the most noble of human beings, and so I have the right to judge about friendship. You have hope, and the world before you, and you have no reason for despair. But I... I have lost everything and cannot begin life again."

As he said this, his face showed a calm, settled grief that touched my heart. But he said nothing more and soon went to his cabin.

The ship, 19th August

Yesterday the stranger said to me, "You can easily see, Captain Walton, that I have suffered great and terrible misfortunes. I had decided that the memory of these evils should die with me. But you have changed my mind. You are looking for knowledge and wisdom, as I once did, and I hope that the satisfaction of your wishes will not be a snake that bites you, as mine has been. I do not know whether the story of my disasters will be useful to you. But if you are going down the same road, my tale may show you the danger. Prepare to hear of events which you would normally think impossible. In this wild and mysterious region, however, perhaps you will believe me more easily."

I thanked him warmly for his trust in me, and I said that I was eager to hear his story. But I also told him how much I wished to help him, if anything could still be done to change his fate.

"I thank you for your sympathy," he replied, "but it is useless. My fate is nearly finished. I wait for only one event, and then I shall rest in peace. I understand your feeling," he continued, seeing that I wanted to interrupt him, "but you are mistaken, my friend, if you will allow me to call you that. Nothing can change my destiny. Listen to my history, and you will see how completely it is decided."

He told me that he would begin his story the next day, when I was free. I have decided to write down every evening, as closely as I can, the words he has spoken during the day. If I am busy, I shall at least make notes. This manuscript will certainly give you great pleasure, Margaret. But for me, who know him, and who hear it from his own lips, how much more interesting it will be when I read it on some future day!

Even now, as I begin my task, his full voice sounds in my ears. I see his bright, sad eyes looking at me with all their melancholy sweetness. I see his thin hand lifted with emotion, while his face is lit by the soul within. Strange and terrible must be his story, and frightful the storm which destroyed the noble ship on its course!

This is the story he told me. I have written it in his own words, as far as I am able.

Chapter 3: Geneva

I was born in Geneva, and my family is one of the most respected in that city. My name is Victor Frankenstein. For many years my ancestors were councillors and judges, and my father held several public positions with honour. Everyone respected him for his honesty and his tireless work for the good of the people.

My father married late in life. My mother, Caroline, was the daughter of his oldest friend, a merchant who had lost all his money and died in poverty. My father found her alone, kneeling by her father's coffin and weeping bitterly. He took her under his protection, and two years later she became his wife. There was a great difference between their ages, but this only seemed to bring them closer. My father loved her with a respectful, almost protective devotion.

I was their first child, and for several years their only one. I can still remember their patience, their kindness and their self-control. I was their plaything and their idol, and something better: their child, the innocent and helpless creature given to them by Heaven. They believed that it was their duty to bring me up to be good, and that my future happiness or misery depended on them.

When I was about five years old, we were travelling in Italy and spent a week on the shores of Lake Como. My mother, who was always kind to the poor, often visited the cottages of the peasants. One day she entered a poor little house where a peasant and his wife were giving a meagre dinner to five hungry children. Among them was one who attracted my mother far more than all the rest. She was a thin, very fair little girl. Her hair was the brightest living gold, her blue eyes were clear, and her face was so sweet that no one could look at her without love.

The peasant woman told my mother the child's story. She was not their daughter. She was the orphan of a Milanese nobleman, and her mother, a German, had died when she was born. The poor family had cared for her, but they had very little. My mother asked if she could take the girl home, and the peasants, who loved the child, agreed because they knew it would be better for her.

So Elizabeth Lavenza came to live in our house. She became more than my sister. She was the beautiful and adored companion of all my games and pleasures. On the evening before she arrived, my mother said to me playfully, "I have a pretty present for my Victor. Tomorrow he shall have it." And when she presented Elizabeth to me the next day, I understood her words like a child and believed that Elizabeth was mine, to protect, love and cherish. No word could express what she was to me. She was more than a sister, for till death she was to be mine only.

We grew up together, and there was never a quarrel between us. Elizabeth loved poetry and the wild beauty of the Alps, the changing seasons, the storm and the calm. I, on the other hand, wanted to discover the secrets of the world. Heaven and earth were the things I longed to learn about, and the hidden laws of nature filled me with a kind of joy that was close to a fever.

My brother Ernest was born seven years after me, and later a little brother, William, the youngest and sweetest of us all. But my closest friend outside the family was Henry Clerval, the son of a merchant of Geneva. Henry was a boy of rare talent and imagination. He loved adventure, and he loved stories of knights and heroes. He wrote plays for us to act in, and he dreamed of doing great deeds for mankind. Henry and Elizabeth, each in their own way, softened my nature. Without them I might have become sullen, lost in my studies.

When I was thirteen years old, we went on a holiday to the baths near Thonon. One rainy day I found a volume of the works of Cornelius Agrippa, an old writer on magic and alchemy. I opened it without interest, but soon my excitement turned into enthusiasm. A new light seemed to shine upon my mind. I ran to tell my father about my discovery.

My father looked carelessly at the title page of the book. "Ah! Cornelius Agrippa!" he said. "My dear Victor, do not waste your time on this. It is sad trash."

If he had taken the trouble to explain that modern science had shown these old ideas to be false, I would probably have thrown the book away. But that careless look did not satisfy me. I continued reading, and when I returned home I found all the works of Agrippa, and later of Paracelsus and Albertus Magnus. I read their wild ideas with delight. I believed that they had found secrets that modern scientists did not know. I dreamed of discovering the elixir of life, which would protect the human body from every disease and make man safe from everything except a violent death. What glory would follow such a discovery!

When I was fifteen, we were staying in our house near Belrive when a terrible storm came over the mountains of the Jura. I stood at the door and watched it with curiosity and delight. Suddenly a stream of fire came out of an old and beautiful oak which stood about twenty yards from our house. When the dazzling light disappeared, the oak had vanished. Nothing remained but a blasted stump. The next morning we found that the tree had not been split, but reduced to thin ribbons of wood. I had never seen anything so completely destroyed.

A scientist who was visiting us explained the theory of electricity, which was new and astonishing to me. His words destroyed my faith in Agrippa and Paracelsus in a single moment. For a while I turned to mathematics, which seemed to stand on firm and secure foundations. It was the last effort of my guardian angel to save me. But destiny was too strong, and her laws had decided my complete and terrible destruction.

Chapter 4: Ingolstadt

When I was seventeen, my parents decided that I should study at the university of Ingolstadt, in Germany. The day of my departure was fixed, but before it arrived, the first misfortune of my life occurred: an omen, as it were, of my future misery.

Elizabeth caught scarlet fever. She was in great danger, and my mother, who could not bear to stay away, nursed her day and night. Elizabeth was saved, but the fever passed to my mother. On the third day it was clear that she would die. On her deathbed she joined the hands of Elizabeth and myself.

"My children," she said, "my firmest hopes of future happiness were placed on your union. This hope will now be the comfort of your father. Elizabeth, my love, you must take my place and care for my younger children. Oh, I am sorry to be taken from you. But these are not thoughts suitable for me now. I will try to die calmly, and I hope to meet you in another world."

She died peacefully, and her face showed love even in death. I need not describe the feelings of those whose dearest ties are torn apart. It was a long time before we could believe that she, whom we saw every day and whose very existence seemed part of our own, had gone for ever.

My departure for Ingolstadt was delayed by these events, but at last the day came. Henry Clerval spent the last evening with us. He wanted very much to come with me to the university, but his father, a narrow-minded merchant, saw no use in education and wanted his son to work in his business. Henry spoke little, but I could read in his bright eyes a firm decision not to be chained to the dull life of commerce.

The next morning I left. Elizabeth, my father and Clerval came with me to the carriage. I threw myself into it and gave way to the saddest thoughts. I, who had always been surrounded by loving companions, was now alone. I had to make new friends and protect myself.

The journey was long and tiring. At last I saw the high white tower of the town. I went to my lodgings and spent the evening as I pleased.

The next morning I visited some of the professors. Chance, or rather the evil influence of the Angel of Destruction, first led me to Mr Krempe, professor of natural philosophy. He was a rough man with a short body, a harsh voice and an ugly face. He asked me about my studies, and when I mentioned the names of Agrippa and Paracelsus, he stared at me.

"Have you really spent your time studying such nonsense?" he cried. "Every minute you have wasted on those books is lost entirely. Good God! In what desert land have you lived, where nobody told you that these fancies are a thousand years old and as mouldy as they are ancient? My dear sir, you must begin your studies entirely anew."

He gave me a list of books to buy and dismissed me. I did not like Mr Krempe, and his lessons did not excite me. I despised the uses of modern science. It was very different when the masters of science looked for immortality and power. Those views were impossible, but they were grand. Now the aims of the scientist seemed to be small and practical.

A few days later I attended the first lecture of Mr Waldman, professor of chemistry. He was quite different from his colleague. He was about fifty years old, with a kind face and a voice that was the sweetest I had ever heard. He began with a short history of chemistry and ended with words that I shall never forget.

"The ancient teachers of this science," he said, "promised impossibilities and achieved nothing. The modern masters promise very little. They know that metals cannot be changed into gold and that the elixir of life is a dream. But these philosophers, whose hands seem made only to dabble in dirt, and whose eyes are fixed on the microscope, have indeed performed miracles. They penetrate into the hidden places of nature and show how she works. They ascend into the heavens; they have discovered how the blood flows and the nature of the air we breathe. They have new and almost unlimited powers."

As he spoke, I felt as if my soul were struggling with a real enemy. One by one, the keys of my being were touched, and soon my mind was filled with one thought, one purpose. So much has been done, cried my soul, but I will achieve more, far more. I will open a new way, explore unknown powers, and reveal to the world the deepest mysteries of creation.

I visited Mr Waldman the next day. He listened with kindness to my story and smiled at the names of Agrippa and Paracelsus, but without the contempt of Mr Krempe. He said that those men had laid the foundations of modern knowledge. He took me into his laboratory and explained the uses of his machines. That day decided my future.

From then on, natural philosophy, and particularly chemistry, became almost my only occupation. I read with passion, attended lectures and became friends with the scientists of the university. Two years passed in this way, and I did not visit Geneva once. My progress was so rapid that the students were astonished and the professors were proud of me.

One of the things that particularly attracted my attention was the structure of the human body, and indeed of any animal that has life. Where, I often asked myself, did the principle of life come from? It was a bold question, and one which has always been thought a mystery. To examine the causes of life, we must first look at death. I studied anatomy, but this was not enough. I had to observe the natural decay of the human body.

My father had taken great care that my mind should not be disturbed by supernatural horrors. I had never trembled at a tale of ghosts. Darkness had no effect on me, and a churchyard was to me only a place for bodies that had lost life. Now I spent days and nights in vaults and charnel houses, the buildings where the bones of the dead were kept. I watched how the fine form of man was destroyed and wasted. I saw how the worm inherited the wonders of the eye and brain.

Then, in the middle of this darkness, a sudden light broke over me. It was so brilliant and wonderful, and yet so simple, that I was surprised that among so many men of genius I alone should discover so astonishing a secret.

After days and nights of incredible labour and fatigue, I succeeded in discovering the cause of life. More than that: I became capable of giving life to lifeless matter.

I see by your eagerness, my friend, and by the wonder and hope in your eyes, that you expect me to tell you the secret. That cannot be. Listen patiently until the end of my story, and you will easily see why I keep it hidden. I will not lead you, unprepared and eager as I then was, to your destruction. Learn from me, if not by my words, then at least by my example, how dangerous is the acquirement of knowledge, and how much happier is that man who believes his native town to be the world than he who wants to become greater than his nature will allow.

Chapter 5: A Dreary Night of November

When I found this astonishing power in my hands, I hesitated for a long time about how I should use it. Although I possessed the ability to give life, preparing a body to receive it, with all its delicate fibres, muscles and veins, remained a work of unimaginable difficulty and labour. At first I doubted whether I should attempt to create a being like myself or one of simpler organisation. But my imagination was too excited by my first success to permit me to doubt that I could give life to an animal as complex and wonderful as man.

Because the smallness of the parts was a great obstacle to my speed, I decided to make the being of gigantic size: about eight feet in height, and wide in proportion. Once I had made this decision, I spent some months collecting and arranging my materials, and then I began.

No one can imagine the variety of feelings which pushed me forward, like a hurricane, in the first enthusiasm of success. Life and death appeared to me as boundaries which I should break through first, pouring a torrent of light into our dark world. A new species would bless me as its creator; many happy and excellent natures would owe their existence to me. No father could claim the gratitude of his child as completely as I should deserve theirs. And I thought that if I could give life to lifeless matter, I might in time be able to renew life where death had apparently given the body to corruption.

These thoughts supported my spirits while I continued my work with tireless passion. My cheek had grown pale with study, and my body had become thin through being shut up indoors. With the moon gazing on my midnight labours, I hunted nature to her hiding places. Who can understand the horrors of my secret toil, as I dabbled among the damp of the grave, or tortured the living animal to give life to the lifeless clay? My limbs now tremble and my eyes swim with the memory. But then a resistless and almost frantic impulse urged me on. I collected bones from charnel houses, and I disturbed, with profane fingers, the tremendous secrets of the human frame. In a lonely chamber at the top of the house, separated from all the other rooms by a gallery and a staircase, I kept my workshop of filthy creation. The dissecting room and the slaughterhouse supplied many of my materials.

The summer months passed while I was entirely devoted to this one aim. It was a most beautiful season; never did the fields produce a richer harvest or the vines a more abundant vintage. But my eyes were blind to the charms of nature. The same feelings which made me neglect the scenes around me also caused me to forget those friends who were so many miles away and whom I had not seen for so long. I knew that my silence made my father uneasy, but I could not tear my thoughts away from my work. I wished, as it were, to postpone everything connected with my feelings of affection until my great object was complete.

Every night I was oppressed by a slow fever, and I became nervous to a most painful degree. The fall of a leaf startled me, and I avoided other people as if I were guilty of a crime. Sometimes I grew alarmed at the wreck I saw I had become. Only my determination kept me going.

It was on a dreary night of November that I beheld the accomplishment of my toils. With an anxiety that almost amounted to agony, I collected the instruments of life around me, so that I might put a spark of being into the lifeless thing that lay at my feet. It was already one o'clock in the morning; the rain beat miserably against the window, and my candle was nearly burnt out. Then, by the glimmer of the half-extinguished light, I saw the dull yellow eye of the creature open. It breathed hard, and a convulsive motion shook its limbs.

How can I describe my emotions at this catastrophe, or how can I describe the wretch whom I had tried so hard to form? His limbs were in proportion, and I had selected his features as beautiful. Beautiful! Great God! His yellow skin hardly covered the muscles and arteries beneath. His hair was a flowing, shining black, and his teeth were as white as pearls. But these luxuries only made a more horrible contrast with his watery eyes, which seemed almost the same colour as the dull white sockets in which they were set, with his shrivelled skin and his straight black lips.

For nearly two years I had worked for the sole purpose of giving life to a lifeless body. For this I had deprived myself of rest and health. I had desired it with a passion far beyond moderation. But now that I had finished, the beauty of the dream vanished, and breathless horror and disgust filled my heart. Unable to bear the sight of the being I had created, I rushed out of the room and paced up and down my bedroom for a long time, unable to sleep.

At last I threw myself on the bed in my clothes and fell into a troubled sleep, disturbed by the wildest dreams. I thought I saw Elizabeth, in the bloom of health, walking in the streets of Ingolstadt. Delighted and surprised, I embraced her. But as I printed the first kiss on her lips, they became livid with the colour of death; her features appeared to change, and I thought that I held the corpse of my dead mother in my arms. A shroud covered her body, and I saw the grave-worms crawling in the folds of the cloth.

I woke from my sleep with horror. A cold sweat covered my forehead, my teeth chattered, and every limb shook. Then, by the dim yellow light of the moon, as it forced its way through the window shutters, I saw the wretch, the miserable monster whom I had created. He held up the curtain of the bed, and his eyes, if eyes they may be called, were fixed on me. His jaws opened, and he muttered some sounds, while a grin wrinkled his cheeks. He may have spoken, but I did not hear. One hand was stretched out, seemingly to hold me, but I escaped and rushed downstairs. I took refuge in the courtyard of the house, where I remained for the rest of the night, walking up and down in the greatest agitation, listening carefully, fearing every sound as if it announced the approach of the demonic corpse to which I had so miserably given life.

Oh! No mortal could bear the horror of that face. A mummy brought back to life could not be as hideous as that wretch. I had looked at him while he was unfinished; he was ugly then, but when those muscles and joints were able to move, it became a thing such as even Dante could not have imagined.

Morning came at last, dismal and wet. I walked through the streets without knowing where I was going, afraid to return to my lodgings. At the inn where the coaches stopped, I saw the coach from Switzerland arrive, and when the door opened I saw, to my astonishment, Henry Clerval.

"My dear Frankenstein," he exclaimed, "how glad I am to see you! How fortunate that you should be here at the very moment I arrived!"

Nothing could equal my delight on seeing Clerval. His presence brought back to my thoughts my father, Elizabeth and all those scenes of home so dear to my memory. He told me that his father had at last allowed him to study at the university. Then he looked at me closely.

"But, my dear Frankenstein," he said, "I did not notice before how very ill you appear; so thin and pale. You look as if you had been watching for several nights."

We walked to my college, and I trembled at the thought that the creature might still be there. I asked Henry to wait at the bottom of the stairs and ran up to my room. I threw the door open, as children do when they expect a ghost to be waiting on the other side. But nothing appeared. The room was empty; my hideous guest had gone.

I could hardly believe that such good fortune could be mine. When Clerval came up, I clapped my hands and laughed loudly in an unnatural way. Henry stared at me in alarm. Then, suddenly, I thought I saw the dreaded figure glide into the room. "He can tell!" I cried. "Oh, save me! Save me!" I struggled furiously and fell down in a fit.

This was the beginning of a nervous fever which kept me in bed for several months. During all that time Henry was my only nurse. He knew that my father was too old for such a long journey, and that my illness would make Elizabeth wretched, so he hid the seriousness of my condition from them. The form of the monster was always before my eyes, and I talked about him without stopping. My words surprised Henry. At first he believed them to be the wanderings of my disturbed imagination, but the constant return to the same subject persuaded him that my disorder had its origin in some uncommon and terrible event.

Chapter 6: News from Geneva

Very slowly, and with frequent relapses, I recovered. I remember the first time I was able to look at the outside world with any pleasure. The fallen leaves had disappeared, and the young buds were shooting from the trees that shaded my window. It was a divine spring, and the season contributed greatly to my recovery. I felt the return of joy and affection in my heart; my gloom disappeared, and in a short time I became as cheerful as before I was attacked by that fatal passion.

"Dearest Clerval," I exclaimed, "how kind, how very good you are to me. This whole winter, instead of studying, as you promised yourself, you have spent in my sick room. How shall I ever repay you?"

"You will repay me entirely if you do not upset yourself, but get well as fast as you can. And since you appear to be in such good spirits, may I speak to you on one subject?"

I trembled. One subject! What could it be? Could he mean the thing of which I did not dare even to think?

"Calm yourself," said Clerval, who saw that my colour had changed. "I will not mention it if it upsets you. But your father and cousin would be very happy to receive a letter in your own handwriting. They hardly know how ill you have been and are worried by your long silence. Here is a letter from Elizabeth that has been waiting for you some days."

Elizabeth's letter was full of tenderness. She told me the news of home: that Ernest wished to become a soldier, and that little William had grown into a beautiful child with sweet laughing blue eyes and dark eyelashes. She also told me the story of Justine Moritz, a girl whom my mother had taken into our house years before. Justine's own mother had treated her badly, but in our family she had learnt gentleness and loyalty. She was now a servant in our house, but she was treated as a friend, and she loved little William as if he were her own child. Elizabeth begged me to write, even a single line, to reassure them all.

I wrote immediately, and my recovery continued. Clerval and I spent the summer studying oriental languages together. Henry loved Persian and Arabic poetry, and I found comfort in the warmth and gentleness of these writers. But I could not bear to enter a laboratory, and the very sight of my chemical instruments brought back the symptoms of my nervous illness. When Mr Waldman praised my astonishing progress in front of Clerval, I suffered terribly. Mr Krempe was even worse. "He has outstripped us all," he cried with rough good humour. "A youngster who, a few years ago, believed in Cornelius Agrippa as firmly as in the Gospel, has now placed himself at the head of the university!"

In May we went on a walking tour of the countryside around Ingolstadt. The fresh air and Henry's cheerful conversation restored my health and spirits completely. When we returned, I found the following letter from my father waiting for me.

"My dear Victor,

You have probably waited impatiently for a letter to fix the date of your return to us. But that would be a cruel kindness, and I dare not do it. What would be your surprise, my son, when you expected a happy and glad welcome, to see, on the contrary, tears and wretchedness? And how, Victor, can I describe our misfortune?

William is dead! That sweet child, whose smiles delighted and warmed my heart, who was so gentle, yet so happy! Victor, he is murdered!

Last Thursday, the evening being warm and calm, Elizabeth, your two brothers and I went for a walk in Plainpalais. William ran away to hide, and we could not find him. At five in the morning I discovered my lovely boy, whom the night before I had seen blooming and active in health, stretched on the grass, livid and motionless. The print of the murderer's finger was on his neck.

That same evening William had begged Elizabeth to let him wear a very valuable miniature, a small portrait of your mother, which she possessed. This picture is gone, and was doubtless the temptation which urged the murderer to the deed. Elizabeth cannot stop weeping; she blames herself unjustly for his death. Come, dearest Victor; you alone can comfort her.

Your affectionate and afflicted father,
Alphonse Frankenstein"

Clerval, who had watched my face as I read, was surprised to see the despair that followed the joy I had first shown on receiving news from home. I threw the letter on the table and covered my face with my hands.

"My dear Frankenstein," exclaimed Henry, "are you always to be unhappy? My dear friend, what has happened?"

I motioned to him to take up the letter, while I walked up and down the room in extreme agitation. Tears poured from Clerval's eyes as he read the account of my misfortune.

I left for Geneva immediately. The journey was long and sad. It was completely dark when I arrived near the city, and the gates were already shut, so I was obliged to pass the night in a village on the other side of the lake. A storm was approaching, and I decided to visit the spot where my poor William had been murdered. I crossed the lake in a boat and watched the lightning playing on the summit of Mont Blanc in the most beautiful shapes. The storm grew violent, and thunder burst with a terrific crash over my head.

"William, dear angel!" I cried. "This is your funeral, this is your dirge!"

As I said these words, I saw in the gloom a figure which stole from behind a clump of trees near me. I stood fixed, gazing intently: I could not be mistaken. A flash of lightning illuminated the object and showed me its shape plainly. Its gigantic height and the deformity of its appearance, more hideous than anything belonging to humanity, instantly told me that it was the wretch, the filthy demon to whom I had given life.

What did he want there? Could he be, and I shuddered at the thought, the murderer of my brother? No sooner had that idea crossed my imagination than I became convinced of its truth. My teeth chattered, and I was forced to lean against a tree for support. The figure passed me quickly, and I lost it in the darkness. Then, in another flash, I saw it climbing the almost vertical rocks of Mont Salève with enormous speed. It soon reached the summit and disappeared.

I remained motionless. The thunder stopped, but the rain still continued, and the scene was covered in impenetrable darkness. I thought about the being whom I had thrown among mankind and given the power to do horrible things, such as the deed he had now done. He was like my own vampire, my own spirit let loose from the grave and forced to destroy everything that was dear to me.

Chapter 7: Justine

Day dawned, and I walked towards the town. The gates were open, and I hurried to my father's house. My first thought was to tell everyone what I knew about the murderer and to have him hunted at once. But I stopped when I thought about the story I had to tell. A being whom I myself had formed, and given life, had met me at midnight among the cliffs of an inaccessible mountain. Who would believe such a story? Besides, even if I could persuade my family, what man could follow a creature that was able to climb the vertical sides of Mont Salève? These thoughts convinced me to remain silent.

It was about five in the morning when I entered my father's house. I told the servants not to disturb the family and went into the library. There, above the fireplace, hung a picture of my mother kneeling by her father's coffin. Below it was a miniature of William, and my tears flowed when I looked at it.

While I was standing there, my brother Ernest entered. He welcomed me with sad affection. "Welcome, my dearest Victor," he said. "Ah! I wish you had come three months ago; then you would have found us all joyous and delighted. You come to us now to share a misery which nothing can relieve. Poor Elizabeth is quite inconsolable. And now that the murderer has been discovered..."

"The murderer discovered! Good God! How can that be? Who could attempt to pursue him? It is impossible; one might as well try to overtake the winds."

"I do not know what you mean," replied my brother in a tone of wonder. "But the discovery has completed our misery. No one would believe it at first, and even now Elizabeth will not be convinced, in spite of all the evidence. Who would have thought that Justine Moritz, who was so gentle and fond of all the family, could suddenly become capable of so frightful a crime?"

"Justine Moritz! Poor, poor girl, is she the accused? But it is wrongfully. Everyone knows that. No one believes it, surely, Ernest?"

But Ernest explained that on the morning after the murder Justine had been taken ill, and a servant had found, in the pocket of the dress she had worn on the night of the murder, the miniature of my mother. This was the very picture which had been the murderer's temptation. When Justine was shown it, her confusion seemed to confirm her guilt.

"You are all mistaken," I said. "I know the murderer. Justine, poor, good Justine, is innocent."

At that moment my father entered. Misfortune was deeply marked on his face, but he tried to welcome me cheerfully. Then Elizabeth came in. Time had changed her since I last saw her; it had given her a loveliness greater than the beauty of her childhood. She welcomed me with the greatest affection. "Your arrival, my dear cousin," she said, "fills me with hope. You perhaps will find some means to prove my poor guiltless Justine innocent."

"She is innocent, my Elizabeth," I said, "and that shall be proved. Fear nothing."

The trial began at eleven o'clock. During the whole of this miserable affair I suffered living torture. It was to be decided whether the result of my curiosity and my lawless experiments would cause the death of two of my fellow beings: one a smiling baby full of innocence and joy, the other far more dreadfully murdered, with every disgrace that could make the murder memorable in horror.

Justine was calm. She was dressed in mourning, and her face, always attractive, was made exquisitely beautiful by the seriousness of her feelings. She seemed confident in her innocence and did not tremble, although she was stared at and hated by thousands. Witnesses described how she had been out all night and how the miniature had been found in her pocket.

Justine explained that she had gone to visit an aunt, and that on her way home she had heard about William's disappearance. She had spent hours looking for him, until the city gates were shut. Then she had passed the night in a barn belonging to a cottage, where she slept for a few minutes. As for the picture, she could not explain how it had come into her pocket. "I believe that I have no enemy on earth," she said, "and none surely would have been so wicked as to destroy me for no reason."

Elizabeth bravely asked to speak, and she described Justine's goodness and her devotion to William and to my mother. A murmur of approval went through the crowd. But when the crowd heard that this was the girl whom our family had treated with such kindness, their anger against Justine grew even stronger, because it seemed to them that she had shown the blackest ingratitude.

I could not bear it. I knew the truth, and I could not tell it. The tortures of the accused did not equal mine; she was supported by innocence, but the fangs of remorse tore my chest and would not let go.

The next morning I went to the court. All the votes had been cast; they were all black, and Justine was condemned. Worse still, I was told that she had confessed. Elizabeth and I visited her in prison, and she threw herself at Elizabeth's feet, weeping bitterly.

"I did confess," she said, "but I confessed a lie. My confessor threatened me with excommunication and hell fire until I almost began to think that I was the monster that he said I was. I signed it so that I might obtain forgiveness. Now only this lie is heavy on my soul. Dear lady, do you believe that your Justine, whom your aunt honoured so much, could do such a thing?"

"Oh, Justine! Forgive me for having doubted you even for one moment," cried Elizabeth.

Justine tried to comfort us both. "I do not fear to die," she said. "God raises my weakness and gives me courage. I leave a sad and bitter world. Learn from me, dear lady, to submit patiently to the will of Heaven."

Justine died on the scaffold the next day. Elizabeth's passionate pleas to the judges were useless. Mine were never spoken, because I knew they would be taken for the ravings of a madman. And so Justine, too, was a victim of my unhallowed arts.

Chapter 8: The Meeting on the Glacier

Nothing is more painful to the human mind than the dead calm of inaction and certainty which follows quick events. Justine died, she rested, and I was alive. Sleep fled from my eyes. I wandered like an evil spirit, for I had committed deeds of mischief beyond description, and more, much more, I was persuaded, was still to come.

My father saw the change in my character and tried to reason with me. "Do you think, Victor," he said, "that I do not suffer also? No one could love a child more than I loved your brother. But is it not a duty to the survivors that we should not increase their unhappiness by excessive grief?"

His advice was good, but it did not help me. I often rowed alone on the lake at night, and more than once I was tempted to plunge into the silent water so that it might close over me and my sufferings for ever. But I was stopped by the thought of Elizabeth, whom I loved so tenderly, and of my father and surviving brother. How could I leave them unprotected against the fiend whom I had let loose among them?

My hatred of the monster could not be described. When I thought of him, I gnashed my teeth, my eyes became inflamed, and I ardently wished to destroy the life which I had so thoughtlessly given. I wanted to see him again, so that I could punish him for the death of William and Justine.

In August, my family decided to go on a trip to the valley of Chamounix, hoping that the change of scenery would distract me from my grief. The grand mountains, the white pyramids of ice and the roaring rivers did, for a time, calm my mind. One morning I decided to go alone to the summit of Montanvert. I remembered the effect that the view of the enormous, ever-moving glacier had produced on my mind when I first saw it. It had filled me with a sublime ecstasy that gave wings to the soul.

The path up the mountain was steep and dangerous. Rain was falling, and thick mists hid the summits of the mountains. It was nearly noon when I arrived at the top. For some time I sat upon the rock that overlooks the sea of ice, called the Mer de Glace. Then I descended upon the glacier. Its surface was very uneven, rising like the waves of a troubled sea, and full of deep cracks. The field of ice is almost a league in width, and it took me nearly two hours to cross it.

Suddenly I saw the figure of a man, at some distance, advancing towards me with superhuman speed. He bounded over the cracks in the ice, among which I had walked with such caution. His height, as he approached, seemed to exceed that of man. A mist came over my eyes, and I felt faint, but I was quickly restored by the cold wind of the mountains. As the shape came nearer, I saw, sight tremendous and abhorred, that it was the wretch whom I had created. I trembled with rage and horror and decided to wait for him and then fight him to the death.

He approached. His face showed bitter anguish combined with contempt and wickedness, while its unearthly ugliness made it almost too horrible for human eyes.

"Devil," I exclaimed, "do you dare approach me? And do you not fear the fierce vengeance of my arm upon your miserable head? Go away, vile insect! Or rather, stay, so that I may trample you to dust!"

"I expected this reception," said the demon. "All men hate the wretched; how, then, must I be hated, who am miserable beyond all living things! Yet you, my creator, hate and reject me, your creature, to whom you are bound by ties that only the death of one of us can break. You plan to kill me. How dare you play with life in this way? Do your duty towards me, and I will do mine towards you and the rest of mankind. If you agree to my conditions, I will leave them and you at peace. But if you refuse, I will fill the throat of death until it is satisfied with the blood of your remaining friends."

My rage was without limits. I sprang at him, but he easily avoided me.

"Be calm! I ask you to hear me before you give way to your hatred. Have I not suffered enough? Remember that you have made me more powerful than yourself; my height is greater than yours, my joints more supple. But I will not be tempted to fight you. I am your creature, and I will be even mild and gentle to my natural lord and king, if you will also do your part. Oh, Frankenstein, remember that I am your creature. I ought to be your Adam, but I am rather the fallen angel, whom you drive from joy for no misdeed. Everywhere I see happiness, from which I alone am excluded. I was benevolent and good; misery made me a fiend. Make me happy, and I shall again be virtuous."

"Go away! I will not hear you. There can be nothing in common between you and me; we are enemies."

"How can I move you? Believe me, Frankenstein, I was good; my soul glowed with love and humanity. But am I not alone, miserably alone? You, my creator, hate me. What hope can I have from your fellow creatures, who owe me nothing? They reject and hate me. The desert mountains and dreary glaciers are my refuge. Listen to my tale. When you have heard it, abandon me or pity me, as you shall judge that I deserve. But hear me. The guilty are allowed, by human laws, bloody as they are, to speak in their own defence before they are condemned. Listen to me, Frankenstein. You accuse me of murder, and yet you would, with a satisfied conscience, destroy your own creature."

For the first time, I felt what the duties of a creator towards his creature were, and that I ought to make him happy before I complained of his wickedness. These thoughts made me agree to his demand. We crossed the ice and climbed the opposite rock. The air was cold, and the rain again began to fall. We entered a hut in the mountains, and the fiend lit a fire with an air of satisfaction. I sat down by the fire, which my hated companion had lit, and he began his tale.

Chapter 9: The Creature's First Days

It is with considerable difficulty that I remember the beginning of my existence. All the events of that period appear confused and unclear. A strange multitude of sensations seized me, and I saw, felt, heard and smelt at the same time. It was, indeed, a long time before I learnt to distinguish between the operations of my various senses.

I remember that a stronger light pressed upon my nerves, so that I was obliged to shut my eyes. Then darkness came over me and troubled me. I walked, and I believe I went down some stairs. Soon I left your apartment, and on a sensation of cold I had covered myself with some clothes. I went into the forest near Ingolstadt, and there I lay by the side of a brook resting from my fatigue, until I felt tormented by hunger and thirst. I ate some berries which I found hanging on the trees or lying on the ground. I quenched my thirst at the brook, and then, lying down, I was overcome by sleep.

It was dark when I awoke. I felt cold, and half frightened, finding myself so alone. I was a poor, helpless, miserable wretch; I knew nothing and could distinguish nothing. But feeling pain invade me on all sides, I sat down and wept.

Soon a gentle light stole over the heavens and gave me a sensation of pleasure. I started up and saw a radiant form rise from among the trees. It was the moon. I gazed at it with a kind of wonder. It moved slowly, but it lit my path, and I again went out in search of berries.

Several days and nights passed. Slowly my senses became clearer. I began to distinguish the insects from the plants, and the plants from each other. I found that the sparrow uttered only harsh notes, while those of the blackbird and thrush were sweet and enticing. Sometimes I tried to imitate the pleasant songs of the birds, but I was unable. Sometimes I wished to express my sensations in my own way, but the rough and inarticulate sounds which broke from me frightened me into silence again.

One day, when I was oppressed by cold, I found a fire which had been left by some wandering beggars, and I was overcome with delight at the warmth I felt from it. In my joy I thrust my hand into the live coals, but quickly drew it out again with a cry of pain. How strange, I thought, that the same cause should produce such opposite effects! I examined the materials of the fire, and to my joy I found it was composed of wood. I quickly collected some branches. I learnt to keep the fire alive with wet wood at night and to cook the food I found. I discovered that berries were spoiled by this operation, but that nuts and roots were much improved.

Food, however, became scarce, and I left the place where I had lived to look for a land where my few needs could be more easily satisfied. After three days of wandering through deep snow, I saw a small hut on a piece of rising ground. Here was an old man sitting near a fire, preparing his breakfast. He turned on hearing a noise, and seeing me, he shrieked loudly and, quitting the hut, ran across the fields with a speed of which his weak body hardly appeared capable. His appearance was different from any I had ever seen before, and his flight somewhat surprised me. But I was enchanted by the appearance of the hut. Here the snow and rain could not penetrate, and I greedily ate the remains of the shepherd's breakfast: bread, cheese, milk and wine. The last, however, I did not like.

The next day I continued my journey and reached a village. How miraculous it seemed! The huts, the neater cottages and the fine houses attracted my admiration by turns. The vegetables in the gardens and the milk and cheese that I saw placed at the windows of some of the cottages awoke my appetite. I entered one of the best of these, but I had hardly placed my foot within the door before the children shrieked and one of the women fainted. The whole village was roused. Some fled, some attacked me, until, badly bruised by stones and many other kinds of missile weapons, I escaped to the open country.

I took refuge in a low hovel, quite bare, which was joined to a neat and pleasant cottage. The hovel was so low that I could only sit upright in it, but it was dry. Its floor was made of earth, and the wind entered it through countless gaps. Yet I found it an agreeable shelter from the snow and rain, and, above all, from the barbarity of man.

As soon as morning dawned, I crept from my den so that I might view the cottage and discover whether I could remain in the shelter I had found. My hovel stood against the back of the cottage. One side was open to the pig sty and a clear pool of water. I covered every crack by which I might be seen with stones and wood, in such a way that I could move them to pass out. The only light I enjoyed came through the sty, and that was sufficient for me.

Having arranged my home in this way, and carpeted it with clean straw, I retired, for I saw the figure of a man at a distance, and I remembered too well my treatment the night before to trust myself in his power. But first I found that I could see into the cottage through a small crack in the wall, where a board had been badly fitted. Through this crack a small room was visible, whitewashed and clean, but very bare of furniture.

In one corner, near a small fire, sat an old man, leaning his head on his hands in a sorrowful attitude. A young girl was busy arranging the cottage. Soon she took something out of a drawer, which occupied her hands, and she sat down beside the old man. He took up an instrument and began to play, producing sounds sweeter than the voice of the thrush or the nightingale. It was a lovely sight, even to me, poor wretch, who had never seen anything beautiful before.

The silver hair and kind face of the old man won my respect, while the gentle manners of the girl enticed my love. He played a sweet, mournful air which I saw drew tears from the eyes of his gentle companion. The old man did not notice them until she sobbed audibly. Then he said a few words, and the fair creature, leaving her work, knelt at his feet. He raised her and smiled with such kindness and affection that I felt sensations of a peculiar and overpowering nature. They were a mixture of pain and pleasure, such as I had never before experienced, either from hunger or cold, warmth or food. I withdrew from the window, unable to bear these emotions.

Soon after this the young man returned, bearing on his shoulders a load of wood. The girl met him at the door and helped him to remove his burden. She took some of the fuel into the cottage and placed it on the fire. Then she and the youth went apart into a nook of the cottage, and he showed her a large loaf and a piece of cheese. She seemed pleased and went into the garden for some roots and plants, which she placed in water and then upon the fire.

Chapter 10: The Cottagers

I lay on my straw that night, but I could not sleep. I thought of the occurrences of the day. What chiefly struck me was the gentle manners of these people, and I longed to join them, but dared not. I remembered too well the treatment I had suffered the night before from the barbarous villagers. I decided that, whatever course of conduct I might later think it right to pursue, for the present I would remain quietly in my hovel, watching and endeavouring to discover the motives which influenced their actions.

The cottagers rose the next morning before the sun. The young woman arranged the cottage and prepared the food, and the youth departed after the first meal. This day was passed in the same routine as the one before it. The young man was constantly employed out of doors, and the girl in various laborious occupations within. The old man, whom I soon perceived to be blind, spent his leisure hours on his instrument or in contemplation. Nothing could exceed the love and respect which the younger cottagers showed towards their venerable companion.

They were not entirely happy. The young man and his companion often went apart and appeared to weep. I saw no cause for their unhappiness, but I was deeply affected by it. If such lovely creatures were miserable, it was less strange that I, an imperfect and solitary being, should be wretched. Yet why were these gentle beings unhappy? They possessed a delightful house, for so it was in my eyes, and every luxury. They had a fire to warm them when chill, and delicious food when hungry. They were dressed in excellent clothes, and, still more, they enjoyed one another's company and speech, interchanging each day looks of affection and kindness. What did their tears mean?

After a long time I discovered one of the causes of the uneasiness of this gentle family. It was poverty, and they suffered that evil in a very distressing degree. Their food consisted entirely of the vegetables of their garden and the milk of one cow. Often the two young people placed food before the old man when they reserved none for themselves.

This trait of kindness moved me deeply. I had been accustomed, during the night, to steal a part of their store for my own consumption. But when I found that in doing this I caused pain to the cottagers, I stopped, and satisfied myself with berries, nuts and roots, which I gathered from a neighbouring wood.

I discovered also another means through which I was able to help their labours. I found that the youth spent a great part of each day in collecting wood for the family fire. During the night I often took his tools, whose use I quickly discovered, and brought home enough firewood for the consumption of several days.

I remember the first time I did this. The young woman, when she opened the door in the morning, appeared greatly astonished on seeing a great pile of wood on the outside. She uttered some words in a loud voice, and the youth joined her, and he also expressed surprise. I observed with pleasure that he did not go to the forest that day, but spent it in repairing the cottage and cultivating the garden. Later I heard them speak of a "good spirit" who had brought the wood, and I was secretly delighted.

By degrees I made a discovery of still greater importance. I found that these people possessed a method of communicating their experience and feelings to one another by spoken sounds. I perceived that the words they spoke sometimes produced pleasure or pain, smiles or sadness, in the minds and faces of the hearers. This was indeed a godlike science, and I ardently desired to become acquainted with it.

It was a difficult task, because their pronunciation was quick, and the words they uttered did not have any apparent connection with visible objects. But by great application, and after I had remained in my hovel during the space of several revolutions of the moon, I discovered the names that were given to some of the most familiar objects of discourse. I learnt and applied the words fire, milk, bread and wood. I learnt also the names of the cottagers themselves. The youth and his companion each had several names, but the old man had only one, which was father. The girl was called sister, or Agatha, and the youth Felix, brother, or son.

I cannot describe the delight I felt when I learnt the ideas belonging to each of these sounds and was able to pronounce them. I distinguished several other words, without being able as yet to understand or apply them, such as good, dearest, unhappy.

I had admired the perfect forms of my cottagers: their grace, beauty and delicate complexions. But how was I terrified when I saw myself in a clear pool! At first I started back, unable to believe that it was indeed I who was reflected in the mirror. And when I became fully convinced that I was in reality the monster that I am, I was filled with the bitterest feelings of despair and humiliation. Alas! I did not yet entirely know the fatal effects of this miserable deformity.

As the sun became warmer and the light of day longer, the snow vanished, and I saw the bare trees and the black earth. From this time Felix was more employed, and the heart-moving signs of coming famine disappeared. Their food, as I afterwards found, was coarse, but it was wholesome, and they procured enough of it.

My days were spent in close attention, so that I might more quickly master the language. And I may boast that I improved more rapidly than the Arabian, who understood very little and conversed in broken accents, while I comprehended and could imitate almost every word that was spoken. But I am going too fast. I must first tell you how that Arabian came to the cottage.

I had dreamed that one day I would show myself to these good people. I imagined that they would be disgusted at first, until, by my gentle manners and kind words, I should first win their favour and afterwards their love. These thoughts excited me and led me to apply with fresh enthusiasm to the art of language. My organs were indeed harsh, but supple. And although my voice was very unlike the soft music of their tones, yet I pronounced such words as I understood with tolerable ease. It was as the ass and the lapdog in the fable; yet surely the gentle ass, whose intentions were affectionate, although his manners were rude, deserved better treatment than blows and curses.

Chapter 11: Safie and the Books

One day in spring there was a knock at the cottage door. It was a lady on horseback, accompanied by a country guide. The lady was dressed in a dark suit and covered with a thick black veil. Agatha asked a question, to which the stranger only replied by pronouncing, in a sweet accent, the name of Felix.

Her voice was musical, but unlike that of either of my friends. On hearing this word, Felix came up hastily to the lady, who, when she saw him, threw up her veil, and I beheld a face of angelic beauty and expression. Her hair was a shining raven black, and curiously braided. Her eyes were dark, but gentle, although animated. Her features were well proportioned, and her complexion wonderfully fair.

Felix seemed overjoyed when he saw her. Every trace of sorrow vanished from his face, and it immediately expressed a degree of ecstatic joy of which I could hardly have believed it capable. His eyes sparkled, as his cheek flushed with pleasure, and at that moment I thought him as beautiful as the stranger.

I soon perceived that, although the stranger uttered articulate sounds and appeared to have a language of her own, she was neither understood by nor understood the cottagers. They made many signs which I did not comprehend, but I saw that her presence spread gladness through the cottage, dispelling their sorrow as the sun dissipates the morning mists. Her name was Safie. Felix began to teach her his language, and I, hidden in my hovel, learnt with her. In two months I understood most of the words uttered by my protectors.

Felix taught Safie from a book called Ruins of Empires, a history of the ancient nations of the world. Through this work I obtained a rough knowledge of history. I heard of the slothful Asiatics, of the wonderful genius of the Greeks, of the wars and virtues of the early Romans and of the decline of that mighty empire. I heard of the discovery of the American continent, and I wept with Safie over the unhappy fate of its original inhabitants.

These wonderful stories inspired me with strange feelings. Was man, indeed, at once so powerful, so virtuous and so magnificent, and yet so vicious and base? I heard of the division of property, of immense wealth and miserable poverty, of rank, descent and noble blood. I learnt that the possessions most respected by men were high and unspoiled descent united with riches. And what was I? Of my creation and creator I was absolutely ignorant, but I knew that I possessed no money, no friends, no kind of property. I was, besides, endowed with a figure hideously deformed and loathsome. I was not even of the same nature as man. I was more agile than they and could live on a coarser diet. I bore the extremes of heat and cold with less injury to my frame. My height far exceeded theirs. When I looked around, I saw and heard of none like me. Was I, then, a monster, a blot upon the earth, from which all men fled and whom all men disowned?

Some time passed before I learnt the history of my friends. Their name was De Lacey. They were from a good family in France, where they had lived for many years in Paris, surrounded by friends and possessed of every enjoyment. Safie's father was a Turkish merchant who had lived in Paris for many years, until, for some reason which I could not learn, he became hateful to the government. He was arrested and condemned to death; the injustice of his sentence was very obvious. Felix happened to be present at the trial, and his horror and indignation were uncontrollable. He made a solemn vow to free the old man.

Felix visited the prison and promised the merchant that he would help him escape. The Turk, in order to secure Felix's help, promised him the hand of his daughter. Felix had already seen Safie through the bars of the prison window, and he loved her. The escape succeeded, but the Turk was false. The plan was discovered, and De Lacey and Agatha were thrown into prison. Felix returned to France to give himself up, hoping to free them. He failed. Their fortune was taken from them, and they were condemned to perpetual exile from their native country. They found a miserable shelter in this cottage in Germany. The Turk, learning that his deliverer was now poor and ruined, refused to let his daughter marry a Christian beggar. But Safie, learning where her lover was living, escaped from her father and travelled alone across Europe to find him.

Such was the history of my beloved cottagers. It impressed me deeply. I learnt, from the views of social life which it showed me, to admire their virtues and to hate the vices of mankind.

One night during my usual visit to the neighbouring wood, I found on the ground a leather bag containing several articles of dress and some books. I eagerly seized the prize and returned with it to my hovel. Fortunately the books were written in the language, the elements of which I had learnt at the cottage. They were Paradise Lost, a volume of Plutarch's Lives and The Sorrows of Werter.

I cannot describe to you the effect of these books. In Werter I found gentle and domestic manners, combined with lofty sentiments and feelings. Plutarch taught me high thoughts; he elevated me above the wretched sphere of my own reflections, to admire and love the heroes of past ages. But Paradise Lost excited different and far deeper emotions. I read it as a true history. It moved every feeling of wonder and awe that the picture of an all-powerful God warring with his creatures was capable of exciting. Like Adam, I was apparently united by no link to any other being in existence. But his state was far different from mine in every other respect. He had come forth from the hands of God a perfect creature, happy and prosperous, guarded by the special care of his Creator. He was allowed to converse with, and acquire knowledge from, beings of a superior nature. But I was wretched, helpless and alone. Many times I considered Satan as the fitter emblem of my condition, for often, like him, when I saw the happiness of my protectors, the bitter gall of envy rose within me.

Another circumstance strengthened these feelings. Soon after my arrival in the hovel, I had discovered some papers in the pocket of the clothes which I had taken from your laboratory. At first I had neglected them, but now that I was able to read, I began to study them with care. It was your journal of the four months that preceded my creation. You recorded in these papers every step you took in the progress of your work, mixed with accounts of domestic events. You, doubtless, remember these papers. Here they are. Everything is related in them which bears reference to my accursed origin. The whole detail of that series of disgusting circumstances which produced it is set in view. The tiniest description of my odious and loathsome person is given, in language which painted your own horrors.

"Hateful day when I received life!" I exclaimed in agony. "Accursed creator! Why did you form a monster so hideous that even you turned from me in disgust? God, in pity, made man beautiful and attractive, after his own image. But my form is a filthy type of yours, more horrid even from the very resemblance. Satan had his companions, fellow devils, to admire and encourage him. But I am solitary and hated."

Chapter 12: Rejection

Autumn passed thus. I saw, with surprise and grief, the leaves decay and fall, and nature again assume the barren and bleak appearance it had worn when I first saw the woods and the lovely moon. Yet I did not heed the bleakness of the weather; I was better fitted by my nature for the endurance of cold than heat. My chief delights were the sight of the flowers, the birds and all the gay apparel of summer, and when those deserted me, I turned with more attention towards the cottagers.

I decided at last to speak to the old man. I reasoned that his blindness would protect me from the horror which my appearance caused in others. One day, when the sun shone on the red leaves that covered the ground, Safie, Agatha and Felix departed on a long country walk, and the old man, at his own desire, was left alone in the cottage. My heart beat quickly; this was the hour and moment of trial, which would decide my hopes or realise my fears. I approached the door of the cottage and knocked.

"Who is there?" said the old man. "Come in."

I entered. "Pardon this intrusion," I said. "I am a traveller in want of a little rest. You would greatly oblige me if you would allow me to remain a few minutes before the fire."

"Enter," said De Lacey, "and I will try in what manner I can relieve your wants. But, unfortunately, my children are from home, and, as I am blind, I am afraid I shall find it difficult to procure food for you."

"Do not trouble yourself, my kind host. I have food. It is warmth and rest only that I need."

I sat down, and a silence followed. I knew that every minute was precious to me, yet I remained undecided in what manner to begin the interview. At last I spoke. I told him that I was about to throw myself on the protection of some friends whom I sincerely loved, but who had never seen me. "I am full of fears," I said, "for if I fail there, I am an outcast in the world for ever. They are kind; they are the most excellent creatures in the world. But, unfortunately, they are prejudiced against me. Where they ought to see a feeling and kind friend, they behold only a detestable monster."

"That is indeed unfortunate," said the old man, "but if you are really blameless, cannot you undeceive them? I am blind and cannot judge of your face, but there is something in your words which persuades me that you are sincere. I am poor and an exile, but it will give me true pleasure to be in any way useful to a human creature."

"Excellent man! I thank you and accept your generous offer."

At that instant I heard the steps of my younger protectors. I had not a moment to lose. Seizing the hand of the old man, I cried, "Now is the time! Save and protect me! You and your family are the friends whom I seek. Do not desert me in the hour of trial!"

"Great God!" exclaimed the old man. "Who are you?"

At that instant the cottage door was opened, and Felix, Safie and Agatha entered. Who can describe their horror and consternation on seeing me? Agatha fainted, and Safie, unable to attend to her friend, rushed out of the cottage. Felix darted forward, and with supernatural force tore me from his father, to whose knees I was clinging. In a transport of fury, he dashed me to the ground and struck me violently with a stick. I could have torn him limb from limb, as the lion tears the antelope. But my heart sank within me as with bitter sickness, and I refrained. I saw him on the point of repeating his blow, when, overcome by pain and anguish, I quitted the cottage and in the general tumult escaped unnoticed to my hovel.

Cursed, cursed creator! Why did I live? Why, in that instant, did I not extinguish the spark of existence which you had so wantonly given? I spent the night in the wood, howling with rage and misery. When I returned two days later, the cottage was empty. Felix had paid the landlord and taken his family away for ever. Their departure broke the only link that held me to the world. For the first time, feelings of revenge and hatred filled my heart. That night I placed dry wood around the cottage, and when the moon had sunk, I set fire to it and danced with fury round the burning building, until the flames consumed it entirely.

And now, with the world before me, where should I go? I decided to go to you, my father, my creator. From your papers I knew that you lived in Geneva. Who could I ask for pity and justice but you, who had given me life? My travels were long, and the sufferings I endured intense. I travelled only at night, fearful of meeting the face of a human being.

One morning in spring, when I was walking through a deep wood, a young girl came running towards the spot where I was hiding, laughing, as if she were running from someone in play. Suddenly her foot slipped, and she fell into the rapid stream. I rushed from my hiding place and, with extreme labour, saved her from the force of the current and dragged her to the shore. She was senseless, and I was trying by every means in my power to restore her, when a man suddenly appeared. He tore the girl from my arms and hastened towards the deeper parts of the wood. I followed quickly, I hardly knew why, but when the man saw me draw near, he aimed a gun at my body and fired. I sank to the ground, and he escaped into the wood.

This, then, was the reward of my kindness! I had saved a human being from destruction, and as a recompense I now writhed under the miserable pain of a wound which shattered the flesh and bone. The feelings of kindness and gentleness which I had felt only a few moments before gave place to hellish rage. I swore eternal hatred and vengeance to all mankind.

At last I reached Geneva. One evening, while I rested in a field near the city, a beautiful child came running into the place I had chosen for my hiding. Suddenly, as I gazed on him, an idea seized me that this little creature was unprejudiced and had lived too short a time to have learnt a horror of deformity. If, therefore, I could seize him and educate him as my companion and friend, I should not be so lonely in this peopled earth.

I grabbed the boy as he passed. As soon as he saw my form, he placed his hands before his eyes and uttered a shrill scream. "Monster! Ugly wretch! Let me go. My papa is a syndic, he is M. Frankenstein. He will punish you."

"Frankenstein! You belong, then, to my enemy, to him towards whom I have sworn eternal revenge. You shall be my first victim."

The child still struggled and called me names which carried despair to my heart. I grasped his throat to silence him, and in a moment he lay dead at my feet. I gazed on my victim, and my heart swelled with hellish triumph. "I too can create desolation," I cried. "My enemy is not invulnerable. This death will carry despair to him."

As I looked at the child, I saw something glittering on his chest. It was a portrait of a most lovely woman. Then I left the spot and entered a barn, which had seemed to me to be empty. A woman was sleeping on some straw. She was young and beautiful, and I thought: here is one whose smiles are given to all but me. Then the fiend that was in me whispered: not I, but she, shall suffer. The murder I have committed because I am forever robbed of all that she could give me, she shall pay for. I bent over her and placed the portrait securely in one of the folds of her dress.

You know the rest. I am alone and miserable. Man will not associate with me. But one as deformed and horrible as myself would not deny herself to me. My companion must be of the same species and have the same defects. This being you must create."

Chapter 13: The Promise

The being finished speaking and fixed his eyes upon me, expecting a reply. But I was bewildered and could not arrange my ideas well enough to understand the full extent of his proposal. At last my anger returned.

"I refuse it," I replied, "and no torture shall ever force me to agree. You may make me the most miserable of men, but you shall never make me base in my own eyes. Shall I create another like yourself, whose joint wickedness might destroy the world? Go! I have answered you. You may torture me, but I will never consent."

"You are wrong," replied the fiend, "and instead of threatening, I am content to reason with you. I am malicious because I am miserable. Am I not hated by all mankind? You, my creator, would tear me to pieces and triumph. If any being felt emotions of kindness towards me, I would return them a hundredfold. For that one creature's sake I would make peace with the whole human race! What I ask of you is reasonable and moderate. I demand a creature of another sex, but as hideous as myself. It is true, we shall be monsters, cut off from all the world, but for that reason we shall be more attached to one another. If you consent, neither you nor any other human being shall ever see us again. I will go to the vast wilds of South America. My food is not that of man; I do not destroy the lamb and the kid to satisfy my appetite. Acorns and berries are enough for me. My companion will be of the same nature as myself and will be content with the same fare. We shall make our bed of dried leaves; the sun will shine on us as on man and will ripen our food."

His words had a strange effect on me. I pitied him and sometimes felt a wish to console him. But when I looked at him, when I saw the filthy mass that moved and talked, my heart sickened, and my feelings changed to horror and hatred. I thought, too, that I had no right to withhold from him the small portion of happiness which was yet in my power to give.

"You swear," I said, "to be harmless. But have you not already shown a degree of malice that should reasonably make me distrust you?"

"How is this? I must not be trifled with. If I have no ties and no affections, hatred and vice must be my portion. The love of another will destroy the cause of my crimes. My vices are the children of a forced solitude that I hate, and my virtues will necessarily appear when I live in communion with an equal."

After a long pause, I made my decision. "I consent to your demand," I said, "on your solemn oath to leave Europe for ever, and every other place in the neighbourhood of man, as soon as I shall deliver into your hands a female who will accompany you in your exile."

"I swear," he cried, "by the sun and by the blue sky of heaven, that if you grant my prayer, while they exist you shall never see me again. Depart to your home and begin your labours. I shall watch their progress with unspeakable anxiety. And do not fear; when you are ready, I shall appear."

Saying this, he suddenly left me, fearful, perhaps, of any change in my feelings. I saw him descend the mountain with greater speed than the flight of an eagle, and he was quickly lost among the waves of the sea of ice.

I returned to Geneva, but day after day, week after week, I could not find the courage to begin my work. I feared the vengeance of the disappointed fiend, yet I was unable to overcome my disgust at the task. I learnt that some discoveries had been made by an English philosopher which were essential to my success, and I thought of travelling to England for this purpose.

One day my father called me aside. "I confess, my son," he said, "that I have always looked forward to your marriage with our dear Elizabeth as the bond of our domestic comfort and the stay of my declining years. But perhaps you regard her as your sister, without any wish that she might become your wife. Perhaps you have met another whom you may love."

"My dear father, reassure yourself. I love my cousin tenderly and sincerely. I have never seen any woman who excited, as Elizabeth does, my warmest admiration and affection."

But I could not marry while the monster's demand hung over me. How could I enter into a festival with this deadly weight around my neck? I must first perform my engagement and let the monster depart with his mate before I allowed myself the delight of a union from which I expected peace. I asked my father to let me travel to England first, and he agreed, on condition that Clerval should come with me. Elizabeth and I would be married immediately on my return.

So, at the end of September, I left my native country again. I packed my chemical instruments, and Henry and I travelled down the Rhine. Henry delighted in everything: the vineyards on the banks of the river, the ruined castles on the edges of cliffs, the singing of the boatmen. "This is what it is to live," he cried. "Now I enjoy existence!" I, on the contrary, was a miserable wretch, haunted by a curse that shut up every avenue to enjoyment.

We arrived in London at the end of December and stayed there for several months. Clerval wished to meet men of genius and talent; I, on the other hand, collected the information I needed from the most distinguished natural philosophers of the city. Then we travelled north, to Oxford, to Matlock and Cumberland, and at last to Edinburgh and Perth. There I told Clerval that I wished to make a tour of Scotland alone.

"I may be gone a month or two," I said, "but do not worry about me. Leave me to peace and solitude for a short time, and when I return, I hope it will be with a lighter heart."

Henry tried to persuade me otherwise, but seeing me so determined, he stopped. "I would rather be with you," he said, "in your solitary rambles, than with these Scotch people, whom I do not know. Hurry back, my dear friend, so that I may again feel myself somewhat at home, which I cannot do in your absence."

Having parted from my friend, I decided to visit some remote spot of Scotland and finish my work in solitude. I did not doubt that the monster followed me and would show himself to me when I had finished, so that he might receive his companion.

Chapter 14: The Broken Promise

I chose one of the most remote of the Orkney Islands as the scene of my labours. It was a place well fitted for such a work, being hardly more than a rock, whose high sides were continually beaten by the waves. The soil was barren and hardly gave pasture for a few miserable cows and oatmeal for its inhabitants, who were five in number. There were only three huts on the whole island, and one of these was empty when I arrived. I hired it. It contained only two rooms, and these showed all the squalidness of the most miserable poverty. The thatch had fallen in, the walls were unplastered, and the door was off its hinges.

In this retreat I spent the mornings in labour. In the evenings, when the weather permitted, I walked on the stony beach of the sea to listen to the waves as they roared and dashed at my feet. During my first experiment, a kind of enthusiastic frenzy had blinded me to the horror of my work. Now I went to it in cold blood, and my heart often sickened at the work of my hands.

One evening I sat in my laboratory. The sun had set, and the moon was just rising from the sea. I did not have enough light for my work, and I remained idle, considering whether I should stop for the night. As I sat, a train of thought came to me which led me to consider the effects of what I was doing.

Three years before, I had created a fiend whose unparalleled cruelty had filled my heart for ever with the bitterest remorse. I was now about to form another being, of whose character I knew nothing. She might become ten thousand times more evil than her mate. He had sworn to leave the neighbourhood of man and hide himself in deserts, but she had not. She might refuse to keep a promise made before her creation. They might even hate each other. He might hate his own ugliness even more when he saw it in a female form; she, too, might turn with disgust from him to the superior beauty of man. And even if they left Europe and lived in the deserts of the new world, one of the first results of the sympathies for which the demon thirsted would be children. A race of devils would be spread upon the earth, who might make the very existence of the human species a condition full of terror. Had I the right, for my own benefit, to inflict this curse upon future generations?

I trembled, and my heart failed within me. Then, looking up, I saw by the light of the moon the demon at the window. A ghastly grin wrinkled his lips as he gazed on me, where I sat fulfilling the task which he had given me. Yes, he had followed me in my travels; he had hidden in caves or taken refuge in wide and desert heaths. Now he came to mark my progress and claim the fulfilment of my promise.

As I looked on him, his face expressed the utmost extent of malice and treachery. I thought with a sensation of madness of my promise to create another like him. Trembling with passion, I tore to pieces the thing on which I was engaged. The wretch saw me destroy the creature on whose future existence he depended for happiness, and with a howl of devilish despair and revenge, he withdrew.

Several hours passed, and I remained near my window gazing on the sea. Then I heard the paddling of oars near the shore, and a person landed close to my house. A few minutes later, the door opened, and the wretch appeared.

"You have destroyed the work which you began," he said. "What do you intend? Do you dare to break your promise? I have endured toil and misery. I left Switzerland with you; I crept along the shores of the Rhine, among its willow islands and over the summits of its hills. I have lived many months in the heaths of England and among the deserts of Scotland. I have endured incalculable fatigue, and cold, and hunger. Do you dare destroy my hopes?"

"Go! I do break my promise. Never will I create another like yourself, equal in deformity and wickedness."

"Slave, I reasoned with you before, but you have proved yourself unworthy of my kindness. Remember that I have power. You believe yourself miserable, but I can make you so wretched that the light of day will be hateful to you. You are my creator, but I am your master; obey!"

"The hour of my weakness is past, and the period of your power has arrived. Your threats cannot move me to do an act of wickedness. Leave me. I am firm."

"Are you to be happy while I grovel in the intensity of my wretchedness? You can destroy my other passions, but revenge remains. I will watch with the cunning of a snake, so that I may sting with its poison. Man, you shall repent of the injuries you inflict. Beware; for I am fearless, and therefore powerful."

"Devil, stop. Do not poison the air with these sounds of malice. I have declared my decision to you, and I am no coward to bend beneath words. Leave me; I cannot be changed."

"It is well. I go; but remember, I shall be with you on your wedding-night."

I started forward and exclaimed, "Villain! Before you sign my death warrant, be sure that you are yourself safe."

I would have seized him, but he escaped me and left the house with great speed. In a few moments I saw him in his boat, which shot across the waters with the swiftness of an arrow and was soon lost amid the waves. His words rang in my ears: I shall be with you on your wedding-night. That, then, was the moment fixed for my destiny. I did not think of my own death. But when I thought of my beloved Elizabeth, of her tears and endless sorrow, tears, the first I had shed for many months, streamed from my eyes.

Two days later I put the remains of my half-finished creature into a basket, with a great quantity of stones, and rowed out to sea at midnight. I cast my basket into the water and listened to the gurgling sound as it sank. Then I lay down in the boat, and I fell asleep. When I woke, a strong wind had carried me far from the island. For many hours I drifted helplessly, until at last I saw land and steered towards a small town on the coast.

As I tied my boat, several people crowded towards the spot. They looked at me with hostility. "You will be taken before Mr Kirwin, the magistrate," said a rough man, "to give an account of the death of a gentleman who was found murdered here last night."

I was in Ireland. I was not afraid, because I was innocent. But when I was led into the room where the body lay, and I saw the lifeless form of Henry Clerval stretched before me, the marks of the fiend's fingers on his neck, I threw myself on the body. "Have my murderous machinations deprived you also, my dearest Henry, of life?" I cried. "Two I have already destroyed; other victims await their destiny."

I was seized with convulsions and carried from the room. For two months I lay in prison on the point of death, raving, and calling myself the murderer of William, of Justine and of Clerval. Mr Kirwin showed me great kindness, and at last he brought my father to me. Evidence was found that I had been in the Orkneys at the hour of Henry's death, and the grand jury rejected the charge. I was free, but freedom was useless to me. Clerval was dead, and the fiend was alive.

Chapter 15: The Wedding Night

My father took me home by way of Paris, where we rested for some time. There I received a letter from Elizabeth. "If you love another," she wrote, "do not let me stand in the way of your happiness. I confess to you, my friend, that I love you. But I desire your happiness as well as my own, and if you do not wish to marry me, I shall not be unhappy."

This letter reminded me of what I had forgotten, the threat of the fiend: I shall be with you on your wedding-night. Such was my sentence, and on that night the demon would employ every art to destroy me. Well, be it so; a deadly struggle would then surely take place, in which, if he were victorious, I should be at peace, and his power over me would be at an end. If he were defeated, I should be a free man. I wrote to Elizabeth that I loved her with all my heart. "I have one secret, Elizabeth," I wrote, "a dreadful one. When revealed to you, it will chill your frame with horror. I will tell it to you the day after our marriage."

We returned to Geneva. Elizabeth welcomed me with warm affection, but there were tears in her eyes as she looked at my thin body and feverish cheeks. She, too, was changed. She was thinner and had lost much of that heavenly liveliness that had once charmed me. But her gentleness made her a fitter companion for one ruined and miserable as I was.

The marriage was fixed for ten days later. I carried pistols and a dagger always about me and was ever on the watch to prevent trickery. As the day approached, the threat appeared more like a delusion, and the happiness I hoped for from my marriage seemed more certain. But it was not my own death that the monster intended.

After the ceremony there was a large party at my father's house. Then Elizabeth and I left by boat for Evian, where we would spend the first night of our marriage. It was a beautiful day, and the wind was favourable. We saw Mont Blanc and the mountains of the Jura, and the sun shone on the lake. Those were the last moments of my life during which I enjoyed the feeling of happiness.

We landed at eight o'clock and walked for a short time on the shore. Then the wind rose, and a heavy storm of rain descended. I had been calm during the day, but as soon as night hid the shapes of objects, a thousand fears arose in my mind. I was anxious and watchful, while my right hand grasped a pistol which was hidden in my chest. Every sound terrified me, but I decided that I would sell my life dearly and not shrink from the conflict until my own life or that of my enemy was extinguished.

Elizabeth observed my agitation in timid and fearful silence. "What is it that agitates you, my dear Victor?" she asked. "What is it you fear?"

"Oh! Peace, peace, my love," I replied. "This night, and all will be safe. But this night is dreadful, very dreadful."

I passed an hour in this state of mind, when suddenly I thought how fearful the combat which I expected would be to my wife. I begged her to go to bed, and I decided not to join her until I had obtained some knowledge of the situation of my enemy. She left me, and I continued some time walking up and down the passages of the house, inspecting every corner that might afford a hiding place.

But I discovered no trace of him. I was beginning to think that some fortunate chance had prevented the execution of his threats, when suddenly I heard a shrill and dreadful scream. It came from the room into which Elizabeth had gone. As I heard it, the whole truth rushed into my mind. My arms dropped, and the motion of every muscle was suspended. Then the scream was repeated, and I rushed into the room.

Great God! Why did I not then die? There she was, lifeless and inanimate, thrown across the bed, her head hanging down and her pale features half covered by her hair. Everywhere I turn I see the same figure: her bloodless arms and relaxed form flung by the murderer on its bridal bier. The murderous mark of the fiend's grasp was on her neck, and the breath had ceased to come from her lips.

While I still hung over her in the agony of despair, I happened to look up. The windows of the room had before been darkened, and I felt a kind of panic on seeing the pale yellow light of the moon illuminate the chamber. The shutters had been thrown back, and with a sensation of horror not to be described, I saw at the open window a figure, the most hideous and abhorred. A grin was on the face of the monster; he seemed to jeer, as with his fiendish finger he pointed towards the corpse of my wife. I rushed towards the window and, drawing a pistol from my chest, fired. But he avoided me, leapt from his station, and, running with the swiftness of lightning, plunged into the lake.

People from the house searched the lake and the countryside for hours, but they found nothing. I returned to Geneva with the terrible news. My father could not bear it. Elizabeth had been more than a daughter to him, and in a few days he died in my arms.

What then became of me? I do not know. I lost all sensation, and chains and darkness were the only objects that pressed upon me. For many months, I was told, I lived in a solitary cell, for they believed I was mad. When at last I was released, I went to a magistrate of the town and told him the whole history of the monster. He listened with growing wonder, but in the end he said that it would be impossible to follow such a creature across the mountains and ice.

"I see that you do not believe my story," I said, "and I shall have to find the destroyer myself."

Chapter 16: The Chase to the North

My first decision was to leave Geneva for ever. My country, which, when I was happy and beloved, was dear to me, now became hateful. I provided myself with a sum of money, together with a few jewels which had belonged to my mother, and departed.

Before I left, I went at night to the cemetery where William, Elizabeth and my father rested. Everything was silent except the leaves of the trees, which were gently moved by the wind. I knelt on the grass, kissed the earth, and with trembling lips exclaimed, "By the sacred earth on which I kneel, by the shades that wander near me, I swear to pursue the demon who caused this misery, until he or I shall die in mortal conflict. And I call on you, spirits of the dead, to aid and conduct me in my work."

I was answered through the stillness of night by a loud and fiendish laugh. It rang on my ears long and heavily; the mountains echoed it, and I felt as if all hell surrounded me with mockery and laughter. Then a well-known and abhorred voice whispered close to my ear, "I am satisfied, miserable wretch! You have decided to live, and I am satisfied."

I darted towards the spot from which the sound came, but the devil escaped my grasp. Suddenly the broad disk of the moon arose and shone full upon his ghastly and distorted shape as he fled with more than mortal speed.

I have pursued him ever since, and for many months this has been my task. Guided by a slight clue, I followed the windings of the Rhône, but in vain. The blue Mediterranean appeared, and by a strange chance I saw the fiend enter by night and hide himself in a ship bound for the Black Sea. I took my passage in the same ship, but he escaped, I do not know how.

Through the wilds of Tartary and Russia, although he still avoided me, I have always followed in his track. Sometimes the peasants, scared by this horrid apparition, informed me of his path. Sometimes he himself, who feared that if I lost all trace of him I should despair and die, left some mark to guide me. Often, when I was dying of hunger, I found a meal prepared for me in the desert. Sometimes he left marks in writing on the bark of the trees or cut in stone. "My reign is not yet over," these words said in one of these inscriptions. "You live, and my power is complete. Follow me; I seek the everlasting ices of the north, where you will feel the misery of cold and frost, to which I am impassive. Come on, my enemy; we have yet to wrestle for our lives."

Scoff on, devil! My vengeance will soon be achieved. As I went north, the snows thickened and the cold increased to a degree almost too severe to support. The rivers were covered with ice, and no fish could be caught. Another message, cut in the trunk of a tree, read: "Prepare! Your toils only begin. Wrap yourself in furs and provide food, for we shall soon enter upon a journey where your sufferings will satisfy my everlasting hatred."

My courage was strengthened by these scornful words. I bought a sledge and dogs and crossed the snows with great speed. I reached a wretched village on the seashore, and there I learnt that a gigantic monster had arrived the night before, armed with a gun and many pistols. He had frightened the people of a lonely cottage away and taken their store of winter food. He had then placed it in a sledge, and, harnessing a number of trained dogs, he had set off across the frozen sea in a direction that led to no land.

At first I despaired, but revenge revived me. I exchanged my land sledge for one fitted for the uneven surface of the frozen ocean, bought a great quantity of food, and left the land.

I cannot guess how many days have passed since then. Immense and rugged mountains of ice often blocked my passage, and I often heard the thunder of the ground sea, which threatened my destruction. Once, after the poor animals had climbed to the top of a sloping ice mountain, and one of them had died of exhaustion, I saw a dark speck upon the plain. I uttered a wild cry of joy when I recognised a sledge and the distorted shape of a well-known form within. Oh, with what a burning gush did hope revisit my heart! Within two days I was only a mile behind my enemy.

But then, when I seemed almost to have him in my grasp, my hopes were suddenly extinguished. The wind rose, the sea roared, and with a mighty shock like an earthquake, the ice split and cracked with a tremendous and overwhelming sound. In a few minutes a raging sea rolled between me and my enemy, and I was left drifting on a piece of ice that grew smaller every moment.

Many hours passed in this way. Several of my dogs died, and I myself was about to sink under the weight of my distress, when I saw your ship riding at anchor, which gave me hopes of help and life. I had no idea that ships ever came so far north and was astonished at the sight. I quickly destroyed part of my sledge to make oars, and by these means I was able, with great fatigue, to move my ice raft towards your ship.

Walton, I have one request. If I die, swear to me that he shall not escape. Swear that you will seek him and satisfy my vengeance in his death. But do not trust him. He is eloquent and persuasive, and once his words had even power over my heart. Remember William, Justine, Clerval, Elizabeth and my father, and thrust your sword into his heart.

Chapter 17: Walton's Last Letters

26th August

You have read this strange and terrible story, Margaret. Do you not feel your blood freeze with horror, like that which even now chills mine? Sometimes, seized with sudden agony, he could not continue his tale. At other times his voice was broken, yet piercing, as he spoke the words so full of anguish. His tale is connected and told with an appearance of the simplest truth. Yet I own to you that the letters of Felix and Safie, which he showed me, and the appearance of the monster seen from our ship, brought to me a greater conviction of the truth of his story than his own words, however earnest and connected.

I tried to learn the secret of the creature's formation, but on this point he was silent. "Are you mad, my friend?" he said. "Would you also create for yourself and the world a demon enemy? Peace, peace! Learn my miseries and do not seek to increase your own."

2nd September

My beloved sister, I write to you surrounded by peril. We are still surrounded by mountains of ice which leave no escape and threaten every moment to crush my ship. The brave fellows whom I have persuaded to be my companions look towards me for help, but I have none to give. Frankenstein has grown weaker every day. A feverish fire still glimmers in his eyes, but he is exhausted.

5th September

This morning, as I sat watching the pale face of my friend, half a dozen sailors came to the cabin. Their leader told me that they had been chosen by the other sailors to make a request. If the ice broke and a free passage opened, they asked me to promise that I would turn the ship south immediately.

Before I could answer, Frankenstein, who had been silent and seemed hardly to have strength enough to listen, raised himself up. His eyes sparkled, and his cheeks flushed.

"What do you mean?" he said. "What do you demand of your captain? Are you, then, so easily turned from your plan? Did you not call this a glorious expedition? It was glorious because it was full of danger and terror, because at every new incident your courage was to be called forth. You were hereafter to be hailed as the benefactors of your species. And now, at the first imagination of danger, you shrink away and are content to be passed down as men who had not strength enough to endure cold and peril. Oh! Be men, or be more than men. Do not return to your families with the stigma of disgrace marked on your brows. Return as heroes who have fought and conquered, and who do not know what it is to turn their backs on the enemy."

The men were moved, and they looked at one another. They went away to consider his words. But I knew that, if the ice opened, they would not continue.

7th September

The die is cast. I have agreed to return if we are not destroyed. Thus are my hopes blasted by cowardice and indecision; I come back ignorant and disappointed. It requires more philosophy than I possess to bear this injustice with patience.

12th September

It is past; I am returning to England. I have lost my hopes of utility and glory; I have lost my friend.

On the eleventh the ice broke, and a passage towards the south became perfectly free. When the sailors saw this, a shout of joy broke from them, loud and long. Frankenstein woke and asked the cause of the noise. "They shout," I said, "because they will soon return to England."

"Do you then really return?"

"Alas! Yes. I cannot withstand their demands."

"Do so, if you will; but I will not. You may give up your purpose, but mine is assigned to me by Heaven, and I dare not." But as he tried to rise, the effort was too great, and he fainted.

Later he opened his eyes and spoke to me for the last time. "I do not find my past conduct blameable. In a fit of enthusiastic madness I created a rational creature and was bound towards him to assure, as far as was in my power, his happiness and well-being. But my duties towards my fellow beings had a greater claim on me. Farewell, Walton! Seek happiness in tranquillity and avoid ambition, even if it be only the apparently innocent one of distinguishing yourself in science and discoveries. Yet why do I say this? I have myself been blasted in these hopes, yet another may succeed."

His voice became fainter as he spoke. About half an hour afterwards, he tried again to speak, but was unable. He pressed my hand weakly, and his eyes closed for ever, while the light of a gentle smile passed away from his lips.

But what is this sound? It is midnight. The breeze blows fairly, and the watch on deck scarcely move. Again there is a sound as of a human voice, but hoarser. It comes from the cabin where the remains of Frankenstein still lie. I must rise and examine.

Over the body hung a form which I cannot find words to describe: gigantic in height, yet uncouth and distorted in its proportions. As he hung over the coffin, his face was concealed by long locks of ragged hair, but one vast hand was extended, in colour and apparent texture like that of a mummy. When he heard the sound of my approach, he stopped his cries of grief and sprang towards the window. Never did I see a vision so horrible as his face, of such loathsome yet appalling hideousness. I shut my eyes and tried to remember my duty with regard to this destroyer. I called on him to stay.

He paused, looking at me with wonder, and then turned again towards the lifeless form of his creator. "That is also my victim!" he exclaimed. "In his murder my crimes are complete. Oh, Frankenstein! Generous and self-devoted being! What does it help that I now ask you to pardon me? I, who destroyed you by destroying all you loved. Alas! He is cold; he cannot answer me."

"Your repentance is now useless," I said. "If you had listened to the voice of conscience before you carried your evil vengeance to this extreme, Frankenstein would still be alive."

"Do you think," said the demon, "that I was then dead to agony and remorse? He suffered not in the thousandth part the anguish that was mine. My heart was made to be open to love and sympathy, and when it was turned by misery to vice and hatred, it did not endure the violence of the change without torture such as you cannot even imagine. You hate me, but your hatred cannot equal that with which I regard myself. Do not fear that I shall be the instrument of future mischief. My work is nearly complete. Neither yours nor any man's death is needed to finish the series of my being. I shall leave your ship on the ice raft which brought me here, and I shall seek the most northern point of the globe. I shall collect my funeral pile and burn to ashes this miserable frame, so that its remains may give no light to any curious and unhallowed wretch who would create another such as I have been. I shall die. I shall no longer feel the agonies which now consume me."

"Farewell! I leave you, and in you the last of humankind whom these eyes will ever see. Farewell, Frankenstein! Soon these burning miseries will be extinct. I shall ascend my funeral pile triumphantly and exult in the agony of the torturing flames. The light of that fire will fade away; my ashes will be swept into the sea by the winds. My spirit will sleep in peace, or if it thinks, it will surely not think thus. Farewell."

He sprang from the cabin window, as he said this, upon the ice raft which lay close to the ship. He was soon borne away by the waves and lost in darkness and distance.
